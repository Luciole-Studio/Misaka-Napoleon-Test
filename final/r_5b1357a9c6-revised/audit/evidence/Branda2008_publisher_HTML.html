1“You must make it your guiding principle that the war must feed the war,” Such was Napoleon’s recommendation to Marshal Soult when he appointed the latter général en chef of the Army of the South in Spain. [1] The Duke of Dalmatia was also told to take “the most e?cient measures possible to provide for the pay and material needs of your army”. The theory expounded by Napoleon was that his campaigns abroad should be self-?nancing. The three methods used to achieve this goal were: the seizure of money and property (so-called ‘ordinary contributions’ or saisies ordinaires); the ?nancial gain derived from peace treaties; and the amounts saved via the policy of using allied troops or by having French soldiers maintained by the allied states in which they were stationed.

2Once the army had been equipped and clothed in France at the expense of the French Treasury, it was expected to be self-su?cient thereafter. And there was nothing new about this approach; it had long been the practice of all the armies of the world. Bonaparte, as commander in chief of the army in Italy, had received the same instructions for his ?rst campaign. However, as might be expected given his character, Napoleon was not content to let an old principle be applied loosely. As with all other areas of the State, he wanted to organise this source of revenue in his own way, to the extent that it was to become one of the decisive elements of his foreign policy.

3The campaign administrators (commissaires de guerre) and the army’s paymasters (trésoriers-payeurs) kept a precise record of the money and property seized during the course of military operations. They also maintained accounts showing not only what was needed to provide for the soldiers’ requirements (pay, food, material) but also what was left over. The amounts obtained under the terms of peace treaties were also carefully monitored, up to the ?nal payment by the country in question. A separate set of accounts was kept for each campaign. Napoleon insisted on knowing the ?nancial gains from each ofhis victories, so as to better assess their pro?tability: “I did not make as much from this campaign as I did from the last”, he wrote to Mollien after Wagram. [2] The accounts of the military administration are therefore a primary source of information when investigating whether the war paid for itself. It very likely that this accounts system was not perfect, and it could not have kept track of the army’s every action (or exaction). Plunder, theft and pillage were obviously not included. But my main focus here is how the war ?nanced itself rather than an assessment of its cost.

4The cost of war could also be limited by other, more indirect, means. This included the supply of conscripts by allies and the stationing of French troops in foreign territories. Many of the treaties, in particular those with the German states and the Kingdom of Italy, stated that conscripts should be provided for the Grande Armée. The cost of maintaining these men was covered by the countries concerned, which eased the strain on the French Treasury. The stationing of French divisions or regiments at the expense of the host country, as in Italy, Hanover and Naples, was similarly ?nancially attractive.
I shall begin by looking at what the Grande Armée’s gains were while in enemy territory.

I – Seizure of ‘ordinary contributions’

5Once the troops had entered enemy territory, army administrators went about organising a range of seizures, both in kind and in money. The enemy’s public ?nances were requisitioned and all taxes received were con?scated. In many cases, the local authorities (from the smallest village to the capital itself ) were also ordered to provide additional contributions. All these funds helped ease the ?nancial pressure on the French Treasury, particularly with regard to the troop payroll. The net result, however, was to make the payment of army wages heavily dependent on the capacity of the occupied countries to pay their dues. In wealthy regions such as Germany, Prussia and Austria, the military administration was well provided for, but the situation was quite di?erent in Poland, Russia and Spain. Delays in payment were therefore very common, largely as a result of this unpredictable ?nancing system. In any case, the army had always taken a cautious approach to paying its soldiers. Payment took place at the very end of a campaign. In this way, those who were injured could be paid less and those that were missing did not have to be paid at all.

6There was a large amount of requisitioning (food, cattle, goods, hardware) during the wars. As a rule, it was carried out under the authority of the commissaries (commissaires ordonnateurs), who themselves took their orders from the Paymaster general (Intendant général) of the Grande Armée (Comte Daru). Here again, it was a matter of compensating for the organisational failings of the Grande Armée, particularly regarding provisions. The rapidity of troop movements during the campaigns placed huge strain on the army’s logistical support structure. As a result, soldiers had to live o? the land as best they could. [3]
The ?nal statement for the Domaine Extraordinaire [4] (see appendix) shows that the lion’s share of the ‘ordinary contributions’ (amounting to over 800 million francs) went towards covering military expenditure in situ. The ensemble of elements con?scated, the “natural” consequence of military operations, was part of what came to be called ‘ordinary’ contributions, as opposed to the ‘extraordinary’ contributions exacted as per the terms of the peace accords or alliance treaties, which I shall discuss in the next section.

II – ‘Extraordinary contributions’: the ?nancial side to diplomatic treaties

7During the Consulate, the payments received as a result of the diplomatic treaties went towards balancing the State budget. For example, the sale of Louisiana to the United States brought in 50 million francs. The alliance with the Cisalpine Republic, which was to become the Kingdom of Italy in 1805, provided over 300 million francs for the ?nancing of the French army stationed in Italy. As for Spain, she had to pay nearly 75 million francs. Altogether, France received over 450 million francs by these means.

8After Austerlitz, Napoleon changed the system.

9The millions garnered from the signature of diplomatic agreements no longer brought direct relief to the Imperial budgets. This was Napoleon’s intention. “If [according to Napoleon] the war was to feed the war, then peace, after victory, should furnish the rewards”. [5] And these “rewards” were to bene?t the army and the army alone. Napoleon determined to have the contributions placed in a fund for the Army’s exclusive use. [6] His aim was to have the funds to reward the most deserving soldiers and to erect structures in France that celebrated the glory of the Empire and its military might, at the losers’ expense. To this end, an entity known as the Extraordinary crown domain (Domaine Extraordinaire de couronne) was created, “comprising land and property that the Emperor, exercising his right to peace and to war, acquired in the course of military conquests or treaties”. This state within a state, whose income and expenditure was not controlled in any way or governed by any law, was to be managed at the sovereign’s complete discretion. It was a “slush fund”, but one which was entirely above board.

10Once the military operations had been completed, each major town or city, each state occupied by the French army was assigned a contribution commensurate with its level of wealth. And this took no account whatsoever of the amounts already exacted by the army paymasters (intendance) during the war, nor did it include the ‘unauthorised’ seizures made by o?cers and other lower ranks. As Napoleon wrote to Daru: “If they [the Prussians] want to bring to account stealing that individual o?cers may have undertaken, instead of them being in debt to me, it will be I who am indebted to them”. [7] All told, Europe as a whole, apart from Italy, had to pay ‘extraordinary contributions’ worth about 414 million francs between 1805 and 1813. A list is given in the appendix.

11Of this total, only 276 million was actually paid into the co?ers of the ‘extraordinary domain’ (Domaine Extraordinaire). Prussia, for example, never completely settled all its ‘extraordinary contributions’. This led to a period of occupation lasting nearly seven years, as the territory could not be liberated until all the payments had been made. However, as the historian Michel Kerautret points out, “the issue of this contribution was to poison (and the term is not excessive) Franco-Prussian relations for the years following [the peace of Tilsit]”. [8]

12The funds contained in the ‘extraordinary domain’ (Domaine Extraordinaire) enabled Napoleon to distribute millions of francs to the dignitaries in his regime and to erect monuments in Paris. He also lent 154 million francs to the French Treasury, which I have included in my calculation of the self?nancing war.

13In total, the ‘extraordinary contributions’ brought in over 600 million francs (454 million paid directly and 154 million borrowed from the ‘extraordinary domain’ (Domaine Extraordinaire)).
All in all, including ‘ordinary contributions’, France’s allies and the countries defeated by Napoleon paid directly or indirectly over 1.4 billion francs, in itself a considerable sum. Paradoxically, without the wars this system was in serious danger of grinding to a halt. In 1802, after the peace of Amiens, hundreds of thousands of men suddenly returned to France, threatening to throw the imperial ?nances into disarray; particularly if Napoleon wanted to maintain more or less the same-sized military strength, just in case. It is but a short step from this to saying that the Napoleonic system needed war to function…, but that is another story.

III – Valuable allies

14When his armies were not at war, Napoleon had to “export” his soldiers, in one way or another, in order to lessen the ?nancial burden of maintaining them. Fortunately, so to speak, from 1805 onwards a large number of men were engaged in military operations abroad. Napoleon was categorical on the matter to the Russian ambassador Alexander Kurakin, in 1811: “All troops that are too costly to maintain must be sent to enemy territory; this is how I use them and my ?nances are the better for it”. But enemy territories were not the only solution for dealing with soldiers that weighed too heavily on the imperial ?nances. Numerous regiments were stationed in Italy, Westphalia, Hanover or Naples, all countries administered by members of the imperial family (Murat, Eugène and Jérôme). This “family spirit” brought relief to the empire’s ?nances, saving the regime approximately 130 million francs.

15Foreign conscripts were an additional source of savings.

16As Napoleon’s in?uence grew in Europe, virtually all of his allies (willing or not) had to provide troops for the Grande Armée. The Kingdom of Italy and Spain were among the ?rst countries to supply soldiers in this way for the 1805 campaign. The campaign in Prussia in 1806 saw soldiers called up from the German states, including Bavaria, Saxony and also Westphalia. Since the cost of maintaining these military units (pay, equipment, food) fell entirely upon their home country, this represented a further saving for the imperial ?nances, although no o?cial ?gures indicate precisely by how much. My estimate draws on military statistics (these are reasonably precise in terms of troop numbers and the tours of duty undertaken by these contingents) and is based on the daily cost of maintaining one soldier during the Empire (less than two francs). According to my calculations (for the detail, please see the appendix), the most signi?cant savings were achieved in 1809 (around 56 million francs – over 166,000 men were engaged at the various stages of the campaigns in Germany and Spain that year) and in 1812 (60 million francs). As for total savings, I estimate these to be over 253 million francs. Whilst this ?gure is no more than a rough estimate, nonetheless it gives an order of magnitude in assessing as closely as possible how far the war ?nanced the war.

17Through the use of allied contingents (253 million) and by dispatching French troops to satellite states (129 million), the imperial ?nances thus made savings totalling over 380 million francs. Income generated by the war as a proportion of total military ?nancing
My examination of the various methods used to make the war pay for itself produced the following estimates:

18

  • 809 million francs in ‘ordinary contributions’
  • 607 million francs in ‘extraordinary contributions’
  • 352 million francs in savings achieved through the use of allied conscripts and the sending of French troops to be stationed abroad
This gives a total of around 1.8 billion francs.

19Though large, this ?gure needs to be seen in the context of the additional expenditure incurred by the imperial ?nances as a result of the war. Once the peace of Amiens had broken down, the breakeven point of around 500 million francs, obtained in year X (1802), ceased to apply. Over three billion francs needed to be found. Napoleon’s self-?nancing war supplied just over 600 million. To ?ll the de?cit, France resorted to new tax duties (over 1.2 billion in tax on alcohol and tobacco, 80 million in additional taxation and 137 million in customs duties), the sale of property (232 million) and deposits by o?cers of the state (59 million). Furthermore, the Banque de France and Napoleon himself (via his civil list) lent money to the French Treasury. The ?nal de?cit of around 500 million francs in unpaid expenditure was left for the First Restoration.

20By comparing these ?gures to those for the self-?nancing war, we get a clearer picture of the way in which the various methods of war ?nancing ?t together. Only slightly more than two ?fths (42%) of the additional cost incurred by the war was actually covered by the war itself. [9] The lion’s share (nearly 58%) was met using French resources, namely taxation, the selling of national or communally owned properties, money from the Banque de France or was just left as de?cit (?nally paid by the First and Second Restorations).

21In other words, the war fell a long way short of paying its way in full.

22But with so many imperial victories, why such a limited amount?

23In the ?rst place, the con?ict was not con?ned to battles on land. Around one-third of military spending was devoted to the imperial navy, and it was very di?cult for overseas victories or conquests to pay for themselves. In actual fact, France lost virtually all its colonies after Trafalgar. And despite the heroic exploits of the Surcouf brothers, privateers could provide only very limited resources. And even though Napoleon sought to limit his maritime engagements, he nonetheless invested heavily in ship building and harbour infrastructure, at great expense.

24Second, the incessant round of military campaigns meant the constant conscription of fresh troops and the never-ending need for replacement equipment. The 75% increase in military spending in the 1805 to 1812 period bears eloquent witness to the increasing costs.

25Third, the defeated powers were not always very well o?. This was not only true for Spain and Russia but also for Austria, frequently in a situation of quasi-bankruptcy. Prussia had the greatest di?culty paying its contributions. The ?nancial clout of the German states, as signi?cant as it was, could not be enough to meet the entire cost of the war. And all these countries were seriously a?ected by the continental blockade, particularly in 1810 and 1811.

26Ultimately, the only country genuinely capable of providing the French Empire with enough money to cover the cost of its wars was England, but that country was missing from his list of conquests. In fact France and England were the only two countries independently wealthy enough to withstand several years of war.

27April 1814 saw the fall of the Empire, but the story does not stop here. After the ?rst abdication, the allies were relatively magnanimous towards France (no contributions were exacted), but it was not let o? so lightly after Waterloo. Just as the Grande Armée had done, the allies demanded reparations, at a total cost of 1.4 billion francs (see appendix). All told, the sums exacted from France e?ectively cancelled out the nearly 1.8 billion francs obtained as a result of the war. In other words, the self?nancing war turned out to be an advance that had to be repaid over future generations.
So the war de?nitely did not pay for the war.
But it was paid for (that is to say what could be paid in ?nancial terms) and this must ultimately be Napoleon’s greatest quality, albeit indirectly. Despite the enormous sums needed, the French Empire was never at the root of any public bankruptcy, unlike the preceding regimes, from Louis XIV to the Directory. In addition, Napoleon’s establishing of the French ?scal model in the four corners of Europe marked the beginning of a modern understanding of public ?nance. But this ?nancial rigour came at a price. In France and in the annexed territories, the political price Napoleon paid for re-introducing indirect contributions (tax on alcoholic beverages, tobacco and salt) was as high as that for conscription. More importantly, his desire to make war pay for itself was to scar diplomatic relations in Europe for generations; as evidenced by the grudge that Prussia bore France until 1870 and beyond.

Estimate of savings achieved by France through the use of allied contingents

28To establish the amount saved, I ?rst calculated the number of soldier days which Napoleon got “free of charge”, and then multiplied this ?gure by the daily cost of maintaining a soldier under the Empire.

Number of soldier days for the allied contingents

29My calculations do not take account of the quartering days but only the number of days spent in active campaign service (rather as though Napoleon was using mercenaries). [13] Thanks to the outstanding research carried out by Colonel Carles, [14] I was able to calculate this number of days on a campaign-bycampaign basis. For the Italian Royal Guard, for example, 9,000 men were made available to France during the whole of 1809, which represents a total of 9,000 x 365 = 3,285,000 soldier days.

Daily cost of maintaining a soldier under the Empire

30Thanks to Mollien, we know the daily maintenance cost for a soldier during the Empire (the Treasury minister was after all in the best position to make this kind of estimate). The cost amounts to 1.91 francs (pay, equipment, engineering, travel, and military accoutrements included). [15]

Calculation of the savings generated through the use of allied soldiers

31I then simply multiplied the number of soldier days for the allied contingents by their daily maintenance cost to get the amount saved for each military unit. Using the same example of the Italian Royal Guard, 3,285,000 days equated to a saving of 6.274 million francs (3,285,000 x 1.91) for France.
