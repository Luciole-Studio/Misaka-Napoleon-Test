 “You must make it your guiding principle that the war must feed the war,” Such was Napoleon’s recommendation to Marshal Soult when he appointed the latter général en chef of the Army of the South in Spain.1 The Duke of Dalmatia was also told to take “the most efficient measures possible to provide for the pay and material needs of your army”. The theory expounded by Napoleon was that his campaigns abroad should be self-financing. The three methods used to achieve this goal were: the seizure of money and property (so-called ‘ordinary contributions’ or saisies ordinaires); the financial gain derived from peace treaties; and the amounts saved via the policy of using allied troops or by having French soldiers maintained by the allied states in which they were stationed. Once the army had been equipped and clothed in France at the expense of the French Treasury, it was expected to be self-sufficient thereafter. And there was nothing new about this approach; it had long been the practice of all the armies of the world. Bonaparte, as commander in chief of the army in Italy, had received the same instructions for his first campaign. However, as might be expected given his character, Napoleon was not content to let an old principle be applied loosely. As with all other areas of the State, he wanted to organise this source of revenue in his own way, to the extent that it was to become one of the decisive elements of his foreign policy. The campaign administrators (commissaires de guerre) and the army’s paymasters (trésoriers-payeurs) kept a precise record of the money and property seized during the course of military operations. They also maintained accounts showing not only what was needed to provide for the soldiers’ requirements (pay, food, material) but also what was left over. The amounts obtained under the terms of peace treaties were also carefully monitored, up to the final payment by the country in question. A separate set of accounts was kept for each campaign. I shall begin by looking at what the Grande Armée’s gains were while in enemy territory.

## I) Seizure of ‘ordinary contributions’

 Once the troops had entered enemy territory, army administrators went about organising a range of seizures, both in kind and in money. The enemy’s public finances were requisitioned and all taxes received were confiscated. In many cases, the local authorities (from the smallest village to the capital itself ) were also ordered to provide additional contributions. All these funds helped ease the financial pressure on the French Treasury, particularly with regard to the troop payroll. The net result, however, was to make the payment of army wages heavily dependent on the capacity of the occupied countries to pay their dues. In wealthy regions such as Germany, Prussia and Austria, the military administration was well provided for, but the situation was quite different in Poland, Russia and Spain. Delays in payment were therefore very common, largely as a result of this unpredictable financing system. In any case, the army had always taken a cautious approach to paying its soldiers. Payment took place at the very end of a campaign. In this way, those who were injured could be paid less and those that were missing did not have to be paid at all.

## II) ‘Extraordinary contributions’: the financial side to diplomatic treaties

 During the Consulate, the payments received as a result of the diplomatic treaties went towards balancing the State budget. For example, the sale of Louisiana to the United States brought in 50 million francs. The alliance with the Cisalpine Republic, which was to become the Kingdom of Italy in 1805, provided over 300 million francs for the financing of the French army stationed in Italy. As for Spain, she had to pay nearly 75 million francs. Altogether, France received over 450 million francs by these means. After Austerlitz, Napoleon changed the system. The millions garnered from the signature of diplomatic agreements no longer brought direct relief to the Imperial budgets. This was Napoleon’s intention. “If [according to Napoleon] the war was to feed the war, then peace, after victory, should furnish the rewards”.5 And these “rewards” were to benefit the army and the army alone. ## III) Valuable allies

 When his armies were not at war, Napoleon had to “export” his soldiers, in one way or another, in order to lessen the financial burden of maintaining them. Fortunately, so to speak, from 1805 onwards a large number of men were engaged in military operations abroad. Napoleon was categorical on the matter to the Russian ambassador Alexander Kurakin, in 1811: “All troops that are too costly to maintain must be sent to enemy territory; this is how I use them and my finances are the better for it”. But enemy territories were not the only solution for dealing with soldiers that weighed too heavily on the imperial finances. Numerous regiments were stationed in Italy, Westphalia, Hanover or Naples, all countries administered by members of the imperial family (Murat, Eugène and Jérôme). This “family spirit” brought relief to the empire’s finances, saving the regime approximately 130 million francs. Foreign conscripts were an additional source of savings. As Napoleon’s influence grew in Europe, virtually all of his allies (willing or not) had to provide troops for the Grande Armée. The Kingdom of Italy and Spain were among the first countries to supply soldiers in this way for the 1805 campaign. The campaign in Prussia in 1806 saw soldiers called up from the German states, including Bavaria, Saxony and also Westphalia. Since the cost of maintaining these military units (pay, equipment, food) fell entirely upon their home country, this represented a further saving for the imperial finances, although no official figures indicate precisely by how much. My estimate draws on military statistics (these are reasonably precise in terms of troop numbers and the tours of duty undertaken by these contingents) and is based on the daily cost of maintaining one soldier during the Empire (less than two francs). In the first place, the conflict was not confined to battles on land. Around one-third of military spending was devoted to the imperial navy, and it was very difficult for overseas victories or conquests to pay for themselves. In actual fact, France lost virtually all its colonies after Trafalgar. And despite the heroic exploits of the Surcouf brothers, privateers could provide only very limited resources. And even though Napoleon sought to limit his maritime engagements, he nonetheless invested heavily in ship building and harbour infrastructure, at great expense. Second, the incessant round of military campaigns meant the constant conscription of fresh troops and the never-ending need for replacement equipment. The 75% increase in military spending in the 1805 to 1812 period bears eloquent witness to the increasing costs. Third, the defeated powers were not always very well off. This was not only true for Spain and Russia but also for Austria, frequently in a situation of quasi-bankruptcy. Prussia had the greatest difficulty paying its contributions. The financial clout of the German states, as significant as it was, could not be enough to meet the entire cost of the war. And all these countries were seriously affected by the continental blockade, particularly in 1810 and 1811. Ultimately, the only country genuinely capable of providing the French Empire with enough money to cover the cost of its wars was England, but that country was missing from his list of conquests. In fact France and England were the only two countries independently wealthy enough to withstand several years of war. April 1814 saw the fall of the Empire, but the story does not stop here. After the first abdication, the allies were relatively magnanimous towards France (no contributions were exacted), but it was not let off so lightly after Waterloo. Just as the Grande Armée had done, the allies demanded reparations, at a total cost of 1.4 billion francs (see appendix). All told, the sums exacted from France effectively cancelled out the nearly 1.8 billion francs obtained as a result of the war. In other words, the self- financing war turned out to be an advance that had to be repaid over future generations. So the war definitely did not pay for the war. But it was paid for (that is to say what could be paid in financial terms) and this must ultimately be Napoleon’s greatest quality, albeit indirectly. ## How did the financing of Napoleon's campaigns reflect his view on how wars should fund themselves?

Napoleon advocated for a self-financing war system, wherein the demands from conquered territories and diplomatic treaties would cover the expenses of his military endeavors. He believed that the wars should sustain themselves, using the seized wealth from victories to bolster the army. His policy dictated that contributions from treaties should not relieve state budgets but fund the rewarding of soldiers and the erection of monuments. This reflects a dual vision of operational pragmatism and strategic glorification of his military achievements .

## Discuss the implications of making diplomatic treaties financially beneficial for the French Treasury during Napoleon's era.

Making diplomatic treaties fiscally advantageous ensured that France could offset some of the costs incurred from sustaining its military campaigns. By embedding financial clauses into these treaties, Napoleon aligned military success with economic gain, enhancing France's financial resilience. This approach, however, could engender diplomatic tensions, as it placed economic burdens on defeated nations and allies, highlighting a precarious balance between short-term financial gain and long-term diplomatic relations. This policy effectively transformed treaties into instruments of fiscal policy, emphasizing economic extraction over mere cessation of hostilities .

## Why did Napoleon's military financing system particularly emphasize the use of allied troops, and how was it beneficial?

Napoleon's financing system utilized allied troops to offload the cost of sustaining a large standing army. By deploying allied contingents, Napoleon effectively reduced the financial strain on the French Treasury as these troops were maintained at the expense of the allied states, thus providing significant cost savings. For instance, during the Italian campaign, the use of Italian troops represented a considerable saving of soldier maintenance costs. This strategic deployment of allied troops allowed France to channel its resources more efficiently towards other military and imperial objectives .

## Analyze the strategic rationale behind Napoleon maintaining a large number of troops engaged abroad after the Peace of Amiens in 1802.

Post-Peace of Amiens, Napoleon faced the threat of economic destabilization from demobilizing a massive army. The strategic rationale to maintain troops engaged abroad was manifold: it reduced unemployment risks within France, minimized the economic burden of augmenting army size, and continued to derive fiscal benefits via territorial requisitions. This strategy also ensured France retained a military edge and deterrence capacity, ready to tackle renewed hostilities. Engaging troops internationally allowed Napoleon to manage internal financial pressures while keeping his imperial ambitions intact .

## What were the implications of Napoleon's reliance on "unauthorized" seizures by his officers during his campaigns?

Napoleon's toleration of 'unauthorized' seizures by his officers during campaigns added a layer of complexity to the financial and diplomatic dimensions of his wars. While it allowed for more flexible requisitions, it caused disparities in wealth accrued by different officers, potentially undermining discipline and uniformity. Furthermore, it complicated post-war financial settlements with occupied territories, as seen in his correspondence, which humorously suggested that accounting for these would result in a net debt to Prussia rather than a benefit .

## How did the financial strategies implemented during the Napoleonic Wars illustrate the economic pressures faced by France?

The financial strategies during the Napoleonic Wars show France's need to alleviate economic pressure through external means. By employing requisitions, demanding contributions from occupied territories, and utilizing allies to bear the costs of garrisoning troops, Napoleon sought to reduce the financial strain on France's Treasury. The reliance on 'ordinary' and 'extraordinary' contributions from these regions illustrates these pressures, highlighting France's inability to sustain prolonged military campaigns purely from domestic resources. This dependency underscores the balance Napoleon attempted between military ambitions and economic stability .

## Evaluate how Napoleon's creation of the "Domaine Extraordinaire" reflected his approach to state and military finance.

The creation of the "Domaine Extraordinaire" exemplified Napoleon's pragmatic and centralized approach to military finance, reflecting his broader strategy of ensuring that military gains were translated into political and economic capital controlled directly by him. This autonomous financial entity allowed Napoleon to channel resources towards military rewards and grand state projects without legislative oversight, underscoring his view that military victories should self-finance further military and state endeavors. This reflects his adeptness in consolidating power and exerting financial control independently of conventional state structures .

## How did the Napoleon's system of financing military campaigns depend on the economic capacity of occupied territories?

Napoleon's system relied heavily on requisitions and contributions from occupied territories. The financial support needed to pay troops was extracted from seized local public finances, confiscated taxes, and additional contributions demanded from local authorities. This approach made the French army's payroll dependent on the capacity of these regions to pay their dues. Wealthier areas like Germany, Prussia, and Austria provided ample resources, whereas poorer regions like Poland, Russia, and Spain led to delayed payments due to their lesser economic capability .

## In what ways did Napoleon's financial policies affect Franco-Prussian relations after the Peace of Tilsit?

Napoleon's financial policies had a lasting impact on Franco-Prussian relations post-Tilsit. The "extraordinary contributions" exacted from Prussia led to prolonged hostilities and resentment, as they struggled to meet these financial demands, resulting in extended occupation periods. This created a climate of distrust and animosity, souring diplomatic relations. The occupation marked by the delayed payments illustrated the coercive financial burden imposed on Prussia, poisoning relations in subsequent years as evidenced by historian Michel Kerautret's description .

## What role did the "extraordinary contributions" play in Napoleon's financial strategy post-victory, and how were they managed?

The 'extraordinary contributions' were significant in Napoleon's financial strategy as they transitioned the burden from the state budget to a more isolated financial pool benefiting mainly the army and Napoleon’s personal projects. These contributions were derived from diplomatic treaties, with the income directed into the 'Extraordinary crown domain', a fund managed discretely by Napoleon. This fund was utilized to reward soldiers and maintain imperial grandeur, showing Napoleon's vision of military success funding further glory, shifting away from direct state relief .
