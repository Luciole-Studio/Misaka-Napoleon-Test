 Both Britain and Revolutionary France became ‘armed

I shall consider Britain first, and I shall separate out the two services, military and naval since their 
experiences are different.

I) The British land militar
Before 1793 there were two systems whereby a man could choose, or be forced, to join the military. 
One was traditional army recruitment. The second was the militia. In 1793, this latter was organised 
according to a law of 1757 whereby every English and Welsh county had to supply (and pay) a given 
quota of men (aged between 18 and 45) who had been selected by ballot (although the buying of 
substitutes was legal and widely practised). The total sought by the government in 1793 was 32,000. 
Those balloted would be under martial law during active service, and in peacetime they were obliged to 
do a month’s military training under the leadership of voluntary local gentry. The system was however 
only partly effective. In 1796, for example, in principally agricultural areas (territories therefore low in 
population) such as Dorset, Bedfordshire and Montgomeryshire (Wales) the proportion of eligible men 
serving in the militia was one in ten. In highly populated new industrial areas such as the West-Riding 
of Yorkshire and Lancashire the proportion could be as low as one in thirty and even one in forty-five. 
In the end, however, since those balloted were only asked to perform at home this service bore on them 
only lightly. 
As for the regular British army, it traditionally had significant problems raising anything like sufficient 
numbers of men, and it only attracted the poor or socially un-adapted (petty thieves, outsiders, bastard 
getters, etc.). Indeed it seems clear from the contrasting recruiting experiences of the army and navy 
and the size of their manpower, that the army was the navy’s poor cousin. In 1793 the army numbered 
a mere 45,000 men, of which two-thirds were stationed abroad.3
 Clearly this was no match for the 
huge continental armies, particularly France with its population three times that of Britain and where 
conscription levels reached 60, 80 and 100 thousand men per year, rising to 2 million for the years 
1800-1814, in other words 36% of all mobiliseable men and almost 7% of the French population as a 
whole. Given the shortfall in numbers in the British army, a major effort had to be made to encourage 
large numbers of men to step up to fight for their country.

The act had indeed prefigured this, and it has been ventured that the government preferred arming a 
wealthy (and so establishment) amateur rather than a peasant whose loyalties might be unpredictable. 
Then came the military defeat in the summer of 1797, where France beat the First Coalition, and in the 
following autumn the young general Bonaparte began dictating terms to Austria. Now Britain found 
herself faced the direct threat of invasion in the shape of the menacingly named ‘Armée d’Angleterre’ 
massing on the coasts of northern France. In its scramble for manpower, the British government not 
only set in motion the first ever national census (if you are out to get soldiers, you need to know the size 
of your population) but also more specifically military aimed measures with the Defence of the Realm 
Act of 1798. In this the state asked each county the number of men present within county borders and 
whether those men were willing to fight if the invasion took place. Almost simultaneously with the act 
came a remarkable knee-jerk civilian reaction to volunteer. In the four months between April and July 
1798, the number of volunteers increased from 54,000 to 116,000.4
 Indeed, the occurrence was so 
spontaneous that the British government was taken by surprise and almost worried by the amplitude of 
the civilian response, acting quickly to provide a legal framework for it. And the sheer numbers caused 
fears in government that an armed populace might be dangerous. Some in parliament, however, notably 
William Dundas (soon to be Secretary at War), were confident in fact that patriotic action together 
would serve as the cement which would bring ‘British’ society together. Dundas himself remarked 
(referring to Scottish volunteers of the 1790s) ‘You will recollect, many parts of this country which were 
most disaffected, but were insensibly cured of it by being enrolled under arms along with others of a 
different description. If, on the other hand, they are not so associated, they become prey to the intrigues 
of traitors and enemies, being debarred the privilege of bearing arms on the right side’.5
 Parliament also 
felt that the public in its wisdom had chosen the mode of defence most suitable for Britain, and some 
high-profile politicians, such as Pitt, became colonels of their own volunteer regiments.

marginalized by military participation as the lower orders were brought into closer contact with and

But the huge mass of volunteers in 1803 proved difficult to manage. Size alone, 380,000 men, 
was a problem. By August, the government limited the volunteer mass size to six times the militia 
establishment, but it was soon realised that training and arming such enormous numbers was completely 
impracticable. Local feelings also led to opposition to official army control. There were many small 
corps which refused amalgamation, men in the corps refused to serve outside their localities (it was 
national service but on their own terms). And the whole was operating almost completely outside the 
control of military law.
In short, the situation up to 1804 can be described, with Cookson, as illogical and incoherent. 
In 1796, Dundas had reacted to the changes in the strategic system by enlarging the militia. In 1798 
the same minister had encouraged the volunteer formation, thus starving the militia of men. In 1799, 
the militia had then further been weakened as men were taken from it to be used in a regular army for 
offensive operations. In 1803, Addington and his party returned to the militia as the bed-rock of home 
defence (the Army Reserve scheme with its balloted recruitment) but at the same time encouraged the 
volunteer movement. To quote Cookson, “the volunteers starved the militia system, the militia starved 
the army and the army had negligible authority over the bulk of the country’s fighting men.”6
 In the 
face of these obvious shortcomings, Windham – as war minister – turned almost exclusively in favour 
of compulsory and not voluntary service. Pitt too (as Addington had) came to consider levy en masse as 
appropriate, if only as a pis aller. In his speech to the Commons of 18 July 1803, Pitt noted that he did 
not “like the idea of waiting for the slow progress of a ballot” […] but that “unless volunteers should, 
within a certain date, comply with the condition prescribed, their consent should not be waited for”.
And so after the allied debacle of 1806, all volunteers came to be subsumed by the militia model. 
And later, as the British began finally to see success in the Peninsular campaign, even traditionally 
sluggish army recruitment began to take off as men fancied playing on a winning side. The great

II)The Royal 
Simultaneously with this huge drive to arm the nation, stood the permanent counter-point of naval 
recruitment. The primary service Britain in terms of investment and importance in the Napoleonic 
period was the navy. And as befitted Britain’s primary defence and attack force, the mere 16,000 men 
before 1789 rose to a staggering 140,000 by 1812.7
With the beginning of the war with Revolutionary France in 1793, parliament decreed that the 
manpower in the Royal Navy should increase to 45,000 (the population of England before the first 
census of 1801 was estimated at 8.6 million). The total number of men required for the fleets rose to 
85,000 in 1794 and 120,000 in 1799. Three methods were used in the attempt to meet these quotas: 
volunteers: the pressgang and (post-1795) the Quota Acts.
A man who entered the navy as a volunteer was given his conduct money and two months pay 
in advance. However becoming a sailor was also a way of avoiding debtors’ prison, since the Navy 
protected a man from his creditors provided that the debt was less than twenty pounds. On the other 
hand, it was not always clear whether a volunteer had not in fact been pressganged – often men captured 
by the pressgangs were given the chance to volunteer and thus receive their pay. Volunteers were much 
appreciated and usually made the best crewmen, as the expression ran ‘Better one volunteer than three 
pressed men’.
As for the Impress service, this ancient organisation came into high profile during the wars with 
Revolutionary France. Each man ‘impressed’ received a loan (or prest) of a ‘shilling’ (that is he paid the 
‘King’s shilling’ to enlist) and became a ‘(im)prest man’. The service was present in every major port in 
the kingdom, and the regulating officer in charge, and the local hard men he hired as ‘gangers’ would 
roam the countryside attempting to ‘encourage’ men aged between 18 and 55 to join the navy. No-one 
was safe from the gang, and often the only escape route when captured was to bribe the gang or to join 
it. A preferred target for the pressgang was the merchant navy, so it was not infrequent to find special

The final form of recruitment was the quota system. It functioned rather like the Defense of the 
Realm act and was enshrined by two acts of parliament (or Quota acts) passed by Pitt in the March and 
April of 1795. In these, every county was required to supply to the navy a quota of men, in proportion 
to the county population and the number of ports – for example, London was asked to provide 5,704 
men, whilst Yorkshire, the largest county, was obliged to offer 1,081. Although considerable rewards 
went with replying to the call of the quota, this was in fact a system of conscription. And the quota 
selectors naturally chose men who were landsmen (the sailors had already been taken by the press gangs) 
and for local reasons they often selected the local ‘bad sort’. As with volunteering, small time criminals 
were sometimes given the choice of a prison sentence or service in the Navy. Given the exceedingly 
rough justice prevalent in 18th-century prisons, many preferred the call of the sea. Those chosen could 
also pay for substitutes. 
All three systems were remarkable in that they produced the goods. The census of 1801 recorded 
that the numbers of men voted and those actually serving were largely the same.8
 And perhaps a more 
surprising entry recorded in the 1801 census was the confirmation that naval manpower was made up 
of willing recruits. And this parity in government desires and actual figures continued at least until 
1804. Statistics furthermore show that whilst 75% of the men aboard were the product of compulsory 
service they were nevertheless also seasoned seamen.
Indeed, the striking feature of the navy is the relative success not only of its operations but also 
its recruitment. Contrary to popular belief, Royal Navy life was not hell on sea. British warships were 
cleaner and less uncomfortable than their merchant counterparts (the source of much recruiting), food 
and drink was plentiful. To be sure discipline was tough, but excessive brutality was not condoned by 
the Admiralty. Sadistic officers were beaten up on shore and sometimes even sued by their men. Indeed, 
as N. A. M. Rodger has recently pointed out, the navy’s successes would be hard to explain if the navy 
had been ‘a sort of floating concentration camp’. The navy was filled by volunteers and crews often 
devotedly followed beloved officers.

III) French
In the French army of the Revolutionary and Napoleonic period, the prevailing model was 
conscription and obligatory long-term service. As the politician Dubois-Crancé memorably (and 
presciently) noted in December 1789 to the Assemblée: “I hold it as axiomatic in France that every 
citizen must be a soldier and every soldier a citizen, or we will never have a constitution.” Indeed, ‘gardes 
nationaux’ (a sort of bourgeois militia) sprang up after the fall of the Bastille. These corps were composed 
of men who took up arms as volunteers and as they did so, their desire to protect the Nation was the act 
of a citizen. These regiments of national guard were barely recognised however by the legislature, which 
merely opened enrolment registers. This passivity as regards volunteers changed on 20 April, 1792, with 
the declaration of war. Now the Assemblée set about organising the volunteers by raising vast levies, 
calling every citizen able to bear arms to serve the ‘patrie en danger’. These novel ‘volunteer battalions’ 
were designed to be complementary to the army, but the volunteers only signed short term service 
agreements. When these lapsed after a year, most volunteers left the battalion, forcing the Convention 
to pass a decree (dated 24 February, 1793) ordering an obligatory levy of 300,000 men. Whilst the men 
called up were still described as volunteers, they were essentially conscripts (but with paid replacements 
allowed). The decree of 23 August of the same year stipulated the obligatory call up of all citizens aged 
between 18 and 24. However, initial abuses caused by a system based on the arbitrary date of 23 August 
1793 caused much unhappiness and a great deal of desertion – those born on 22 August were not to be 
called up for years and those turning 25 on 24 August saw themselves obliged to serve for three or four 
supplementary years. In the period from the beginning of 1794 up to Campoformio in 1797, numbers 
dropped from a theoretical number under arms of 1.2 million (actually 800,000) to 365,000 men. This 
catastrophic situation was solved by the passing of the Jourdan-Delbrel law of 19 Fructidor An VI (5 
September 1798), which ordered the forced conscription in peace time of all 18-21 year olds and in 
wartime all 18-24 year olds. In peace time, service was to last 4 years in the infantry and 6 in the cavalry. 
In wartime, conscripts had to stay in their regiments until the end of hostilities. Conscripts were chosen 
by ballot, but after a request by Delbrel, they were allowed to pay for replacements. This extremely harsh 
form of military service which firmly placed the army ‘at the heart of French society’, remained coercive 
and based on conscription throughout the period 1793 to 1815. The system was thus set in motion 
four years before Napoleon came to power. But he was to use it with great efficacy throughout all the

IV) French N
On St Helena, Napoleon was to remark: “the navy of Louis XVI had been destroyed when I 
came to power”.9
 Indeed as early as 1792, the French navy was profoundly disorganised by massive 
emigration amongst French navy officers – 1,200 out of the 1,657 recorded for the Grand-Corps on 1 
January 1789.10 And during the Terreur noble naval men who did not emigrate were thrown out of the 
navy and imprisoned. The shortfall in the Revolutionary navy was made up by men from the French 
merchant navy, though these had no military experience and were often appointed for political reasons. 
This unsatisfactory situation was corrected by the passing of a law of 25 October 1795 (further corrected 
by Bonaparte by an Arrêté of 17 August, 1800), which obliged officer recruits to have minimum age 
and experience. The situation was just as poor with respect to simple seamen. Maritime recruitment 
during the ancient regime had never provided enough sailors, and things got worse when the French 
navy’s traditional recruiting grounds, the west and the south east, joined the Counter-Revolution. Naval 
defeats, captured corsairs and ships boarded during blockades compounded the manpower problems. 
And when the sailors returned to France after Amiens, they were simply to be retaken again in the 
first hostilities of the war as it began again. There were furthermore desertions (ranging from 15% to 
30% for certain vessels), with men passing to corsairing. And recourse to a French version of the press 
gang merely brought in men who were unsuited or unwilling to serve properly. Other men came from 
the maritime equivalent of the military conscription, indeed sometimes they were landsmen, whose 
presence on board ship was of doubtful utility. After Trafalgar, such non-naval men were excluded from 
the naval conscription, and conscription was confined to coastal areas.11

V) Conclusi
Both Britain and France were thoroughly militarised societies, both in army and naval terms. 
They were the living examples of the ‘armed state’. The contrast between the different experiences in 
conscription and voluntary military service is however striking. Cooke maintains that the reasons for the 
massive insubordination in France are mostly obvious. Firstly the fact that the conscription in France 
was coercive, and secondly the fact that it was only aimed at a narrow band of the population (single 
men aged between 20 and 25 years old) and they were obliged to give unlimited service. Alan Forrest 
in his definitive study Conscripts and Deserters: the Army and French Society during the Revolution and 
Empire (Oxford 1989) gives ample description of the hundreds of thousands of Frenchmen who evaded 
service either by desertion or by disobedience of conscription laws. On the other hand, compulsion 
in Britain was carefully circumscribed and therefore better accepted. Substitution was a much more 
effective part of the British system than it was in France – although it was perceived by the middle 
classes as a war tax. Whilst it is true that opposition to military services in Britain occurred in the 
decade preceding the Napoleonic period - Ireland in 1793, England in 1796-7 (namely, the Spithead 
and Nore mutinies) and Scotland in 1797 – but there was nothing in the ‘Napoleonic’ decade and a 
half, including the highpoint of 1803 when 100,000 men were balloted for the militia. Perhaps the 
main reason for the high rates of desertion and avoidance of the draft in France is that after the initial 
battles of the Revolution, the ‘patrie’ was no longer really ‘en danger’. Only in 1815 and Waterloo did 
France see anything like the concept of the volunteer. However, in France, this forced enrolment in 
the school of ‘gloire’ was preparation to become citizens of the ‘Grande Nation’. Though a machine for 
individual coercion, conscription was also a mechanism for inculcating ideas of service to the state. And 
it created men who became the cement of society. In Britain, volunteering and service of the nation 
led to aspirations for political enfranchisement. Here, for the first time, average men through their 
national service in the militia felt part of the nation, and consequently wanted a say in how it was run. 
The ground was thus prepared for the yearning for political influence and the consequent social unrest 
characterised by the Ludditism of the early 1810s and the post-war civil disturbances characterised by 
the Peterloo riot, which were to be the bedrock problems of British politics in the post-war years.
