# A Turner thesis for Europe? the frontier in Napoleonic Europe

## Abstract

This paper is a discussion of the frontiers of the Napoleonic empire. It maintains that these frontiers were more complex and more marked than the international boundaries which the French advance swept aside and that contemporary French imperial administrators saw them as such. It proposes that there was an 'outer' and an 'inner' empire, spheres defined less by the amount of time the French ruled them, than the degree to which they were pacified and, most importantly, than the degree to which their elites accepted French public institutions and acculturated to their public mores. This contention is then compared to the 'frontier theses' as expressed in essays on the American frontier by the historian Frederick Jackson Turner, namely, 'Contributions of the West to American Democracy', first published in 1903, and 'The Significance of the Frontier in American History', which appeared in 1893 (the year the American government declared the frontier officially closed), in which Turner stressed most directly, the ability of events on the frontier to mould and drive the political culture of the heartland.

## A TURNER THESIS FOR EUROPE? THE FRONTIER IN NAPOLEONIC EUROPE By Michael BROERS *

In May, 1808, Napoleon arrived at the Chateau de Serrant, in the heart of the Vendée-Militaire, the seat of the Vaubrun, a family of the sword nobility of formerly unimpeachable royalist, counterrevolutionary credentials. The visit was a tangible manifestation of the doctrine of ralliement, so important in the western departments even after the nominal pacification of the early years of the Consulate. 1 To honour the visit, on their part, the Vaubrun refurbished a suite of rooms in the imperial style of the times, to accommodate the Emperor, and they remain the glory of this otherwise modest chateau to this day. They are preserved in mint condition because Napoleon never stayed in them, for no sooner had he arrived than news reached him -in the drawing room of the Vaubrun -of the rising of the Dos Mayo against his brother Joseph, in Madrid. Accordingly, he hurried south to defend his new frontier. When the dust had barely settled on his first, deceptively successful Spanish campaign, Napoleon received news in Valladolid, of the passive -but still irksome -legal resistance of the feudal nobility of the Grand Duchy of Berg to his administrators' attempts to abolish seigniorial obligations.

His reply was the Valladolid Decrees which, although they did little to undermine the material well being of the north German aristocracy, still 'hurt the pride of the privileged classes', in the words of Beugnot, Napoleon's chief minister in Berg. 2 A few months later, Napoleon had rallied his new allies in southern and western Germany, together with his own troops, to confront the renewed hostilities of the Habsburgs. In a time of crisis, the boundaries of Napoleon's realm emerged very clearly, and they were clear for all to see.

It is, therefore, a fundamental point for this paper, that the Napoleonic empire had frontiers, that they were more complex and more marked than the international boundaries the French advance swept aside, and that contemporary French imperial administrators saw them as such. The frontiers discussed here are not the well defined, 'static' boundaries of the Hexagon. Although my definition of the Napoleonic imperial frontier subsumes, most obviously, the Pyrenean border between France and Spain -described by Peter Sahlins as one of the oldest, most stable borders in western Europe foot_3 -it does not correspond to stabile concepts of national borders. This is not the relevant context for imperial expansion, in almost any historical circumstances, and this is why, in the first instance, aspects of Turner's thesis have a certain attraction.

And the new regime had an in-built penchant for geographic certainty. In his study Frontières de France, Daniel Nordman has pointed to the penchant of Napoleonic officials 'to draw a demarcation line' when examining linguistic differences, to work in purely geographic terms, in contrast to the approach of the local notables they gleaned their information from, who thought in terms of hierarchies of language, and of gradations between local patois.

If not yet specifically nationalist in intent, the new state did assume clear regional divides, of marked frontiers. 4 ere was, I think, an 'outer' and an 'inner' empire, spheres defined less by the amount of time the French ruled them, than the degree to which they were pacified and, most importantly, than the degree to which their elites accepted French public institutions and acculturated to their public mores.

In common with the evolution of many other imperial frontiers, flux was present: Broadly, western Germany and northern Italy began as 'frontier' regions, but were eventually acculturated, and the frontier moved on, after the military conquests of 1805-1807. Contemporary Frenchmen obviously thought so. There were 'non-French' parts of Europe that were clearly 'of the Empire' -within the Ciceronean circle, as it were -even if they were not actually imperial territory. Hence, Norvins de Montbreton on Baden, as he recalled his time there as imperial chargé d'affaires: 'The Grand Duchy became a second Alsace, united, rather than separated, by the Rhine, to the point it was impossible to be more in France than in that German state.' foot_5 Such men judged by French standards and they were impervious to many complex realities in those judgements. Yet their belief that some parts of Europe were as French as France had the tangible effect of sharpening the borders of the true frontier for them.

They knew where it began; its existence was a reality for them. There was a heartland; there was a frontier.

By 1808, it seems clear that for all Napoleon's military and diplomatic domination of Europe, his political system had reached the limits within which it would find societies amenable to acculturation. 6 deed, the process of demarcation could sometimes work in reverse. In 1808, the French entered Tuscany filled with great hopes they would find common ground with a state renowned for its enlightened, reforming traditions; within months, however, they came to believe these reforms had been only skin deep. Tuscany soon became 'Indian country' in their eyes. 7 When it advanced further east and south, it was on alien ground, and that was where and when the influence of the frontier on the heartland became most intense. Thus, what concerns me most here, is the impact of administering and attempting to acculturate the frontier on those despatched from the imperial heartland.

Those boundaries were cultural, as well as military and political. They were drawn exactly because the Napoleonic state was, fundamentally, the heir and militant proselytiser of a vision of political culture and, indeed, of civilisation, rooted firmly in the ideology of the French Revolution that sat ill with the societies of eastern and southern Europe, as well as with much of 'old France', notably -but not solely -the Vendée-Militaire. French civilisation, not merely the Napoleonic state, had its limits, and it had to fight on them. Not just this, for on these margins, its enemies were clear, and they were not purely military: In Naples, Murat famously declared he wished most to be remembered as the destroyer of feudalism; in Spain, Joseph sought to ban bullfighting, while Lannes stabled his cavalry's horses in the side chapels of Pamplona Cathedral. The decree of 15 April, 1811, reorganising the Illyrian Provinces is, perhaps, the starkest evidence Napoleon got a lot of answers to this he did not like, but the immediate point is the mission and the message. It was loud and it was clear -and it translated into the particular from this general statement of intent. This amounted to a policy of 'Gallicization', both in its brief to establish the mores and institutions of Revolutionary and Napoleonic France throughout the empire, and in its desire to turn other Europeans -as well as other Frenchmen -into Frenchmen. All this was axiomatic to the servants of the regime, from Napoleon himself down to the Frenchmen at the helm of local government in the imperial possessions. Revolutionary battles continued on the frontiers of the empire, against the oldest enemies -the Church, the aristocracy, and the 'thousand Vendées', as so many French officials called so many parts of the Italian departments.

All this is well known. The demolition of the Napoleonic Empire as a tawdry imitation of the Ancien Régime has long been under way. However, there are still clear paradoxes in need of unravelling.

The socially conservative image of the Napoleonic regime is far from unfounded. Napoleon made no attempt to create a new elite at local level within the core of the empire. The archives, themselves -so well exploited by Louis Bergeron and Guy Chaussinand-Nogaret -are testimony to its determination to seek out and attach 'old money' and 'solid citizens' to itself in the provinces. Les masses de granit 9 was what the regime, itself, not later historians, dubbed them. More to the point, in the present context, it was from their ranks -from their sons, to be precise -that a new generation of imperial administrators was to sally forth, to rule the empire and Gallicise its administrés. Isser Woloch, in a particularly prescient essay, has drawn attention to how accurate was the correlation between the new generation of civil servants, concentrated in the institution of the auditeurs of the Council of State, and the masses de granit. Auditeurs had to prove they could command a private income of 6,000 francs per annum, a sum which put them firmly within the ranks of the provincial elites. 10 Even if many families had to scrimp hard to assure such a sum, auditeurs were almost invariably the scions of families among the 600 highest taxpayers of their native departments; and the 600 highest taxpayers were, almost invariably, landowners; and the auditeurs were almost invariably the young fonctionnaires sent forth to be tested on the wildest frontiers of the Grand Empire. The growing weight of empirical evidence would seem to indicate, however, that these young professionals drawn from the most conservative -as quite distinct from, but not entirely excluding, reactionary or royalist -elements in French society, did not behave in accordance with their backgrounds. In many significant cases, the frontier radicalised them.

The residual 'Jacobinism' of the Grande Armée is well known; its ranks were still fed on revolutionary journalism -the title of its official gazette was the Le Père Duchesne -and could attend Mass only with the permission of their officers, as no chaplains were ever assigned to the imperial forces. They returned to France in 1814, as the démi-soldes, the lifeline between the 1790s and 1830. Perhaps even more intriguing, I might venture here, is how the well-healed sons of the masses de granit came to join them, royalist counts and all; for join them, they did.

Why invoke Frederick Jackson Turner, in the particular, as well as the general? First, I must stress that I do so with care, and in a limited manner; the first such, is to emphasise that I would not seek to enter into the fierce and long debate among American specialists as to the significance of Turner's thesis for their own specialism; I am drawn to certain central aspects of Turner's thoughts on the frontier, because they appear to yield insights into the frontiers of Napoleonic Europe. For all the caveats and qualifications attendant on such a step, I feel it is worth doing.

In seeming contradiction, in the course of my own research on Italy, I have come to think that an imperial model is the most illuminating optic through which to study Napoleonic Europe. Indeed, perhaps one of the most salient failings of Turner's thesis, is his failure to see American expansion as predominantly aggressive. However, like Turner's America -and unlike the imperial models I am so often drawn to, particularly Spanish America --the Napoleonic empire was a contiguous land empire.

It expanded directly from a heartland, across a continent, driven by a sense of 'manifest destiny', of national mission that invoked nation-building in harness with, and inspired by, imperialist expansion.

Invoking Turner thus forges the vital link between la Grande Nation and imperialism.

The Napoleonic frontier produced frontiersmen, and they were all of a type, following the Imperial frontier when the 'imperial heartland' became too tame. Unlike Turner's 'closed frontier' of 1893, however, there was never an opportunity for the Napoleonic regime to dispense with the services of its 'roughnecks' all together. None personified this better than Hugues Nardon, Napoleon's school friend and a man with a Terrorist past. He began his Napoleonic career as prefect of the Loire Atlantique, in the forefront of the pacification of the Vendée, and then in the tough Ligurian posting of Montenotte, where his superiors despaired of his hot headedness, and lack of attention to detail, but lauded his zeal. 20 When a serious illness ended his posting to Parma, he recuperated briefly and moved on to serve in Spain, where he was killed on duty, in 1812. 21 The frontier -and a certain kind of 'frontier spirit' that would not have been out of place in a classic western film -was an integral part of the Napoleonic empire and, in timehonoured fashion there was often little to distinguish the 'good, the bad and the ugly' among the police and the bandits they chased.

-Finally, Turner believed in the enduring influence of the frontier on American life:

Long after the frontier period of a particular region…had passed away, the conception of society, the ideals and aspirations which it produced, persist in the minds of the people…(T)he inherited ways of looking at things, the fundamental assumptions of the American people, have all been shaped by this experience…This experience has been wrought into the very warp and woof of American thought. 22 clear caveat is essential, in this case, before any use might be made of Turner's thesis in a Napoleonic context. The Napoleonic imperial enterprise was elitist, if not quite in the sense that most readily springs to mind -that of an hierarchical, authoritarian regime imposing itself on society. Indeed, imperial expansion, both military and administrative, was far more socially inclusive than anything going on within France. The distinction between American and Napoleonic expansion is, rather, that in America, it encompassed large population movements and, especially, it marked the psyche of a whole people, for better or for worse, at least according to Turner. In the Napoleonic case, the imperial adventure probably meant little or nothing to the French masses, and did not exert an appreciable influence on the masses de granit. At least, not directly. The old nobility was largely absent from even local politics -and from the electoral lists -under the Empire; those among them who went to serve on the imperial frontiers were a minority within a minority. 23 For those who took part, however, the result was an intensified sense of French cultural superiority, and a marked commitment to the ambitions and the ideals of a period they saw as truly 'Revolutionary-Napoleonic'. Their best known voice was Henri Beyle, 'Stendhal', but their legacy was more pervasive. It was probably less the lust to resume imperial expansion they retained and passed on, than their keen sense of cultural superiority in a time of national humiliation, that gives the post-history of 'the Napoleonic frontier' its lasting impact. Nevertheless, the aggressive conquest of Algeria begun in 1829, was spearheaded by veterans of Napoleonic imperialism, who modelled the civil administration of the new colony directly on that of the Illyrian provinces.

That legacy was linked, increasingly, with the republican Left, and I do not in any way deny this.

Rather, I would add new, additional, grist to its mill. In the Restoration period, the 'Napoleonic legacy' is usually sought among military veterans and on the fringes of radical politics, while in the late July Monarchy and under the Second Empire, the desire for a lost gloire remains, all too often, vaguely defined.
