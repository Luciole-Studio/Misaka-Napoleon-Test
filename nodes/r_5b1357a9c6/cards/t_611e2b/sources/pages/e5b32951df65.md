Since 1922 historians in general and British economic historians in particular have depended to a very large extent on the late Professor Eli Heckscher's The Continental System : an economic interpretation for their knowledge of the system's effect on the British economy during the Napoleonic Wars. In 1953, however, the publication of the collective work of Gayer, Rostow and Schwartz {The Growth and Fluctuation of the British Economy, 1790-1850) provided for the first time in an organised form a good deal of essential statistical evidence for a study of the period and it became more and more evident that Heckscher's pioneer work had been rendered inadequate by progress in modern research and by changes in the type of question asked by historians. scholarship. The appearance of Professor Crouzet's Le Blocus Continental is an occasion both for rejoicing and for regret : rejoicing in that M. Crouzet has produced the definitive work for which historians have so long been waiting, and, for British scholars, regret that no native historian has had the energy and enterprise to carry out the task, based as it is very largely on material available in the libraries and business archives of Great Britain.

M. Crouzet opens his book with a masterly survey of the British economy, industry by industry, on the eve of the Berlin decree, in style and amplitude reminiscent of Eue Halévy. He stresses the dynamic quality which the new industrialism had bestowed upon the economy. This, coupled to the aggressive and often naive self-confidence for which the British were so notorious abroad, provided the material and the moral foundations for a successful resistance to the « Corsican usurper ». An extraordinarily wide range of published and unpublished sources is drawn upon for the narrative, and important modifications of received opinions emerge.

British historians have tended on the whole to minimise the effects of the blockade on the United Kingdom and its war-effort. They have given undeserved prominence to Napoleon's fortuitous use of certain British goods at certain times, and have emphasised unduly the attempts of British merchants to circumvent the Continental System, chiefly by smuggling (always a picturesque subject for the writer and the reader), notwithstanding the enormous additional cost of, and the often severe limits to, such transactions. M. Crouzet's detailed examination of the trade statistics, and his emphasis, for example, on direct British trade losses through the seizure of ships and the indirect losses to insurance underwriters in London, will serve to redress the historical balance. He gives, for the first time, full weight to the blockade as the cause of British domestic troubles in 1810-12, culminating in the Lud- dite outbreaks, when a revolutionary situation developed, but without revolutionaries to make use of it. Even Canning despaired of his country's future in the summer of 1811, and M. Crouzet is justified in his claim (p. 855) :

« Nous pensons avoir démontré également que le Blocus Continental fut à l'origine de la crise de 1810, ce véritable ouragan qui secoua toute l'économie britannique, ainsi que des difficultés monétaires que l'Angleterre connut de 1808 jusqu'à la fin des guerres de l'Empire, et qui gênèrent sérieusement son effort de guerre ».

Is it too much to ask that M. Crouzet should now turn his attention to the still obscure question of the effects of the Continental System on the economy of Napoleonic Europe? There is no one better equipped for this task. — W. H. Chaloner.
