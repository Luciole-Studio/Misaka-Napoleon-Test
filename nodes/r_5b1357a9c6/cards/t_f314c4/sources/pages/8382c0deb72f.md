This essay argues that Paul's collection and the sponsorship of the Nazarite ritual in Jerusalem concern an identical historical object. Some reflections on the relationship between the collection/ritual and doctrinal issues are presented. It is argued that (a) Paul was in a subordinate position relative to the Jerusalem leaders (especially James); and (b) that the collection Paul performed amongst his own converts served to undermine his message. Game theory is applied to show how the Jerusalem leaders used the collection to pin down Paul and neutralize the latter's insubordination. [Philosophy of Games (Philosophy Compass)](https://www.academia.edu/34232171/Philosophy_of_Games_Philosophy_Compass_)

[C. Thi Nguyen](https://utah.academia.edu/CThiNguyen)

Philosophy Compass

What is a game? What are we doing when we play a game? What is the value of playing games? Several different philosophical sub disciplines have attempted to answer these questions using very distinctive frameworks. Some have approached games as something like a text, deploying theoretical frameworks from the study of narrative, fiction, and rhetoric to interrogate games for their representational content. Others have approached games as artworks and asked questions about the authorship of games, about the ontology of the work and its performance. Yet others, from the philosophy of sport, have focused on normative issues of fairness, rule application, and competition. The primary purpose of this article is to provide an overview of several different philosophical approaches to games and, hopefully, demonstrate the relevance and value of the different approaches to each other. Early academic attempts to cope with games tried to treat games as a subtype of narrative and to interpret games exactly as one might interpret a static, linear narrative. A faction of game studies, self‐described as “ludologists,” argued that games were a substantially novel form and could not be treated with traditional tools for narrative analysis. In traditional narrative, an audience is told and interprets the story, where in a game, the player enacts and creates the story. Since that early debate, theorists have attempted to offer more nuanced accounts of how games might achieve similar ends to more traditional texts. For example, games might be seen as a novel type of fiction, which uses interactive techniques to achieve immersion in a fictional world. Alternately, games might be seen as a new way to represent causal systems, and so a new way to criticize social and political entities. Work from contemporary analytic philosophy of art has, on the other hand, asked questions whether games could be artworks and, if so, what kind. Much of this debate has concerned the precise nature of the artwork, and the relationship between the artist and the audience. Some have claimed that the audience is a cocreator of the artwork, and so games are a uniquely unfinished and cooperative art form. Others have claimed that, instead, the audience does not help create the artwork; rather, interacting with the artwork is how an audience member appreciates the artist's finished production. Other streams of work have focused less on the game as a text or work, and more on game play as a kind of activity. One common view is that game play occurs in a “magic circle.” Inside the magic circle, players take on new roles, follow different rules, and actions have different meanings. Actions inside the magic circle do not have their usual consequences for the rest of life. Enemies of the magic circle view have claimed that the view ignores the deep integration of game life from ordinary life and point to gambling, gold farming, and the status effects of sports. Philosophers of sport, on the other hand, have approached games with an entirely different framework. This has lead into investigations about the normative nature of games—what guides the applications of rules and how those rules might be applied, interpreted, or even changed. Furthermore, they have investigated games as social practices and as forms of life.

Paul’s great game: Russia’s plan to invade British India
David Schimmelpenninck van der Oye*

History Department, Brock University, Ontario, Canada

This article examines the ill-fated Russian expedition to conquer British India in 1801 with a
Cossack host via Central Asia. Undertaken by Emperor Paul I during his brief diplomatic
dalliance with Napoleon, the enterprise proved highly unrealistic and was abandoned less
than a month after it began. I pay special attention to the knowledge that ofﬁcials in Saint
Petersburg had about the regions to be traversed. I conclude that, despite British fears of a
tsarist overland invasion of their South Asian possessions during much of the nineteenth
century, this poorly planned mission was the only Russian attempt ever made.

Peter the Great was a man of unbounded ambition. Toward the end of his life, Russia’s ﬁrst
emperor bequeathed a remarkable plan to his heirs. His reign had already seen semi-medieval
Muscovy metamorphose into a formidable military machine that replaced Sweden as the domi-
nant power in Northern Europe. It was the tsar’s ardent wish that future Romanovs take his
realm even further. ‘In the name of the most holy and indivisible Trinity, We, Peter, emperor
and autocrat of all the Russias’, commanded ‘all … [his] successors to the throne and the govern-
ment of the Russian nation’ to fulﬁl a series of tasks that would make their realm the greatest on
earth. To carry out this sacred destiny, they would have to ‘keep the nation on a constant war
footing’ and, through a series of diplomatic machinations and armed conﬂicts, subjugate all
Europe. As for Asia,
Approach Constantinople and the Indies as closely as possible. … Hasten Persia’s decadence; pene-
trate to the Persian Gulf; re-establish, if possible, the ancient Levant trade and advance right up to the
Indies, the storehouse of the world. (Robert 1860, 1–7)
Peter’s ‘Testament’ was securely ﬁled in the archives at Peterhof Palace. Nevertheless, its contents
were leaked and copies eventually found their way to the French foreign ministry. The Chevalier
d’Eon de Beaumont, a transvestite Bourbon spy, spirited one of them to Paris around 1760, during
the reign of Empress Elizabeth. Some 25 years later, a second copy came across the desk of Platon
Zubov, Catherine the Great’s last favourite, where his Polish secretary happened to read it; she
transmitted the details to a compatriot serving in France’s Polish Legion, General Michel
Sokolnicki.

The Testament was the lodestone of imperial Russian foreign policy right up to the reign of
Nicholas II. For some two hundred years, Romanov emperors and empresses faithfully followed
their illustrious ancestor’s grand strategy for ensuring geopolitical primacy. Nowhere was this
more evident than in tsarism’s perennial ambition to secure a maritime outlet on the Indian
Ocean by conquering Britain’s vast South Asian possessions.
Virtually none of this is true. With a provenance as dubious as its contents, Peter’s Testament
was ﬁrst published in 1812 by Napoleon’s foreign ofﬁce on the eve of his Grande Armée’s inva-
sion of Russia (Blackstock, 1966; Blanc, 1968; Mezin, 2010; Ragsdale, 1980; Reis, 1985). Never-
theless, like the equally spurious Protocols of the Elders of Zion, the tsar’s apocryphal scheme for
world domination has enjoyed a long afterlife. As recently as 1979, an article in Time magazine
(15 January) invoked it to help explain Soviet policy in the Near East. What makes this artefact of
Napoleonic disinformation particularly durable is that it appears to be supported by historical inci-
dents, thereby lending an air of authenticity to the forgery.2
Speculations about Russian designs on British India are a good example of the ways the past
can distort perceptions of the present. For much of the nineteenth century, many Britons detected
ulterior motives in tsarist Central Asian conquests. The seemingly inexorable Russian advance
into Turkestan appeared to presage ambitions for their own colonies to the south. However,
there is little evidence that Saint Petersburg ever seriously contemplated such a move – save

one attempt by Emperor Paul I in 1801. After surveying earlier Russian interest in India and
the immediate diplomatic context, this article will recount the tsar’s quixotic campaign to drive
the British from their colony with some 22,000 Cossacks and consider the implications for the
Anglo-Russian rivalry over Inner Asia during the nineteenth century.
Ironically, the idea that Russians might reach India over land may well have come from Eng-
lishmen. While there had been commercial links between Kievan Rus and southern Asia as early
as the eleventh century, these tended to be carried out through Central Asian intermediaries and
usually went via the Caspian Sea and other waterways (Rybakov 1948). In 1551, Richard Chan-
cellor and two other explorers founded the Company of Merchant Adventurers to New Lands in
London, to ﬁnd the Northeast Passage, or the maritime route along northern Eurasia’s shore to the
fabled Orient. Rechartered as the Muscovy Company four years later by Queen Mary, its efforts
yielded as little success as similar European attempts to discover a Northwest Passage over the
American continent.
One of the company’s more able agents, Anthony Jenkinson, happened to arrive at Ivan IV’s
court in 1557, a little over a year after the tsar’s conquest of Astrakhan had substantially extended
Russia’s southern frontier. This suggested that India’s lucrative markets might instead be reached
over land. Although the Englishman did reach Bokhara and Persia, the subcontinent proved mad-
deningly elusive (Jenkinson 1886).
Russia’s rulers themselves likewise began to seek access to the India trade via Central Asia.
One of the tasks of Tsar Alexis Mikhailovich’s embassy to Bokhara in 1675 was to establish direct
commercial links with the Mughal Empire. The Posol’skii Prikaz (Foreign Ofﬁce) tasked
Makhmet-Iusup Kasimov, a Tatar merchant accompanying the party, to travel from the khanate
to the court of the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb with a letter from the tsar professing his friendship
and proposing regular trade. Among Kasimov’s other duties was to discover how to proceed to
India either via the Caspian from Astrakhan or over land from Western Siberia. In the event,
Kasimov only made it as far as Kabul, when Aurangzeb had him turned back on the grounds
that ‘since time immemorial no Russian ambassadors had gone to India’ (Kobeiko 1884, vii).
Alexis’s heir, Peter the Great, continued to seek a reliable route to India (Donnelly 1975). Like
his father, Peter’s motive was commerce rather than conquest: to rival the great maritime powers
of the day by establishing an alternate path to the Orient’s lucrative markets. At the turn of the
second millennium, Kievan Rus had prospered as a major trading route between north-western

Europe on the Baltic Sea and Byzantium on the Black Sea, ‘from the Varangians to the Greeks’. A
similar logic drove Peter the Great’s Oriental ambitions. As Sergei Solov’ev pointed out,
Having achieved his principal goal [access to the Baltic], … he did not let the East escape his gaze.
Well aware of its importance to Russia, he understood that Russia’s material well-being would
improve if it became the commercial intermediary between Europe and Asia. (1993, 336)
One possibility was through Persia. Already in 1695, Peter dispatched the merchant Semen Mal-
en’kii on a trade mission via the Near Eastern empire to Aurangzeb’s court. While the Mughal
ruler was more welcoming on this occasion, and even entrusted Malen’kii with an elephant to
take as a present to the tsar, Peter’s envoy did not survive the trip back (Lavrentsova 1958).
Artemy Volynskii’s embassy to Isfahan 20 years later was likewise charged with exploring the
possibility of ‘sending merchants through Persia to India’ (Bushev 1978; Ovchinnikov 1965,
36). When the Safavid dynasty fell in 1722, Peter took a more aggressive course. According to
Lindsay Hughes, one of his motives for the Persian campaign he launched that year was ‘as a
follow-up to Russian probes into a region which promised access to the riches of India and
beyond’ (Hughes 1998, 57; see also Kurukin 2010).
Nevertheless, Central Asia remained the most direct route to India, and Peter sent several mis-
sions there as well.3 None was successful. The most spectacular ﬁasco was Prince Aleksandr
Bekovich-Cherkasskii’s ill-fated embassy to Khiva in 1717. Commissioned by Peter the Great,

the expedition was launched primarily to verify reports of rich gold deposits in the region,
although it was also meant to promote commercial ties with the Mughal Empire (Ovchinnikov
1965).4 In the event, virtually all of the prince’s 7000 troops perished at the hands of the
Khivans. It would be over a hundred years before another approach would be made to the
khanate (Solov’ev 1993; Terent’ev 1906).
Peter’s successors did not abandon his quest for a path to India. The polymath Mikhail Lomo-
nosov argued that there are three requisites for ﬁscal prosperity: internal order, external peace, and
foreign trade. He pointed out that Peter had achieved the ﬁrst and second, but the third still needed
to be accomplished. Lomonosov went on to stress that Russia must pay particular attention to
China, Japan and India (Lomonosov 1950). Catherine the Great may well have read Lomonosov’s
summons, but she was certainly very familiar with Montesquieu’s Spirit of the Laws (1962, v. 2),
which pointed out that since the days of the Roman Empire, trade with India had brought wealth.
Catherine therefore likewise endeavoured to increase commercial intercourse with the subconti-
nent (Atkin 1980).
The empress also ﬂirted with less paciﬁc ideas. According to one possibly apocryphal
account, she may already have been tempted in 1791 during a particularly tense moment in
relations with Great Britain. As the Royal Navy set sail for the Baltic Sea, one of her admirals,
Prince Karl Nassau-Siegen, presented a plan for a diversionary strike at India. The scheme was
the brainchild of a certain M. de St.-Génie and it called for a force to march through Bokhara
and Afghanistan to the British colony. Meanwhile, Catherine would issue a manifesto restoring
the Mughal Emperor to the throne. St.-Génie did not foresee too many difﬁculties. The Islamic
inhabitants of the intervening lands would certainly welcome any army that supported a coreli-
gionist. Furthermore, he knew some reliable guides for the Hindu Kush’s passes. The ageing
empress liked the idea, but her conﬁdant, Prince Potemkin, dismissed it as entirely unfeasible,
and St.-Génie’s proposal was quietly shelved (Eton 1799; Idarov 1884).
Catherine’s Persian campaign in the last year of her reign is much better documented.
Responding to Shah Aga Mohammad’s reoccupation of Georgia in 1795, the empress sent a
force of 30,000 troops under the command of her current favourite’s brother, Valerian Zubov,
to conquer Persia.5 She explained to General Zubov that the operation also had important econ-
omic aims, for control over the empire would ‘open up the routes to India’ (Atkin 1980, 32). The

Zubov was all set to invade Persia itself when events back in the imperial capital intervened.
Catherine died in November of that year and her heir, Paul I, who opposed much of his
mother’s politics, immediately ordered a halt to the war (Atkin 1980).
By the end of Catherine’s reign, Russia was contending with a new threat in the form of revo-
lutionary France. The empress had been horriﬁed by the regicidal violence, not to mention the
threat of republican contagion, as French armies marched beyond their borders. Nevertheless,
she hesitated before becoming too actively involved in the First Coalition against France of
Britain, Prussia, Austria and other powers, preferring instead to ﬁnish partitioning Poland. Her
son and heir, Paul I, was initially inclined to remain even more aloof from the turmoil to the
West. Shortly after he acceded to the throne, the new tsar ordered his diplomats to set up defensive

alliances beyond his empire’s borders but otherwise maintain a policy of neutrality in Europe
(Bazhova 1998; Madariaga 1981; Ragsdale 1980; Sorel 1903).
When an ambitious Corsican general in French service, Napoleon Bonaparte, seized the
Eastern Mediterranean island of Malta less than two years into his reign, Paul could no
longer stay on the sidelines. The Orthodox sovereign had long been infatuated with the vener-
able Catholic knightly order that had made Malta its base. Arranging to have himself elected as
its Grand Master, Paul now saw delivering the knights’ erstwhile home from the revolutionary
inﬁdel as his personal crusade. Malta would become one of the top priorities of the tsar’s diplo-
macy for the remainder of his short reign (McGrew 1979; Nastenko and Iashnev 2005; Touman-
off 1979).
Napoleon’s occupation of the strategic island had come in the context of an expedition to
wrest Egypt from the Ottoman Turks as a stepping-stone to conquering India. Not only were
the French eager to avenge the loss of their South Asian colonies to the British a quarter of a
century earlier, during the Seven Years War, they also hoped to deprive their enemy of a major
source of its wealth and prestige. In the event, the campaign proved to be a failure. While the
French expeditionary force was eventually forced to quit Egypt, its commander remained deter-
mined to regain India (Cole 2007; Symcox 2003).
As for Paul, Napoleon’s refusal to return Malta to the Knights of St John led to a declaration of
war on the French republic. Russia now found itself allied with Austria, Britain, Portugal and
Turkey in the Second Coalition against revolutionary France. Although the war witnessed
some brilliant successes by the Russians under General Suvorov in Italy, within two years Paul
was growing increasingly disillusioned with his allies. A joint expedition with the British to
Holland ended in defeat. Meanwhile, once having driven the French out of Italy with his Austrian
partners, the latter now refused to leave the peninsula. To add insult to injury, when Britain
wrested Malta from the French it reneged on the promise it had made to give Russia a foothold
there. By 1800, Paul was more than ready to reconsider his allegiances.
Over the course of the year, Napoleon, now the French Republic’s ﬁrst consul, began to court
the petulant Russian emperor. His efforts failed to bear fruit before Paul was assassinated in a coup
the following year. Nevertheless, the tsar’s anger drove him to re-establish the League of Armed
Neutrality with Sweden, Denmark and Prussia against the British in December 1800. While it was
not a formal declaration of war, London took it as one, and began to assemble a ﬂeet at Great

On 12 January 1801, Emperor Paul I issued an order to the ataman (chief) of the Don Cossack
Host, Cavalry General Vasilii Orlov:
The English are planning to attack me as well as my Swedish and Danish allies with their ﬂeet and
army. I am ready to take them on, but I must go on the offensive and attack them where the pain
will be greatest and their expectations least. Their dominions in India are best of all. It will take us
three, four months at the most, to march from Orenburg to India. I entrust the expedition to you
and your troops. … All India’s riches will be our reward.6
The ataman was to gather his host and proceed from Orenburg via Bukhara and Khiva to the
English colonies on the Indus River. In a second rescript, Paul explained that Orlov’s goal was
to ‘destroy all [British political and economic domination], liberate the oppressed from their over-
lords, gently bring them under our dependence, and divert their trade to us’.7
Orlov was a good choice for the mission. He had enlisted in the Don Cossacks as a private 25
years earlier and had risen rapidly through the ranks. By the Russo-Turkish War of 1768–1774,
Orlov commanded a regiment and was awarded the Order of St George 4th Class for bravery. His
valour at the siege of the Danubian port of Ismail in 1790 during the next war with Turkey won
him a second George’s Cross. Seven years later, the tsar appointed him ataman of the Don

To what extent was the operation Paul’s brainchild? There is some speculation that Napoleon
had proposed a joint Franco-Russian strike at the British colony. On 16 January the Danish
ambassador to the republic reported to Copenhagen that the ﬁrst consul had told him:
The emperor of Russia could not attack England more damagingly or with more advantage than by
sending an army of 100,000 men from the Caucasus across Persia to fall on the English in India.
(Ragsdale 1980, 80)
And in March Napoleon ordered his minister of the navy and colonies to assign two or three ofﬁ-
cers to draw the public’s attention to a ‘projet d’expédition dans l’Inde’ (Napoléon 1861, 80).
Sometime before 1840 details of a project for an ‘expedition to India by land organized by the
First Consul and Emperor Paul I’ were found among the papers of Count Curt von Stedingk,
Sweden’s ambassador to Saint Petersburg at the time. According to the scheme, Napoleon
would order 35,000 troops from his Army on the Rhine to sail down the Danube and
eventually rendezvous with an equivalent Russian force at the southern Caspian Sea port of
Astrabad. Together, the corps would gallop across Persia and Afghanistan to ‘expel
permanently the English from Hindustan and deliver these beautiful and rich lands from the
British yoke’.
The document also included a brief correspondence between Paul and Napoleon in which the
former explained that there would be plentiful forage and fodder along the way, since the soil
there was well irrigated: ‘Rice is abundant. … There are many cattle, sheep and game, and the
fruits are varied and delicious.’ In any case, hadn’t ‘the Second Alexander’, Nader Shah of
Persia, taken the same route in his assault on the Moghul emperor only 60 years earlier, in
1740? Paul conﬁdently expected the trek to take 35 to 40 days (Hoffmans 1840, 39–56).
No such plan has been found in either the French or Russian archives.8 Even if the document
is genuine, there is the problem of timing: Napoleon’s project was to be delivered to Paul by his
envoy, General Géraud Duroc. As Dmitrii Miliutin (1857) points out in his history of the Second
Coalition, Duroc only arrived in the Russian capital in late May 1801, well after Orlov and his
men had returned from their campaign. The evidence in the Russian State Archive for Military
History (RGVIA) suggests that the decision to attack India was Paul’s alone.9 He had every
reason to be vexed, since perﬁdious Albion had denied his beloved Knights of St John their

rightful home on Malta. To add insult to injury, he had news that the Royal Navy was about to
make course for the Baltic.
Paul’s intelligence about the route to India was sketchy at best. As he put it in a postscript to
his orders to Ataman Orlov, ‘my maps only go as far as Khiva and the Amu Darya. Gathering
details [about the way beyond] is your affair.’10 Indeed, according to Aleksei Vigasin, there
were no Russian maps of India before the nineteenth century.11 What little knowledge Saint
Petersburg had of the area was not promising.12 The situation had not improved signiﬁcantly
since Kasimov’s failed attempt to reach India over a century earlier, when Tsar Alexis’ Prikaz
tainykh del (Ofﬁce of Secret Affairs) compiled a very rough route description based on mer-
chants’ and captives’ accounts (Lavrentsova 1958).
Aside from the famous travelogue by the ﬁfteenth-century merchant Afanasy Nikitin, by

among them was army sergeant Filipp Efremov. Taken prisoner in 1774 by insurgent Cossacks
during the Pugachev rising and sold into slavery in Bokhara, the hardy NCO eventually
escaped and made his way via the Tian-Shan Mountains and Western Tibet to Calcutta. In
1786, four years after returning to his homeland, he published his Ten-Year Journey and Adven-

Such was not the case with the Explanations (Ob”iasneniia) a Greek cleric, Metropolitan
Khrisanf, presented to Prince Platon Zubov on the eve of his brother’s Persian campaign

east. Beginning in Egypt and Syria, he travelled through India, Tibet, Afghanistan, Khiva and
Bokhara, eventually reaching Astrakhan in 1795.
Khrisanf’s arrival in the Russian port happened to coincide with the preparations for Cathe-
rine’s Persian campaign. Here was an excellent opportunity to obtain a ﬁrst-hand overview of the
operation’s possible theatre, and the empress quickly summoned the Greek traveller to her capital.
Presented to Prince Platon Zubov for his brother’s use, the Explanations provided a succinct intel-
ligence report of, among others, Persia, India and Central Asia – as the metropolitan reported,
about their agricultural riches, wealth, and populations of these nations and about the possibility of
subduing them, upon the victories of the Russian army in Persia under the command of General
Count Valerian Aleksandrovich Zubov. (Vigasin 1995, 261)
Khrisanf’s Explanations give the impression that vast conquests were entirely feasible. Thus, it
would take only about 20,000 men to occupy north-eastern Anatolia (the Greek author suggested
that an alliance with Persia to take on the Ottomans would be preferable). As for India, the metro-
politan anticipated many nineteenth-century Russian schemes for taking the British colony:
Over this entire region troops of English origin total twenty, perhaps twenty-ﬁve thousand men and no
more than ﬁfty thousand natives. Such is the strength of the army with which England imposes its
power over its extensive domains! Perhaps your Excellency knows how the strength of the British
forces [in India] compares to that of Russia’s army. (Vigasin 1995, 283)
One Russian ofﬁcial in a good position to know the terrain for Paul’s Indian campaign was the
new military governor of Orenburg, Nikolai Bakhmetev. When queried about trade routes to
Khiva and India by the Senate’s procurer-general, Major General Pavel Obol’nianinov, Bakhme-
tev discouragingly reported in late 1800 that proceeding from Orenburg to India was fraught ‘with
many difﬁculties and dangers’. For one thing, the Kirghiz-Kazak Lesser Horde, ‘a savage people’,

perennially menaced the steppes beyond the frontier town. The Khivans to the south were hostile
and dishonest, while the path from Khiva to Kabul was not without peril either. As for the Hindu
Kush, its high mountains were impassable to horses (Ovchinnikov and Sidorov 1965, 406–409).
Paul may well have been emboldened by a more positive memorandum that Major Egor Blan-
kenagel, a retired ofﬁcer who had travelled to Khiva in 1793, had submitted to the State Council in
1797. According to Blankenagel, conquering Khiva and Bukhara would be fairly straightforward,
thereby clearing the way to India (Arkhiv 1888, 625–635). However, a subsequent letter to the
State Council by the previous governor of Orenburg, Lieutenant-General Baron Otto Igelström,
painted a much more sombre picture about the ease of travelling to India (Arkhiv 1888, 642–652).
Despite Igelström’s discouraging intelligence, on 1 February, Ataman Orlov conﬁdently
reported to his master that he would gather a force of 21,000 men, who would be ready to ren-
dezvous at his assembly point within a fortnight.14 Ultimately, Orlov’s host would total 22,507
cavalry. Mostly composed of Don Cossacks, it also included some 500 Kalmyks. Every soldier
reported with two mounts and 1.5 months of provisions (Batorskii 1886).15 Twelve physicians,
as well as translators competent in the languages of Khiva, Bukhara, India and Persia were
assigned to the group, while Orenburg’s governor, Bakhmetev, provided more maps of the area
to the south. Meanwhile, the imperial treasury allotted 1.67 million roubles for the campaign
(Batorskii 1886).

The tsar was clearly impatient. On 7 February, he wrote to the ataman, ‘This expedition is very
necessary and the sooner the better.’16 So important was the mission that Paul released the dis-
graced veteran of Catherine’s recent Turkish campaign, Major-General Matvei Platov, to
command Orlov’s ﬁrst echelon.17
Informed by some rudimentary English and French maps of India, Orlov gave orders to Esaul
(Captain) Denezhnikov to gather more details about the proposed route. His instructions were
extensive and had to be carried out in great haste: ‘Beginning in Orenburg, what is the best
route for our force, across the Kirghiz-Kazakh Steppe … to Khiva, and thence to India?’18
Orlov was interested in both the terrain and the local population, and he sent the esaul to Orenburg
to conﬁdentially question ofﬁcials as well as residents familiar with the Central Asian caravan
trade. The subaltern was also to recruit a Tatar merchant, along with a Cossack, to reconnoitre
the terrain ‘under the pretext of commerce’ and report back by April. All of this was to be
carried out in the greatest secrecy, ‘so as not to incite rumours that might arouse the hostility
of the Kirghiz-Kazakh and other peoples, since the expedition is not meant to disrupt their
peace’ (Batorskii 1886, 56–58). It would have been difﬁcult for the esaul to complete this ambi-
tious task within less than two months. In any case, by 27 February, Orlov’s host was on the move.
The Cossacks initially advanced at a pace of 30–40 kilometres a day. However, they soon
encountered difﬁculties. While they were still well within the empire’s borders, harsh winter
weather began to slow the march. Meanwhile, a poor harvest the previous year made forage
and fodder scarce. For several days the Cossacks went without any food. As the group approached
the Volga in mid-March, a sudden thaw further hampered their progress. One of the commanders,
Major General Andrian Denisov, recalled:
The snow that year was very deep, but just at this time milder weather set in; the melting snow turned
small gullies into large rivers, and we stumbled more and more through water, unable to make out the
road, since the snow was full of water. (Denisov 1875, 240)
Meanwhile, a steady drizzle dangerously thinned the Volga’s ice. Nevertheless, with the help of
the local peasantry, the men managed to cross the waterway, losing only some horses in the
process (Batorskii 1886; Denisov 1875).
The Volga River proved to be the last obstacle. Back in Saint Petersburg, Paul had been assas-
sinated in a coup during the night of 11 March. His son, Alexander I, was now in power, and one

of his ﬁrst acts was to halt the operation. The following day, the new emperor ordered the ataman
‘and all Cossack regiments accompanying you on this secret mission to return to the Don and to

On 25 March, just before Easter Sunday, Orlov received Alexander’s message as his force
rested at an Old Believer monastery on the upper Irgiz River in what is now Kazakhstan.
‘Lads’, he told his assembled troops, ‘God and the sovereign wish you to return to your parental
homes.’ They did not tarry. By late April, they were back in their stanitsas (villages) (Batorskii
1886).
Paul’s assault on India had barely advanced by 700 kilometres. In his biography of the
emperor, Nikolai Schilder rightly concluded:
The expedition was conceived hastily, without any preliminary dealings with Asian rulers, without
gathering the necessary intelligence about the countries through which the Cossacks would
proceed, without preparing rations, inﬁrmaries, and even without any proper routes. If the expedition-
ary force experienced unbelievable difﬁculties on our soil, it is easy to imagine the sad fate that
awaited the men of the Don had they proceeded further, especially beyond [the Government of] Oren-
burg! (1901, 419–420)

time of Paul … every Russian emperor has entertained plans, or at least had plans submitted to

him, either for invading India … or for destroying our position in that country’ (1885, 41). The
Englishman was only partly correct. Throughout the nineteenth century a number of Russian ofﬁ-
