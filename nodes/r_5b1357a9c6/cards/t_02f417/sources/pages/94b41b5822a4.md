## The Program

In 1775 the French scientific periodical, Observations sur la physique, published in its "literary news" an account of an "extraordinary prize proposed by the Academie Royale des Sciences for the year 1778." The prize was inspired by a report of the controller general of finances, Turgot, on the state of saltpeter manufacture and the diminution of its production. The king, it declared, wanted an improvement in produc tion and at the same time an amelioration of the abuses of the fouille, and had decreed that a prize contest be offered under the auspices of the Academy.1 7 .5 3 .1 3 .2 E ( 2 6 -0 1 -0 1 :1

The fouille (literally, digging) was a widespread institution among European governments for the production of the major constituent of gunpowder, which occurred as an efflorescent salt on the surfaces of masonry buildings and on certain soils. Its collection, generally by scraping walls and sweeping stables, the material collected then being leached for soluble salt, resembled agriculture more than any other technology. It had been practiced generally since gunpowder had be come essential for "national defense," that is, from the mid-17th cen tury, sometimes under the direct control of a government bureaucracy, sometimes under lease as a legally protected private enterprise. The op eration of the fouille, which obligated the property owner to submit to the operations of saltpeter hunters who sometimes insisted on scraping even the walls of his living rooms, was an abuse which was deplored even by many of those who practiced it. The necessities of defense, however, could hardly be denied.

Since the invention of gun powder, India had been the major source of all European supply, and the growth of attempts to produce it domestically represented an effort at national self-sufficiency, most notably in France, which was threat ened by England's increasing control of the destinies of India. French saltpeter was produced under lease, and had oscillated over the period 1690-1775 between 700,000 and 1,800,000 kgm. The Seven Years' War saw the end of French India, and gave the French govern ment reason to scrutinize the lessee's conduct of its saltpeter industry. The scrutiny revealed that the lessee had not merely failed to increase domestic production but had been surreptitiously increasing his imports of Indian saltpeter, for reasons of profitability, and substituting it for the domestic salt he had contracted to produce.2 The result was a decree of June 24, 1775, changing the saltpeter industry from private to gov ernment operation (de convertir ce bail en un Régie), and Turgot's letter of August 17, soliciting the help of the Academy both in unravel ing "this secret of nature" and in managing a prize competition for im provements in saltpeter production.

There had been at least two earlier such competitions, one in 1750, sponsored by the Academy of Sciences of Berlin, and another in 1766, sponsored by the Besançon Academy, "for the least-onerous method of making saltpeter in Franche-Comté." Although few of the contribu tions to either had been published, the literature on the subject was already large, and the Academy's prize committee began collecting it. The author of the Observations' ' "news" noted that the brief time al lowed for the preparation of the program had not allowed the collec tion of descriptions of artificial nitrate works (nitreires artificielles) used in foreign countries, with the exception of the 1747 instructions of the Swedish War College, which he summarizes in a footnote.

A prescription for the artificial production of saltpeter, by the direct imitation of nature, is found in one of the earliest European works to mention the substance, the Bellifortis of Conrad Kyeser (1404).3 * The extensive literature which followed is replete with variant prescriptions of the same kind, the principal variations being in the "mixture," which sometimes consisted solely of rotting animal and vegetable matter, and sometimes-especially under the influence of chemists who believed in the mutual transmutability of salts-included quantities of one kind of salt or another. Proposals also differed in the arrangements proposed. The "apparatus" was usually a pit dug in the earth, with or without a cover, but was sometimes an artificially constructed wall. Germany, which produced the largest literature on the subject, favored walls and the liberal use of salts in the mixture, and was thought by a committee of the French Academy in 1776 to have made "rapid progress" with artificial saltpeter.4 Such statistics as are available, however, indicate that French production was still far greater than that of any other Euro pean state.5 German production was very small, even from natural sources, much of its saltpeter being obtained by importation, especially from Poland and Russia.6 It was Sweden, however, which was con stantly cited, and not only by the French, as the country which had solved the saltpeter problem through artificial production. The Academy was thus on solid ground in believing, as our anony mous author goes on to say, that the matter is primarily a scientific problem. "The chemists have not established an entirely satisfactory theory of the principles of nitric acid [acide nitreux], on its true origin and the mode of its formation." There are three principal theories: first, that of "les anciens chymistes," who thought that "the air of the atmo sphere is the natal place and reservoir of nitric acid," and that it acts on calcerous earths and other alkalis. Against this is the fact that the same earths which abound in saltpeter near inhabited places do not produce any when in their "quarrys." Second, there is the theory of Stahl, which admits only one "primitive acid," vitriolic (sulphuric), which is "trans muted" into nitric acid "by its intimate union with the inflammable principle which separates from animal and vegetable substances." The author, noting that this theory was supported by the prizewinning paper entered into the competition sponsored by the Berlin Academy of Sci ences, admits that it is supported by "many chemical facts"; but he adds that it has not been sufficiently demonstrated by experiment. Final ly there is the theory published in 1717 by the younger (Louis) Lemery, according to which saltpeter is a vegetable product and passes into animals from that source. That ordinary analysis fails to show it in vegetables was attributed by Lemery to its being masked by other prin ciples or to its destruction by fire. In nature it is formed and separated by putrefaction. This, too, says the anonymous author in Observations, needs experimental verification.

He goes on with his personal observations. The theory of Stahl could easily be tested by setting materials susceptible to putrefaction in an environment similar to that in which saltpeter is known to be generated, adding, to one portion, acids, free or combined to various bases, and leaving a portion untreated for comparison. By 1777, the terminal date for the competition, thirty-eight papers had been received, but the competition was extended to 1781 (and sup plements permitted until 1782), as it had been decided that the time allowed had been inadequate. By the latter closing date twenty-eight more contributions had been received, and in 1786 the winning paper was published, along with seven others, and the remainder were sum marized.

Of the sixty-six papers, twenty-one were judged not to contain any thing new-one of them being described as a compilation from the Academy's own earlier collection!-three were incomplete, and one was rejected as being in another language than those permitted in the com petition (French and Latin). The remaining forty-one covered nearly all possibilities known to the lore of saltpeter. Several dealt with the conversion of "the universal acid" and the transmutation of common salt or "vitriolic earth." There were those who held saltpeter to origi nate in vegetables or in a mineral, including one who held it to be pre formed in mines. One held saltpeter to be a living organism.

Most of them dealt with the variety of arrangements for induced putrefaction, which the commissioners had in 1775 classified into vaults, walls, trenches, and beds (voutes, murailles, fosses, et couches ou fas). The conventional form, which was essentially that described by the Swedish War College in its 1747 instructions, consisted of a bed, or heap, of triangular cross section, placed under a protecting roof but otherwise open to the air. Only one contestant recommended a closely similar arrangement, and he neglected to specify the composition of the putrefying mixture. Several recommended leaving off the roof (hanger). Such processes generally required two to three years, but one of the papers published in these instructions claimed to be able to accomplish it in three days. Others felt raw material shortages to be at the root of the problem, and one of these recommended converting city garbage dumps to saltpeter beds, another the establishment of rabbit farms around the saltpeter works, and a third proposed a law requiring beer and wine merchants to conserve urine in vats. Several others proposed legislation, some of which the Commission described as worse than the fouille, but one who advocated that the obligation to supply crude salt peter (or saltpeterish earth) be levied as a direct tax, was proposing what I suspect to have been the real secret of the success of the Swedish program. Lavoisier had inserted an account of his own research "on the exis tence of air in nitric acid and on the means of decomposing and recom posing that acid" in the collection of 1776, and was to insert another, "on the decomposition of saltpeter by charcoal," in that of 1786. In the first he apologizes that the paper is perhaps more theoretical than prac tical and has only a tenuous connection with the methods known for making saltpeter, but adds that the commissioners did not think it use less to insert it in their collection. In 1786 the commissioners added the opinion that Lavoisier's 1776 paper had opened the eyes of the con testants to the operations of nature in the formation of nitric acid15without, however, citing any examples.

In that paper, which he had read to the Academy in March 1776, Lavoisier notes that in his Opusculum (1774) he had reported that "Kunkel's phosphorus" burned under a glass inverted over water, ab sorbs one-fifth of the air, which appears to become part of the resulting phosphoric acid. He thought this one-fifth to be a substance contained in the air, and says that he found the same phenomenon in the case of sulphur. He concluded that air is a constituent of acids, and proposed to study each one separately. Because of the current interest of the Academy in saltpeter, he begins with nitric acid, acknowledging Priest ley's priority, but justifying his own report on the ground that he and Priestley had reached "diametrically opposite conclusions."

His experiment consisted in the decomposition of nitric acid by a metal, using mercury, for reasons of convenience. Since the mercury could be regenerated after the experiment without a change in weight, he doubts the involvement of phlogiston, and concludes that the 126 inches of "air" evolved evidently came from the acid. Accepting Priest ley's proof, in that same experiment, that this "air" is not vaporized nitric acid, Lavoisier calls it "a better air than common air." it both over mercury and over water, in his pneumatic trough, he finds that in the latter case some of the gas condensed, while the water be came acidic. He concludes that nitric acid is composed of two "airs," nitrous air and pure air.16

Again in the 1786 collection Lavoisier inserted a paper, this time without apology for its questionable relevance. The reaction under study this time was the hoary experiment of the detonation of saltpeter by charcoal, which yields an alkali (potassium carbonate), and, as we know, the gases nitrogen and carbon dioxide. Lavoisier knew it, too, as had two others he cites, Cavallo and Berthollet. Neither of these, however, had made any measurements, and Lavoisier, in this paper, is prepared to rectify this. But he goes further, into the activity of nitro gen (mofette) and carbon dioxide (fixed air). He had shown in a paper of 1781 that the latter is 28 percent "charcoal" and 72 percent "oxygen." Henry Cavendish had shown in 1784 that, in sparking a mixture of oxy gen and nitrogen, "nearly all" is absorbed by lime water, forming a "nitre of terrestrial base" (calcium nitrate), thus indicating that the product was nitric acid. Cavendish also thought that some nitric acid was formed when oxygen and hydrogen were "sparked," but Lavoisier was unable to confirm this, and concludes that the acid consists of nitro gen and oxygen, in the proportions of 3 to 7.17

These researches had little more effect on Lavoisier the régisseur than they did on the contestants for the Academy prize. He had encountered one of the methods by which nitric acid (and saltpeter) was produced commercially a century later. And indeed, the other, and more success ful commercial process, the catalytic oxidation of ammonia, was pub lished three years later.18 It seems safe to suppose that 18th-century technology was not equal to the large-scale exploitation of either, and that Lavoisier sensed this in viewing his scientific papers as peripheral to the solution of the problem of saltpeter production. For he simulta neously pursued that problem along what seemed a more promising line.

This line was that of the artificial nitrate works, and was the same as that followed by most of the contestants for the prizes mentioned. ## The Role of the Scientist

One can see in the writings of French chemists, beginning with Lavoisier, a shedding of the Stahlian notions, which supported a belief in synthetic saltpeter, and their replacement by a correct analysis of the composition of nitric acid and of the several bases (potassium, calcium, magnesium) with which it combines. Indeed, the modern processes for the synthesis of nitric acid were known in Paris by about 1790, both the method of sparking a mixture of oxygen and nitrogen and the catalytic oxidation of ammonia having been discovered and published in England toward the end of the previous decade.33 * * * 37 But important as these discov eries were for science, they did not contribute for another century to the solution of the saltpeter problem.

Lavoisier made his principal effort a simultaneous attack on artificial production through the direct imitation of nature. His work, and that of his fellow academicians, went far to inhibit the free-wheeling empiri cism which had previously flourished among the practitioners of this art and which was so well exemplified in the papers received in connec tion with the Academy prize. Lavoisier quickly selected what he thought was the most effective of the processes in use, that of the Swedish War College, and in this it appears that he was correct. But how effective was even that process? Evidence is lacking that artificial saltpeter was produced in significant quantity anywhere, and it does not seem even to have been seriously attempted in France.
