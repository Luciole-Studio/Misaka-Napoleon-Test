


   It was one of the war’s peculiarities that desertion from Cristino ranks,
and subsequent banditry, continued at a high rate even in 1839 when
the Cristino side was in sight of victory.30 Some of this may be explained
by a growing pragmatic indulgence shown by a victorious army, which,
during the revolutions and defeats years earlier, had been prone to more
condign punishment of offenders. Other explanations lie in the pop-
ular anger at the way that Church properties nationalised under the
revolution were not being parcelled out to Cristino veterans, as had
been proposed by some ‘social Liberals’, but were being auctioned off to
local oligarchs to remedy disastrous state ﬁnances. This ‘missed chance’
of popular landownership was explained by Marxists as a failed ‘bour-
geois revolution’. New property owners, who were few, unlike during
the French Revolution, were too weak to advance their ‘historic mission’
of advancing capitalism.31 This ‘failure’ school was partially revised by
post-Cold War scholarship, which presented a more optimistic, but still
mixed, picture of the Liberal property revolution.32 In fact, the 1830s
property revolution was hybrid in nature. Whereas several new buyers
did become property owners for the ﬁrst time, they generally did not
drive radical capitalism but were ‘feudalised’ by the social and cultural
hegemony of the landed nobility, even though the legal separateness of
the nobility had been abolished for good. The undynamic rentier land-
lord with strong contacts with local authorities was the social category
that beneﬁted most.33
   Of course, this property revolution had already been underway in ﬁts
and starts since the 1790s, with Jovellanos’s free-market philosophy,
Godoy’s conﬁscation of a portion of Church property a decade later,
and that of the Liberals in the early 1820s.34 But the greatest nineteenth-
century programme of land nationalisation in Spain happened during
Spain’s greatest civil war, when half-a-million Spaniards were under
arms, and when an 1839 Cortes motion to reward Carlist War veterans
with nationalised land was rejected by Liberal deputies who had spent
six years exhorting ‘the people’ to accept sacriﬁces in the ﬁght against
Carlism.35 Even veterans lucky enough to afford nationalised property
found the auctions rigged in favour of vested interests, as intendants
designated the lands to be offered for auction with the assistance of
local ‘experts’ who were usually the agents of existing landowners. And
the intendants themselves were easily bribed to rig auctions in advance,
despite the theoretical risk of sanctions against such abuses.36 The struc-
tural grievances of Spanish landownership were at their plainest in the
large estates in the south and south-west, which were worked by landless
and (controversially) largely Galician migrant labour, and the wartime

property revolution energised these like never before. A progresista elec-
tion campaign in Córdova six months after the end of the war went
awry when a hired mob of veterans ended up targeting migrant work-

Galicians’, but to attack ‘the fat people’.37
   The consubstantiation of war and revolution provoked economic mil-
itancy across the Cristino ‘home front’. Both desertion and banditry
remained high towards the end of the war as virtual reserve armies of
bandits roamed the Cristino countryside. The generalised states of siege
in Andalucía from 1837 allowed Captain-Generals to make short work
of bandits, especially whenever there was the slightest hint that the
bandits might in fact be ‘Carlists’ as well as mere criminals. Thus, the
Captain-General of Granada in November 1838 was glad to make public
proclamations of the execution of 13 insurgents who had been preying
on rural communities.38 Even though civil authorities were emascu-
lated under states of siege in matters of crime and punishment, by the
following spring the Civil Governor of Granada had concluded that
economic grievances were motivating at least some of this wrongdo-
ing. He parcelled out fallow municipal lands to landless labourers, in a
desperate bid to end the violent lawlessness around Guadix caused by
draft-dodgers and deserters.39
   In Cristino cities Spain’s female tobacco workers gained a reputation
for militancy. Smoking was a key part of Spanish popular culture: a for-
eign traveller was surprised to discover that even women smoked, and
that the only smoke-free places were the Cortes and the judicial bench.40
Spain pioneered cigarettes, displacing snuff and requiring dainty ﬁngers
for production. This changed the sex of the workforce as women work-
ers (known as tabacaleras or cigarreras) vastly outnumbered men workers
by the time of the Carlist War. Tobacco had long been a state monopoly
produced in factories at Madrid, Cádiz, Valencia, Barcelona, La Coruña,
Alicante and – the biggest – in Seville; all of these were in Cristino hands,
and the ﬁnished product reached the front line bearing such images of
Liberal patriotism as Espartero and Princess Isabella. Tobacco for troops
ranked in importance alongside food and munitions, which turned its
production into a patriotic and political matter, even more than it had
already been before the war (when the Jacobin, Romero Alpuente, had
campaigned to remove tax from tobacco).41 The war inﬂected grievances
about conditions and management of the state monopoly. When José
