   The third reason was military. The Carlists had proved secure in their
northern heartland, and the Gómez expedition had shown their abil-
ity to destabilise the enemy’s rearguard. Thus, a three-pronged strategy
gave them the initiative: whereas the actual Royal Expedition marched
south-east, a smaller expedition struck south-west, and the bulk of the
Royal Army remained in the Basque country to harass Cristino garrisons
in a series of local offensives. Moreover, the Royal Government had
demanded the removal of as many Carlist forces as possible from the
Basque country, as the local economy was on the point of collapsing
under the ‘total war’ burdens of men, food and munitions.43 March 1837
had seen Navarrese battalions in Estella openly insubordinate for the
ﬁrst time, shouting ‘Death to the ojalateros and the junta! Long live the
people (paisanos)!’, as their pay arrears had made them desperate. They
disbelieved the King’s promise of redress.44 It remains unclear whether
the Expedition’s champions were exaggerating in their warning that the
Basque country could support only 15 more days of operations.45 But
rising interest from some Carlists in quack military solutions to their
strategic blockade (including a British adventurer’s offer to arm cor-
sairs with a prototype torpedo from the Woolwich arsenal) is highly
suggestive.46
   The fourth reason was moralistic in nature. The ‘re-evangelisation’
of revolutionary Spain was Don Carlos’s main strategy, even though,
in measuring his success in the number of Masses held in liberated vil-
lages, he invited criticism from contemporaries and historians.47 Church
ordinations and attendance had collapsed in the wake of two years
of Cristino anti-clericalism. The Carlist press trailed stories of aban-
doned parishes in the wake of the schism with Rome, the powerlessness
of the bishops, the expulsion or imprisonment of priests by Cristino
soldiers and militia, and, above all, the mendizabalista disentailment;
indeed, some 400 parishes closed due to lack of ministers during the
ﬁrst half of 1836 alone.48 Linked to this moralistic reason was the
real hope that Madrid would bloodlessly pronounce in favour of Don
Carlos as soon as his army reached the city walls. A foreign journalist
who had visited Madrid the previous year reckoned that some ‘three-
fourths of the respectable portion of its population are in favour of
Don Carlos’.49

honourable exile for the ‘widow of Ferdinand VII’ and a Carlist takeover
humanised by an agreement neither to resurrect the Inquisition nor to
oppose ‘sensible reforms’. These terms were agreed in a private letter
from the Queen-Regent to her brother (Ferdinand II of Naples), which
the unofﬁcial Neapolitan representative carried with him when the rev-

appearance of the Carlist Royal Army and its welcome into the capital
by a grateful Queen-Regent. Carlist peace terms had certainly improved
since the previous year, when Don Carlos turned away a Cristino delega-
tion by demanding the dismantling of the Quadruple Alliance, refusing
to recognise the constitutional regime in Portugal and tactlessly suggest-
ing that all Spain offer Masses for the Virgin as Generalísima of the Carlist
forces. Delegates Roncali and Zambrano were aristocrats and right-wing
moderados, enemies of the Calatrava revolution, but Don Carlos saw
in these men not two potential counter-revolutionaries (as they would
indeed become in 1844), but two Liberals.51 But even though pro-Carlist
historians sympathised with Don Carlos’s integrity in 1836, they also
criticised the dynastic peace option of 1837 for making the entire Expe-
dition a hostage to the whims of the Queen-Regent, and for distorting
the campaign into an eschatological carnival of religious piety rather
than a ruthless assault on the capital.52
   In retrospect, Don Carlos’s campaign of re-evangelisation is not as
supine as it may seem. Both the Catholic resistance against revolution-
aries during the Vendée during the 1790s and during the twentieth-
century Mexican Cristiada showed a link between religious fervour
and popular resistance, and Don Carlos had been encouraged by the
Church’s policy of excommunicating expropriators and purchasers of
disentailed ecclesiastical property. But the King’s thinking created two
ﬂaws, one of strategy and another of perception. As the expedition was
supposed to be a government-in-waiting, its mobility was burdened by
excessive numbers of non-combatants and shoestring logistics, which
impeded the Carlists’ ability to transport and deploy sufﬁcient artillery
and alienated ‘liberated’ communities more than necessary by forcing
the ‘liberators’ to live off the land.53 The second ﬂaw was far more
profound. Don Carlos believed that the enemy’s will to ﬁght was still
coerced by the ‘usurping’ elites of 1833, and that the war remained
a dynastic dispute. In fact, such had been the wave of revolutionary
change since the start of the civil war that the Bourbon family troubles
had become irrelevant, or at the very least their solution could no longer
