Their contention that Spain remained royalist, or apolitical, throughout

As the best neo-traditionalist scholar of the First Carlist War, Alfonso

   Even though both the traditionalists and neo-traditionalists sympa-
thised with historical Carlism, they were alienated by the leftward shift
of ‘Carlism 1968’, which inspired a new generation of neo-Carlist schol-
arship. The neo-Carlists are the fourth category, and their revisionism
is impossible to understand without reference to the socialist ‘self-
management’ (autogestión) doctrine advocated from the late 1960s by
the Carlist ‘Prince’ Carlos Hugo de Borbón Parma and his followers. The
neo-Carlists resented how the Francoist ‘families’ of neo-traditionalists
and National Catholics had hijacked their movement since 1937 in
the name of the ‘Movement’, and the neo-Carlist movement accord-
ingly faced repression under the last years of the Franco dictatorship.20
Their most strident scholar was Josep Carles Clemente. Clemente argued
that early Carlism was an inchoate social protest against nineteenth-
century oligarchs who were using the Liberal property revolution to
marginalise villagers’ livelihoods and erode the communitarian model
enshrined in the Basque fueros (devolved economic and political pow-
ers under the Spanish crown)21 Carlism predominated in the Basque
country, he explained, because since the eighteenth century landhold-
ing there had become concentrated in fewer, more exploitative, hands,
causing rents to rise whilst agricultural prices remained depressed, and
to varying degrees this class conﬂict pertained elsewhere in Spain.22
Clemente thus turned Marxist social history against its ‘correct’ con-
stituency, representing Carlism as a ‘social dissidence’ springing from
a form of ‘feudal socialism’ under attack from the centralising and
capitalist drive of Spain’s modernising Liberals;23 a neo-Carlist interpre-
tation that was expanded upon by other scholars equally exasperated
by Spain’s oligarchical liberalism.24 But Clemente’s ire targeted not the
Marxists but the traditionalists who had hijacked the true historical
essence of Carlism, or as he complained in the 1977 publication Nosotros
los carlistas: ‘ﬁrst came the volunteers to raise their standard of King and

Fueros, and later came the traditionalists with an agenda of their own,

   Clemente’s neo-Carlism did not hold the ﬁeld for long, however:
in the early 1990s neo-traditionalism counter-attacked with Alfonso
Bullón de Mendoza’s doctoral thesis, quickly turned into a published
monograph. Bullón de Mendoza found Clemente’s neo-Carlism, along
with conventional Marxist analyses, to be ‘wilful history’ (historia voli-
tiva), left-wing propagandistic distortions foundering upon their lack of
‘objective facts’. Thus, there was not a single coherent Carlist manifesto
offering social justice, and episodes of villagers and combatants assert-
ing their ‘moral economy’ came as much from Liberal as Carlist quarters
(i.e. the side was irrelevant). Meanwhile, the Basque fueros on which
Clemente based part of his argument, were no pre-capitalist model of
social harmony but simply a different model of social exploitation.26
Bullón de Mendoza’s thesis provoked a furious debate with the neo-
Carlists, which was happily sustained more by evidence rather than
assertion. We may add to this by considering how Carlist War newspa-
per extracts qualify Clemente’s ‘social dissidence’ thesis. On one level,
Clemente’s thesis does indeed reﬂect the fears of 1830s Cristino elites
who saw Carlists as reactionary levellers bent on smashing the progress
and hierarchy of respectable society, or, as a moderado newspaper in 1837
put it: ‘Carlism could never be attractive to people who have something
to lose because it is in fact a kind of pure democracy, demagoguery, and
anarchy.’27 This would support the ‘social dissidence’ thesis, until we
ﬁnd that the same Cristino elites also despaired of the excesses made by
the ‘democratic rabble’ on their own side.28 The inference must be that
the First Carlist War should be studied not from the perspective of elite
discourse and ideology but from the perspective of contingency. This
book aims to take this perscpective, using a chronological organisation
to enable the simultaneous analysis of concurrent developments which
shaped the background and the course of the conﬂict. This approach, it
is hoped, will avoid the constrictive, jargon-prone methodologies of so
much of the ideological scholarship.
   A narrative study can make an original contribution for two reasons.

from the ideological hegemony of Marxism on the one hand and the
rebarbative legacy of Francoism on the other. Much of this feat may be
