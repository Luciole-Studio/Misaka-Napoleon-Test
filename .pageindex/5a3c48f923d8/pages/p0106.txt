


   The broadest and deepest networks of popular radicalism had evolved
in Liberal port-cities, in particular, Barcelona, Málaga and Cádiz.13 Meer’s
crackdown on radicalism in Barcelona was mirrored elsewhere on the
Cristino home front in accordance with the moderado election victory
of 1837, by disarming and reforming the militia of Barcelona, Reus
and Tarragona.14 Meer’s successful counter-insurgency against Segarra’s
forces, including his capture of Solsona on 27 July 1838, earned him
political capital to suppress the Radicals.15 The Radical press cast the
moderado Catalan as an honorary member of the ‘Angels of Andalucía’,
the satirical title given to dictatorial captain-generals who imposed mar-
tial law in provincial capitals. Meer imposed a 10,000-reales bond on
all people in Barcelona who could not satisfy the authorities of the
reasons for their stay. The city was full of secret societies and mainly
French expatriate Radicals.16 But the crackdowns by Meer, and other
moderado captain-generals were limited in their effectiveness, for the
progresista press campaign of the late 1830s made heroes out of those
Radicals imprisoned and deported to the garrison colonies in Spanish
North Africa.17
   In Málaga the moderados imposed a state of siege in response to local
authorities’ refusal to cooperate in the judicial investigation into Don-
adío’s and SanJust’s assassinations. Málaga occupied a strategically vital
position: it triangulated Cristino control over the only enduring Carlist
insurgency in Andalucía, the Serranía de Ronda to the west, and its role
as a port grew in importance from 1838 as a series of Carlist prison-
ers’ insurrections in the North African garrison colonies made the city
the staging point for punitive expeditions. Certainly, its importance was
appreciated by allied forces: in October 1836 the commander of a Royal
Navy ship stationed in the port threatened to suspend all British aid
deliveries unless the ‘revolutionary chaos’ came to a halt.18 The risings
of the Militia in 1835 and 1836, as we have seen, started here (whilst
Málaga would either be the ﬁrst, or one of the ﬁrst, provinces to spawn
future revolutions in 1840, 1843, 1854 and 1868).19 Between 1812 and
1835, the name of Málaga’s main square alternated between ‘Royal’ and
‘Constitution’ no fewer than six times. By 1835, the town decided to
save money on stonemasonry by leaving two plaques bearing the alter-
nate names at opposite corners of the square, in the hope that either
might serve the political demands of the day. During the 1836 rev-
olution, however, revolutionaries destroyed the ‘Royal’ plaque and it
was never reinstated.20 Málaga’s radical liberalism can be explained by
several factors. Its modest iron-and-steel industry expanded through-
out the 1830s, to make the city second only to Barcelona in industrial

importance by 1850, yet with classically Liberal free-trade elites instead
of Catalonia’s protectionists. Málaga, during the Carlist War, was the
only Cristino port to register a positive trade balance with the out-
side world, even though this success was tempered by a deﬁcit in
Spanish cabotage and by rampant smuggling, which was afforded local
protection.21 If statistics could have computed the activity of the so-
called mochileros (‘backpackers’), the smugglers who moved contraband
from Gibraltar via the lawless Serranía de Ronda, then Málaga would
have conformed more closely to the Spanish trade deﬁcit.22
   It is hard to explain much of Málaga’s radicalism because its exas-
perated governors did not risk committing their intelligence to paper.
In March 1838 Captain-General Palarea wrote to Madrid saying that he
was successfully penetrating the ‘juntas and afﬁliations of Andalucía’
but the delicate nature of this task obliged him to keep most details
secret.23 That said, a great part of the radicalism was certainly an expres-
sion of the elaborate local economic interests arising from support for
free trade. In 1837 the Secretary of the Treasury professed his disgust
at the fact that only 5 per cent of the contribution owed by Málaga
to help fund the costs of the central government had so far been paid,
an unpatriotic meanness all the more outrageous because ‘[Málaga] has
swamped the nation with illicit goods which have considerably reduced
tax receipts’.24 Whenever carabineers tried to impound contraband, they
were met with all kinds of resistance. In October 1839, vendors went on
strike in protest at a violent anti-contraband operation in one of the
main shopping streets, which forced the head of carabineers to resign.25
Ofﬁcial protection for contraband was matched by that for political
extremism. The moderado state of siege here was the least effective of
all the provincial capitals. The fact that the Militia used the occasion of
the Queen-Regent’s birthday in 1839 to hold political meetings and to
publish satires which ‘scandalously abused the precious right of the free
press’ suggests that the citizens’ force was not cowed as in other Cristino
cities.26 Military governors frequently resigned, sometimes in response
to death threats,27 and always under the relentless pressure of progresista
newspapers which used Málaga as proof that the moderados were more
interested in persecuting their political enemies hundreds of miles from
the front lines than in vanquishing the mortal enemy of liberalism. The
shabby treatment of the widows of two murdered Radicals underlined
this charge.28 By October 1838, the ‘Widows of Comares’ campaign had
borne fruit as controversy forced the Military Governor of Málaga to end
the transportations of Radicals to African garrison prisons and to return
to liberty all those surviving prisoners.29
