   Unlike the brigadistas of the Spanish Civil War, the 1830s auxiliaries
failed to disabuse the enemy of this impression of their mediocrity. The
best auxiliaries were Peninsular War veterans like the Irishman Lieu-
tenant Edward Costello, a brilliant recruiter whose return in 1835 to
Vitoria, scene of a bloody battle a generation earlier, evoked poignant
memories bordering on post-traumatic stress for him and his fellow
handful of veterans.93 But most British auxiliaries were new to ﬁght-
ing. The French and Portuguese auxiliaries, by contrast, were more
seasoned, but political divisions in Paris and Lisbon impeded their full
deployment. The British auxiliaries, which also suffered from equivocal
political support, were slow to acclimatise to northern Spain and spent
the ﬁrst months constructing fortiﬁcations.94 They were decimated in
body by typhus (‘Vitoria fever’) during the 1835–36 winter and spring,
and in soul by the virulent attacks of the Tory press, and their tragic
neglect upon their return to Britain after 1837.95 Chronic arrears of pay
persisted even after the war, when British Legion veterans protested
outside the Spanish legation in London, in a series of stand-offs last-
ing until 1843.96 Even when most arrears were ﬁnally settled, several
Spanish veterans lapsed into pauperdom which caught the eye of social
reformers. Henry Mayhew, in his seminal London Labour and the London
Poor, labelled the ‘Spanish Legion men’ as ‘those who will not work’ and
categorised them under ‘Professional Beggars’.97 Ironically, Mayhew’s
sententiousness was anticipated by the Carlists, who reserved particu-
lar scorn for the British auxiliaries as ‘pirates’ and Protestants, recruited
from the taverns and pie-shops of London.98
   This distasteful image has overshadowed the history of the for-
eign auxiliaries. Certainly, many men joined for the basest motives

of adventure or desperation. As so few auxiliary rank and ﬁle left
memoirs, we must privilege those that have been discovered, such as
that of William Turner, a poverty-stricken volunteer who, after two
years’ service in Spain, returned to England wounded and penniless,
and was later imprisoned for committing acts of fraud.99 Most other
humble biographies are extremely fragmented and yet suggest that
many of the authors’ motives and outcomes were similar.100 Yet two
qualiﬁcations must be made to their mercenary image. Recruits came
mainly from expanding British and Irish cities, and, in the case of
the Glasgow, a particularly strong cohort were weavers proletarianised
by industrialisation.101 It was no small irony that Scottish handloom
weavers and other victims of industrialisation entered Spain alongside
record British cotton imports. The economic grievances of recruits were
championed by their Radical ofﬁcers such as Alexander Somerville, an
Anti-Corn-Law League member who defended his men from Tory-Carlist
insults. Thus, the British auxiliaries were no more scum than the British
army proper which, under Wellington during the Peninsular War, had
defended the old order of ‘physical and intellectual slavery’ whereas
volunteers for Liberal Spain upheld the ‘good of mankind’, an opin-
ion shared by the great utilitarian and pioneering advocate of foreign
intervention in Spain’s civil war, John Stuart Mill.102 This Liberal inter-
nationalism was also held by other prominent ofﬁcers, such as the
journalist, Charles Southwell, who preferred the ‘pseudo-liberalism of
Queen Isabella to the monkish absolutism of Don Carlos’.103 Above all,
the Commander-in-Chief of the ﬁrst British Legion and Radical Mem-
ber of Parliament for the Westminster constituency, General George de
Lacy Evans, linked the cause of British and Spanish radicalism. Evans
was accused by both his Tory opposition at home and the Carlist
enemy abroad of vainglory, greed, and of possessing a self-publicising
Bonaparte complex.104
   In short, European Liberals saw the struggle against Carlism in inter-
national rather than merely Spanish terms. The most inﬂuential auxil-
iary in this regard was Joseph Tánski, a Polish refugee from the Russian
invasion of his homeland in 1831 and an ofﬁcer in the French For-
eign Legion. He left a remarkable account of the political and military
state of the Cristino war effort and a nuanced critique of the Con-
stitution of 1812 beloved of his fellow Spanish Radicals.105 The most
seasoned Liberal internationalists were in the Belgian Auxiliary Legion,
which comprised veterans of the Belgian Revolution and Portuguese
civil war. Portugese civil war veterans used their battle honours to call
