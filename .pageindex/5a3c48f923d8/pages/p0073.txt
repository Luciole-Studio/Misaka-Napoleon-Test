   Meanwhile, serious cracks were appearing in the Quadruple Alliance
itself. Portugal withdrew its legion, citing domestic threats of its own.
France, resentful of Britain’s forward role in the revolution, impressed by
the resignation of the Cristino ambassador to Paris (a move which the
ambassador to Brussels followed)47 and, above all, paralysed by domes-
tic political divisions on Spanish affairs, gave as little support as possible
whilst remaining within the alliance. The French legitimists (dubbed
‘Carlists’, even in France) wanted to support Don Carlos, the govern-
ing party of ‘resistance’ (Guizot, Broglie, Molé) wanted strict neutrality,
and the party of ‘movement’ comprising Republicans and centre-Left
Liberals wanted formal intervention on the side of Spanish liberty.48
Meanwhile, even London’s support could not be taken for granted as
power swayed between the pro-interventionist Palmerston and cautious
Peel. The revolutionary regime thus undertook radical diplomatic mea-
sures. Prime Minister Calatrava concluded a policy, begun in 1835, of

Cristino recognition of the Latin American republics on the American
mainland. Mexico, whose independence had been the bitterest pill for
Spain to swallow and the biggest threat to her remaining possessions
in the Caribbean and Paciﬁc, was recognised in a Treaty of Peace and
Friendship on 28 December 1836, which was ratiﬁed in turn by Mexico
in May 1837, and soon led to an exchange of ministers and commercial
relations.49 Moreover, both the Jackson and Van Buren administrations
of the USA, the rising hemisphere power, supported the Cristino side
for ideological reasons and as a guarantee of continued good diplo-
matic and commercial relations, despite the often pessimistic reports
of US missions to Madrid regarding anarchy and militarisation.50 Yet
this diplomatic sea change in its favour was not immediately appar-
ent to Cristino Spain. Madrid was rightly tormented by the threat that
such remaining Spanish possessions as Cuba might be invaded (Mexico’s
General Santa Anna and President Victoria had recently aborted an inva-
sion of this island), compromising the war effort, or even given up to
Carlist insurrection with outside help. Thus, the peace treaty stipulated
that Mexico would prohibit expeditions being launched from its ports
against Spanish territories and refrain from encouraging agitation for
the liberation of the same.51
   Cristino Spain in 1836 was more worried about Carlist than Bolivarian
subversion of its colonial and ex-colonial relations. During 1836, Don
Carlos’s agents in Rome focused on trying to frustrate the Vatican’s
impending recognition of the former New Spain and putative jewel
in the Pretender’s empire.52 But various schemes also ﬂourished in the
extant colonies, including a bid to raise Cuba for Don Carlos once
Gómez Expedition had been launched in Spain, on another occasion
when Bilbao was under its second siege, and on a third occasion when
the Royal Expedition crossed the Ebro. Conditions were ripe for sub-
version in this colony. Captain-General Tacón, a sexagenarian veteran
of the Spanish American Independence wars and high-handed architect
of administrative and police reforms, countermanded orders from the
revolutionary Cortes in 1836 to hold elections on the island under the
Constitution of 1812. But whilst Tacón’s order held sway in Havana,
Cuba’s second city, Santiago, inspired both by the possibilities of the
Constitution of 1812 and by resentment at the capital’s economic domi-
nance, staged a ‘Liberal’ revolt, which saw the underdeveloped east hold
out for three months against Tacón’s military rule. Tacón’s paciﬁcation
brought him no respite, as news reached him of a mysterious ‘English
agent’ who was en route to the island in a bid to persuade its oligarchs
