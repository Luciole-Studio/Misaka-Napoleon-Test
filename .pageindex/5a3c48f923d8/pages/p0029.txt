conditions in the Basque country.
   But even here the rising had only mixed success. Navarra, where
Carlism was strongest, was actually the slowest to rise, ironically because
popular royalism had always been so dominant here that the local
Royalist Volunteers were barely organised to begin with. The Navarrese
veteran of the Peninsular and Royalist Wars, Santos Ladrón de Cegama,
had ﬁrst to raise Carlists in nearby Logroño before striking north. His
capture and execution on 11 October backﬁred on the Cristinos, as hun-
dreds of Navarrese peasants mobilised to avenge this popular royalist.
Meanwhile, the Deputation of Navarra was caught between the Carlist
sympathies of its members and countrymen and the reality of Cristino
military control.5 It prevaricated by refusing to swear allegiance to the
Cristino monarchy until María Cristina had ﬁrst sworn to observe the
fueros, as was indeed constitutionally proper, but which, more impor-
tantly, reﬂected Realpolitik.6 In the rest of the Basque country, the
greater preparedness of the Volunteers gave local Carlists little difﬁ-
culty in mobilising armed forces, most dramatically in Vizcaya, where
the Royalist Volunteers answered the calls of Carlist insurrectionists and
invaded Bilbao, levying punitive taxes on known Liberals. Six weeks’
‘curfew for the rich’ ensued until Cristino troops reoccupied the city on
25 November, after which Bilbao would become a symbol of the Cristino
and Liberal cause, resisting repeated sieges.7 But at the time, this future
was far from certain, and Bilbao’s civil authorities had accordingly tried
to maintain as ‘neutral’ a posture as possible during the Carlist occupa-
tion, keeping few and ‘antiseptic’ minutes of meetings, whilst prudently
funding the Volunteers’ wages at the same time and even arranging
for the chamber of commerce to ‘lend’ the Carlists a cheap loan of
