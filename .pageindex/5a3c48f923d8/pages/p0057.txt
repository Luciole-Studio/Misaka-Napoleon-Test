Toreno administration had expelled the Jesuit order for the second
time in Spanish history, conﬁscating its assets to reduce the balloon-

government onslaught on corporate Catholicism which would be scaled
back, but not halted, only when the moderados returned to power in
1837. Mendizábal expanded this anti-clericalism by suppressing all reli-
gious houses containing fewer than 12 inmates and auctioning their
assets. Citizens who had bought Church property during the Triennium,
only to see it conﬁscated in the 1823 counter-revolution, were reunited
with their purchases courtesy of a July 1837 decree.124 The tithe, along
with an extra clerical tax levied in Galicia called the Voto de Santiago,
had long been unpopular, but their abolition became politically impossi-
ble to resist as Carlism spread during 1835.125 These were both abolished
and replaced from 1837 by a smaller tax (culto y clero) collected by the
constitutional municipalities in order to pension off monks and nuns
evicted by the disentailment.126
   Mendizábal did not expect formal French or British intervention and
so pledged to use nationalised property to fund a decisive campaign in
the Basque country.127 The degree to which he channelled revolutionary
anti-Carlism was remarkable. His Liberal union saw an initial ‘patriotic’
ﬂurry of donations to the Cristino war effort: ministers volunteered cuts
to their salaries, ladies’ charity circles made clothing for the front and
Liberal students answered the call to enlist.128 Despite the paternalis-
tic opposition of the moderados, enlistment to the militia was expanded
by removing the requirement that unmarried recruits obtain permis-
sion from their heads of family.129 A freer intellectual environment grew
in proportion to the mendizabalista assault on corporate Catholicism.
Barcelona’s medical academy ruled that suicide victims died ‘insane’ and

thus qualiﬁed for Christian burial. The British Protestant missionary and
know-it-all, George Borrow, set up a Bible Society in the same city.130 The
ﬁrst athenaeum came to Madrid and the Radical government histori-
cised its claim to rule by starting to build a pantheon to the fallen masses
of the Second of May.131 Some Liberals even proposed that conﬁscated
Church property should be leased to the landless, thereby inoculating
the rural masses against Carlism. Flórez Estrada, leader of the dissolved
anti-Carlist junta of Santander in 1834, serialised his ‘social liberal’ trea-
tise, La cuestión social, o sea, origen, latitud y efectos del derecho de propiedad,
which culminated in February 1836, with his proposal to grant landless
labourers smallholdings under 50-year renewable leases.132 But the Lib-
eral union waxed sectarian over the winter of 1835–36, as the exaltado
Militia exercised a double regime targeting moderados as well as Carlists.
Tellingly, in February 1836 the leftist Eco congratulated the Militia for
increasing and popularising its enlistment as a ‘dyke against the machi-
nations of the perverse’.133 By the summer, the march of events would
create a revolutionary dictatorship in the Cristino zone.
   Mendizábal’s most important decree was to conscript 100,000 men
for Córdova’s Army of the North. Three separate rounds of conscrip-
tion, each for 25,000 men, had been enacted since the start of the war,
but Mendizábal’s measure augured the mass militarisation of society.
The 100,000 quinta made all males aged between 18 and 40 liable to
serve, the only free exemptions being disability, unigeniture, ordina-
tion and veteran status. Alternatively, draftees could pay 4,000 reales,
or 1,000 reales and a viable horse, in order to avoid service.134 The net
‘blood tax’ collected by the government was some 25 million reales and
700 horses; this income was in the spirit of Mendizábal’s demand for
‘abundant men and money to end the war soon’.135 But the sums col-
lected made little impression on an annual state deﬁcit of 30 million
reales, which was made acute both by the lapsed customs revenues dur-
ing the revolution (some £500,000 worth of British imports had entered
Spain that summer without suffering any duties) and the expanding
army budget, which had reached some 600 million reales annually.136
Only Mendizábal’s long-standing contacts with the Paris and London
bourses, combined with the structural reforms of the desamortización,
kept the Cristino state liquid, ensuring terms of credit which no other
Spanish prime minister could have achieved.137
