   The march of events at local levels escaped Mendizábal’s attempts to
manage anti-clericalism from the centre. Part of the anti-clerical atmo-
sphere in Cristino Spain may be attributed to episodes of anti-Carlist
hysteria returning with a renewed outbreak of the cholera in July 1835.
The rest was caused by the very real attempts of individual clerics to
ﬁght the Liberal revolution.139 The Cristino authorities sent suspect
monks and priests, especially in the Maestrazgo, into internal exile in
Andalucía, and from November 1835 all new entrants into religious
orders required a certiﬁcate of loyalty to Isabel II signed by the civil
governor.140 The need for deportations was clear after the 10 August
1835 Carlist rising of evicted Dominican friars in Manacor (Mallorca)
who, the Radical press alleged, ‘burnt alive’ militiamen they captured,
and whose ‘lenient’ sentences provoked a revolutionary rising by some
1,400 Militia in Palma demanding ‘a sound example be made of the
guilty to guard against future risings’.141 Other religious were terrorised
by the massacres into ﬂeeing into Carlist zones, where nuns nursed
war casualties, or even leaving into Spain altogether: several were wel-
comed to Buenos Aires where they occupied vacancies in River Plate
monasteries.142 Clerics who remained under Cristino control faced a
bleak future of eviction from disentailed monasteries and subjection to
laic authorities in the meantime, either constitutional or ‘double regime’
in nature. In the former case, the Civil Governor of Pontevedra forced
local nuns to observe night curfews and provide charity relief to war
widows, remarking impudently that the sisters should be content to do

God’s true work.143 Elsewhere in Galicia (Ribadeo) the double regime
targeted Carlist clerics as militiamen forced civil authorities to close
local monasteries and expel their inmates. Their vigilantism was sup-
ported in the Radical press, which published their complaints of how
the clerical guerrilla captains they had captured had faced no greater
punishment than deportation to their home parishes, or imprisonment
in Vigo castle from which, in one instance, a cleric escaped with ofﬁ-
cial collusion and rejoined the facción.144 The economic power wielded
by the Church in Galicia over rural leaseholders was shrinking the
Cristino safe zone into the port-cities and fortiﬁed centres. Illicit arms
production for the Carlists was widespread, and was concealed from
passing militia patrols.145 When Latre was dispatched to La Coruña to
replace Morillo as Captain-General of Galicia, both men undertook their
journeys under false names and even physical disguises, Latre being con-
vinced that his execution would follow being identiﬁed by any priest in
his path.146
  The growing Carlism of clerics prevented the Cristino authorities
from relying on them as natural guardians of the social order, which
was always the aim of the Liberals, who wanted only to strip the
Church of its political rather than moral power. But the refusal of
many clerics to accept Cristino political legitimacy drove the double
regime to attack their moral authority. In March 1835, the Mayor of
Vera (Almería) prohibited any contact between women and monks of
the nearby monastery at Beruela, citing the scandals the monks had
been causing amongst local women.147 Radicalised Cristino soldiers and
militiamen targeted Church property and symbols. In April 1835, a
Cristino patrol sent out from Pamplona robbed and vandalised a church
at nearby Yanci.148 Cristino army elites worried about the violent and
socially subversive radicalism that led people to invade abandoned and
conﬁscated Church property.149 In a nationalised Dominican convent
in Barcelona several nuns’ tombs were opened and the remains disre-
spectfully scattered.150 During July 1835, mobile militia near Belchite
rebelled against the government when a popular lieutenant (a certain
Blas Pober) was arrested and a Radical demagogue (a certain Urbina) was
found dead in suspicious circumstances. In response the militia refused
to march out against rural Carlists, and sacked a monastery in the name
of the Constitution of 1812.151 In October 1835, mobile militia used
Church icons along the Imperial Canal linking Aragón with Navarra for
target practice, assaulting the chapel on the Marquess Lazán’s estate,
ﬁring their muskets at the altar of the Virgin of Pilar inside, despite the
fact that the Virgin during the Peninsular War had been ‘nationalised’ as
the celestial Captain-General of Patriot Spain.152 In Madrid, authorities
