                 Still the ﬁgures of British trade with Brazil are in part deceptive, because
           they represent the amounts declared at the departure from Britain and do not
           correspond to actual sales. British merchants grossly overestimated the South
           American markets. They dispatched their agents to the major ports, where
           they were willing to pay high rents for warehouses, pushing away the local
           merchants.37 In 1806 –1807 they endured heavy losses in their exports to Rio
           de la Plata, and in the following year the same happened in Brazil.38 A part of
           the goods sent overseas was never paid for and the merchant houses active in
           the trade of Buenos Aires and Rio de Janeiro had to resort to auction sales to
           dispose of their merchandise, which they often sold at a loss.39 The overesti-
           mation of the capacity of the Brazilian market also indicates that the British
           business community had not gained access to the relevant information that
           could allow it to accurately anticipate the capacity of the market, and that for-
           mer clandestine connections were not so frequent or extensive as to provide
           that kind information.
                 Furthermore, the new associations could not immediately displace the
           strong ties conjoining merchants established in Portugal and Brazil. Traders in
           Rio de Janeiro and Salvador were no longer simple commissioners or factors,
           working for merchants in Lisbon and unable to conduct business on their own
           account, as the Viceroy, Marquis of Lavradio, still depicted them in 1779.40
