   The pressure on Cabrera to agree to a more humanitarian conduct of
the conﬂict grew unbearable once Don Carlos added his name to the
criticism. The King’s censure was wide-ranging, and included criticism
of Cabrera’s zeal in suppressing even the most moderate Liberal tracts
which made it into Carlist territory. Don Carlos did not think it wrong
to censor, only that this was the proper duty of the civilian junta, not
the military. But Don Carlos’s most pressing critique was on humani-
tarian grounds. Finally, early in April, both Cabrera and Van-Halen put
their names to a version of the Eliot Treaty in the eastern zone, Cabrera
signing at his headquarters at Segura on 1 April 1839, whereupon the
emissary took it for Van-Halen’s signature at Lécera on 3 April 1839.
Cabrera had initially been piqued that such an important negotiation
had ﬁrst been opened up only verbally when Van-Halen had despatched
to him a Carlist prisoner as an emissary. The Treaty of Lécera (known by
Carlists as the Treaty of Segura) also excited controversy besides, by civil-
ian politicians and journalists. George Villiers defended Van-Halen’s and
British government policy in this matter from an attack in the House
of Lords by Lord Londonderry on 23 July 1839.130 And the interna-
tional controversy paled in comparison to that raging in the press of
Cristino Spain. In particular, the treaty’s provisions to protect the lives
of captured Carlists who had previously served in Cristino ranks, the
insistence that both able-bodied and wounded prisoners be given treat-
ment equal to those of serving soldiers, and the ban placed on deporting
prisoners overseas all excited the ire of the Liberal press, which pointed
out that these points privileged the Carlists more than the Cristinos.131
Moreover, problems remained as civilians would still be subject to the
rigours and excesses of armies, even though local communities now felt
encouraged to strike bargains with local Carlist forces in order to be able
to work their lands unmolested (deals which local Cristino forces were
obliged to tolerate out of sheer necessity). Also, local commanders were
still prone to ignoring or distorting the provisions. Prisoners numbering
more than 300, for example, were to be left in open towns devoid of
all war-related material and to be quarantined up to a distance of two
leagues’ radius from any troops. The Carlist deputy comandante general
of Aragón interpreted this provision as excluding everyone on pain of
death.132
