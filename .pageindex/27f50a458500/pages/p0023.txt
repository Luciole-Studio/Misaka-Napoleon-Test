Writers of Basque culture have stressed the provinces’ historical pre-
eminence (the three lordships – señoríos – and the Kingdom of Navarra
matched other Spanish kingdoms in longevity). ‘False’ religions had
never penetrated the Basque country, and during the sixteenth cen-
tury the kingdom was rewarded accordingly with the grant of noble
status to all indigenous Basques, which meant that nobility became far
less signiﬁcant as a factor in social differentiation than in the rest of
Spain. This ancient history gave the Basques the conﬁdence to nego-
tiate their Spanishness on their own terms, via their fueros, language,
Catholic piety and the importance of the self-contained homestead – or
caserío (which was handed down in its entirety to a single male heir) –
in family and social relations.48
   The Basque fueros have played a major role in debates about early
Carlism. Marxist historians objectiﬁed the fueros and the local elites
they empowered as a doomed defence of pre-capitalist socio-economic
norms. The popular Catholicism which distinguished pre-capitalist
Basque society, and which was the cause and motive for popular
Carlism, was merged by beneﬁciaries of the fueros into the self-interested
war cry of ‘religion and fueros’, tying their debatable merits to the unim-
peachable merits of religion.49 But it would be superﬁcial to explain
Basque Carlism as a war in defence of the fueros. Whereas Carlist pro-
paganda defended the fueros, it did so in the defensive tone of the
‘ancient constitution’ violated by the pre-war police state (which in
the Basque country intensiﬁed its activities after 1830 whilst relenting
elsewhere in Spain), and by the martial law of Cristino ‘pashas’ once
the war had started.50 In fact early Basque Carlists did not place foral
interests foremost. The Carlists’ second-in-command claimed that the
Basque insurgents of 1833 saw the fueros as secondary to the legitimate
royal succession, and his subaltern, Wilhelm von Rahden, whose for-
eignness made him disinterested, agreed.51 Even though the October
1833 Carlist insurgency in Alava mentioned the fueros, its leader, Valen-
tín Verástegui, had a vested family interest. But the proclamations of
the insurgent king, Don Carlos, were slower to invoke them: not until
September 1834 did he make an explicitly foralist proclamation.52 The
Pretender’s foralism was not proactive but purely in reaction to the fact
that the rest of Spain (which had long been stripped of its fueros) had
failed to rally to his cause. He was forced to rely on the fueros because
