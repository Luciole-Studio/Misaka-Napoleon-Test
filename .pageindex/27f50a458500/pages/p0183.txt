   In July 1837, Zaratiegui launched an expedition of some 280 cavalry
and ﬁve infantry battalions imbued with the same religious liberation
ethos as the Royal Expedition. After defeating Das Antas’s Portuguese
on the Ebro, Zaratiegui pushed into Old Castile towards Madrid.73 Most
of the defenders, including the teenage cadets from the military col-
lege, ﬂed to Madrid, even though several college professors offered their
services to the occupying Carlists. News in the capital of Zaratiegui’s
approach caused panic, and a state of siege was imposed on 6 August,
decreeing a wide range of pro-Carlist activities and opinions to be pun-
ishable by martial law. The capital’s Militia came out in a show of force
and was supplemented by the formation of some companies of ‘distin-
guished citizens’ to patrol suspect districts of the capital (although both
Carlist and Peninsular War experience with these units had revealed
them to be ceremonial rather than warlike in their usefulness).74 Key
military facilities in the Carlists’ path, the Military College at Segovia,
the Artillery College at Alcalá and the Engineers School at Guadalajara,
were evacuated to the capital.75 Zaratiegui suffered the usual Carlist vain-
glory at having conquered a substantial city, and began minting coins
embossed with Don Carlos, and publicising his conquest in the language
of the Christian soldier, thanking Divine Providence for triumphing
over the ‘faithless’ anarchy of the Madrid government and all its false
philosophy.76
   Zaratiegui’s victory speech was untimely, as the pursuit of Espartero’s
battle-hardened Army of the North obliged him to abandon Segovia,
although not before he had emptied the city of its volunteers and
artillery, sabotaging those guns that could not be transported. Yet con-
quests like Segovia and others across Old Castile had served the purpose
of destabilising Cristino defences while the main Carlist invasion of
Cabrera and the Royal Expedition approached the capital from the east.
