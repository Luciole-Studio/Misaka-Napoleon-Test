The defeat at the Améscoas valley handed the initiative to the Carlists,
who now embarked upon a general counteroffensive. Espoz’s recent
strategy of mobile warfare to retain as much territory as possible was
now impossible to sustain, and by the end of May Cristino forces were
driven from all of the Basque country outside the fortiﬁed capitals of
Pamplona, Vitoria, San Sebastián and Bilbao. The greatest loss was the
Baztán valley and the huge stretch of sensitive French border it com-
manded. The evacuation of the Baztán was followed by that of all
Cristino fortiﬁed centres in Navarra outside of Pamplona; the Carlists
occupied them and sometimes even did not even bother to harass
the retreating Cristinos. Liberals, especially those enrolled in Elizondo’s
Militia, felt abandoned and fearful of Carlist reprisals: Oráa called an
extra-ordinary session of the Baztán town hall in order to allay their fears
of the army’s retreat. It was to no avail. This episode crystallised an ide-
ological divide between the army and the militia which had, of course,
long distinguished Spanish liberalism but which now, in the context
of brutal civil war, became an anguished question of survival. Unlike
the Militia, which answered to local government, the army followed
a chain of command deaf to local interests. The militia, by contrast,
was a localised force that in the Carlist north made more enemies than
friends. The army’s retreat from the Baztán turned into a rout in wet
weather, with swollen streams hampering the retreat of often barefoot
soldiers. And the situation for the Cristinos elsewhere in the Basque
country was equally grim. In late April, General Iriarte was defeated at
Guernica, losing almost 500 in action and countless others when sol-
diers careered off a bridge into a fast-ﬂowing river. Captured ofﬁcers were
shot in reprisal for a recent Cristino atrocity elsewhere.156 The only ofﬁ-
