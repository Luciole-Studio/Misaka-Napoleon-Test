   This particular wedge driven between the army elite and the rank and
ﬁle was masked by a humiliation of the throne and altar which appeased
Radical-Liberal opinion and added insult to the injury of Ferdinand’s
royal instincts. In the November 1820 ‘Carvajal affair’, the royal will
came second to the oratorical prowess of the most prominent Liberal in
the Cortes, the ‘divine’ Agustín de Argüelles, author of the Constitution
of Cádiz. Like all Liberals, Argüelles wanted to advance the regalism of
the eighteenth century by subordinating Spain’s traditionally powerful
Catholic Church to state power. Whilst parish priests would be left in
their posts and even paid stipends from the public purse, the cloistered
clergy should be defrocked, put to useful work, and their substantial
lands sold off for proﬁtable and productive uses. Thus, when el divino
threatened to raise the streets of the capital if the King did not sign a bill
to cut the cloistered clergy, Ferdinand ominously left the capital for the
sanctuary of El Escorial, where the clerical party of his younger brother,
Don Carlos, was known to hold sway. Having failed to rally support
there, however, the King had no option but to return to Madrid and
sign the bill into law. Now a virtual prisoner of the Constitution, things
took a turn for the worse for the King as some ‘hotheads’ (exaltados), or
‘men of 1820’ (so-called for their desire to make more radical reforms
than the ‘men of 1812’), triumphantly joined the government.38
   Yet this Liberal bipartisanship did not portend stability. The army lost
its revolutionary unity as Riego and his clique gradually dominated the
levers of power whilst the rank and ﬁle went unpaid and unshod. Just
as the Peninsular War had shown (and as the Carlist War would show)
army ofﬁcers tended to see politics in narrow career terms and their
men as pawns to this end; purely military considerations came second.
