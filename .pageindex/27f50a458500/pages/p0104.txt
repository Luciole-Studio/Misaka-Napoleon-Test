   Moreover, the devolution of power to juntas did not equate to a
democratisation process in the way that rareﬁed studies of the 1835
juntas have argued.74 For one thing, Spanish liberalism never diverged
from an intolerant inability to admit pluralism – or ‘loyal opposition’ –
which in practice meant the exercise of close clientelist networks of
patronage and punishment and rigged elections.75 Mendizábal grew nos-
talgic for his 12 years in Britain where he had discovered that political
adversaries could yet remain friends because of fair play in politics.76
The revolution of the juntas often impaired the war effort by releas-
ing hitherto repressed networks of corruption and cronyism into public
ofﬁce. The Andújar junta soon found itself ﬁghting, in Málaga, a civil
war inside a civil war. The notorious local extortionist, Cuban-born
Vicente Abello – whose coup during the Peninsular War in 1810 had
ruined Málaga’s defence against the French – mobilised a mob to seize
the local Captaincy-Generalship of Granada. Abello justiﬁed this act by
proclaiming the Constitution of 1812, but this did not convince sub-
ordinate juntas at Jaén and Málaga, which led a tax-strike against him.
Andújar promptly deposed him, but Abello’s mob assaulted a meeting of
Andújar revolutionaries held at Granada’s Chancellery, forcing them to
reinstate him. Abello now emerged as a statesman, having successfully
deﬁed the Liberal ‘Right’ of Andújar whilst also violently suppressing his
own Liberal ‘Left’ once he could safely dispense with it. Half-a-dozen
rioters were exiled to the penal colony at Ceuta and a leading mobster,
the ‘notorious Olmo’, was executed.77
   Even the power struggles of the revolutionary juntas paled in com-
parison to the subaltern ranks of the Cristino army who used the
revolutionary climate to vote with their feet. Ofﬁcial bulletins during
1835 complained about Cristino draftees’ illegal emigration to Portugal
and France, and this exodus peaked during the breakdown in authority
in September.78 Popular pressure at local level often forced revolutionary
authorities in Barcelona to suspend the hated draft, and several other
Cristino authorities followed suit, most completely in the Andalucían
town of Antequera.79 Historians of revolutions in politically undevel-
oped societies have noted how communities ‘import’ slogans devised
by elites (‘republic’, ‘Constitution’ and ‘liberty’) and invest them with
their own meaning.80 The meaning the Cristino rank and ﬁle invested
in the revolution was freedom from abusive military service. Sergeants
occupied a key role in the social history of the Cristino army as they
disciplined, championed or subverted their men. In September 1835,
