Cristino Spain moderated its revolutionary Constitution, the Carlists
launched their Expedition as a ‘liberation’ and Cristino Spain was turned
into a praetorian state. We deal with the Constitution of 1837 elsewhere,
but the other two developments bear discussion here. The elevation
by the Cortes of the Commander-in-Chief of the Army of the North,
Baldomero Espartero, to Generalísimo, had both short-term and long-
term consequences. In the short term, Espartero’s overlordship rankled
with his colleagues, in particular the Commander of the Army of the
Centre, Marcelino Oráa. Espartero’s gratuitous failure to acknowledge
Oráa’s victory at Chiva led, in July 1837, to a breakdown of coordina-
tion between the two armies, allowing the Royal Expedition to recover
its fortunes and, indirectly, to inﬂict a serious defeat on the Cristinos at
Herrera.37 It remains moot whether the Cristinos would have handled
the Carlist expedition better had their generals been answerable to the
War Ministry rather than Espartero (Table 7.1).
   The long-term consequences of Espartero’s powers are more poignant,
for they completed the transition to praetorianism, which, apart from a
hiatus during the last quarter of the nineteenth century, would remain
the dominant feature of Spanish politics until the era of Franco. This
transition was not fully understood at ﬁrst for what it was. Espartero,
like Espoz before him, was a man of the Left whose anodyne mantra of
‘Let the national will be fulﬁlled!’ rallied most of both army and Militia
opinion in the ‘peace dividend’ revolution of 1840; only the more rad-
ical wing of the Left noted that the ‘people’s choice’ had in fact turned
into the ‘Spanish Napoleon’. The dominant, pseudo-dictatorial role of
