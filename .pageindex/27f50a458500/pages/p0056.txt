might have worked a decade earlier, but now it was largely irrelevant.
Carlists and Liberals were locked into a ﬁght to the death which was
not just played out on the streets but also at the highest levels of the
state. For Carlists, Cea was a servant of usurpation, and the thin end of
the wedge for godless revolution, and for Liberals, his reforms – and the
associated Royal Statute (see below) – did not go far enough. When Cea,
on 4 November 1833, proclaimed that ‘the principles of government
observed by the late Ferdinand VII will never be changed’, he showed
his fundamental misunderstanding of both sides.62 This popular ten-
sion was described in an 1833 literary journal by the young Romantic
and pessimist, Mariano José de Larra:

  In our vulgar language we are experiencing phrases which have come
  of age and are being spread throughout a whole nation. In political
  vocabulary especially it takes only one word to fall from the lips of
  an orator in his small circle, and a great number of people, anxious
  for words, pick it up, spread it by word of mouth, and with the speed
  of an electric bolt a larger number of living machines repeat and con-
  secrate it, most times without understanding it [Larra’s emphasis], and
  always failing to calculate that it sometimes only takes one word to
  raise a mob, inﬂame passions, and so cause a revolution.63

Meanwhile, Cea’s purging of political factionalism in the army reduced
this force by the time of Ferdinand’s death to the toothless size of
45,000 men. This was certainly too small for a military coup to seize
the state, which was his intention: but it was also too small to crush
the Carlist insurgency that grew in the north upon word of the King’s
death. By 1837, the Cristino army would grow to over 200,000 effec-
tives; but the early months of the civil war would prove the error in
Cea’s strategy.64
