   Bilbao was certainly not a unanimous decision from the Carlist junta
of generals which met to decide the matter at Durango. La Torre urged
a bold breakout invasion south towards Madrid as the best way to
win European recognition and aid and so win the war. But Erro was
resolved, and Lt-General Vicente González Moreno’s even-handed let-
ter on 15 October 1836 swayed the decision. González explained to his
King that Bilbao was the less worse option (‘better’ options, he insisted,
did not exist in civil wars). A strike south, by contrast, would expose
the Basque country to the mercies of the Cristino defensive ring and
so destabilise civilian morale. Moreover, this time the Carlists had more
artillery to use against Bilbao, including some abandoned pieces from
the Peninsular War which had been salvaged and even those 13-inch
shells ﬁred by the British from Pasajes and San Sebastián that had failed
to explode on impact and had been turned in by local peasants.144 Also,
a dispute between Bilbao’s civil and military authorities over the fund-
ing of fortiﬁcations had simmered since the revolution of the juntas,
slowing progress improving the city’s defences.145 The Carlist hold on
Bilbao’s outlying towns and countryside, meanwhile, had strengthened
since 1835 and had emboldened morale against the blockaded Cristinos.
One local soldier made good was ‘generally on duty blockading Bilbao,
and as soon as the cowardly Cristinos withdraw their advanced guard
within the walls for the night, he and half-a-dozen wags will follow
and write some moral or political abuse of the Queen on the town
gates’.146 But Carlist morale was matched by the Cristinos who had for-
tiﬁed the heights and nationalised convents surrounding Bilbao, and
could rely on some 4,300 soldiers and militiamen garrisoning the city
with as many as 86 cannon. When the Carlists launched their assault
on 22 October, the Cristinos were ready for them.147
