   In July 1835 the former royalist colonel, Vicente Mateos, was caught
near Gibraltar with documents incriminating him as Don Carlos’s man
and was imprisoned pending court martial.121 Mateos was executed
amidst the radicalism of the 1835 juntas in retrospective ‘revolution-
ary justice’ for his role in the execution of the Liberal hero, Manzanares,
in the Torrijos expedition of 1831.122 Brigadier Salvador Malavilla was
named Mateos’s successor as Carlist commander in Andalucía. Malavilla
had presided over a plan to raise a loan in Gibraltar to fund the cre-
ation of a proper army. (Seeing the threat of Gibraltar and suspecting
British motives, Narváez, on 22 November 1836, reported the Carlist
deployment of three battalions on the border in the Campo de Gibraltar
as proof of their intent to launder their loot in the British colony.123 )
But when he was arrested and his correspondence uncovered, Malavilla
was executed in Seville (after which the dangerous rank of Carlist
commander in Andalucía passed to Pedro Fermín de Iriberri).124 The
Gibraltar–Serranía de Ronda conspiratorial network proved a potent way
to induce Carlism in a part of Spain which would otherwise have been
entirely lost to the cause of legitimism. May 1836, for example, saw
Seville rocked by a conspiracy led by a group of priests and redun-
dant monks who had hired the allegiance of some tanners, bakers and
sergeants, to spring Carlist prisoners from gaol and seize the city for Don
Carlos. Luckily for the authorities, government spies were active in the
cafés, and arrests were made before the plot was enacted.125 Gibraltar
provided a springboard for cloak-and-dagger smugglers, especially the
mochileros (‘backpackers’) who made the seldom-trodden paths of the
Serranía de Ronda their own, and who, in the context of civil war, could
be recruited to act for Carlist conspiracies.126 The revolutionary authori-
ties knew that Gómez’s invasion could act as a magnet for insurgents in
the Serranía de Ronda and even ﬁfth columnists inside Cristino towns
and cities. In October 1836 the Seville Armament and Defence Commit-
tee warned that gatherings of more than ﬁve people within a league of
known enemy positions would be broken up by bullets, any correspon-
dence with the Carlists pronounced espionage and Seville closed off to
all comers without passports.127
