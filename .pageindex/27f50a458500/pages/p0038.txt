   Only under duress did the Liberals seriously open its ranks to ple-
beians, and even then on the understanding that they would do the
bidding of their social superiors. From the outset the militia vexed the
regular army, which was set on extracting a reward for having liber-
ated the nation. Militiamen enjoyed the same immunity from civil
jurisdiction (fuero militar) as the army, the only vague and practically
meaningless condition for this privilege being ‘when on active duty’.
Thus, as during the Carlist War, militiamen and ofﬁcers shared similar
facilities enabling them to compete on matters of entitlement and hon-
our. The army reserved the power to force the militia to disband under
a state of siege, but this ‘nuclear option’ only displaced inter-service
rivalry, to the streets, cafes and the Cortes where the Jacobin deputy,
Romero Alpuente, repeatedly called for the army to be replaced by the
militia altogether.31 Radicals like Romero Alpuente were representing
the Radical Patriotic Societies and local militias which increasingly exer-
cised an extra-parliamentary ‘double regime’ in Liberal Spain. Romero
Alpuente owed his radicalism in part to his antimilitarism – the Liberals
saw soldiers as brutish mercenaries lacking in civic nationalism – and
in part to his passion to cut the tax burden, the beneﬁts of which, to
his mind at least, should be used to improve living standards. Many
more civilian Liberals, by contrast, assumed that Spain’s depleted post-
imperial coffers should fund the expansion of state power provided for
by the Constitution. The dissident sevillano utilitarian, José María Blanco
White, criticised this careerism, noting that only Spain’s minority classes
of students, literati, merchants and ofﬁceholders could gain from con-
stitutional rule, and his words were borne out by the empleomanía, or
unrestrained competition for the government posts, unleashed by the
Triennium.32 Whilst many Liberals sincerely believed that expanded
government would eventually reverse Spain’s social crisis, empleomanía,
as in other Hispanic polities, should really be understood as the lad-
der undistinguished place-seekers climbed in order to achieve personal
honour.33
   The regime had to recruit constitutional administrators on a dimin-
