   Local authorities thought it expedient to excuse the prison mas-
sacre with reference to ‘the inhumane ferocity and cruelty with which
the defenders of the usurpation sacriﬁced at Hort and other parts of
Catalonia a large number of our prisoners’.198 But behind the scenes,
army reinforcements were quietly entering the city, Pastors was deposed
for his temporising and Captain-General Espoz dared to impose a state
of siege. Proclamations of the Constitution of 1812 were forbidden
and the most egregious Radicals in the militia arrested and exiled to
the Canaries. To guard against sedition, prisoners were dispatched, for
the ﬁrst leg of the journey, on Royal Navy rather than Cristino ships.
Amongst the deported was Aviraneta, who was accused of inciting the
massacre with alarmist claims of a Sardinian-led international Carlist
invasion bound for Catalonia.199
   But there were international plots, and Don Carlos was playing to the
international gallery of public opinion when, on 20 February, his man-
ifesto condemned the Liberals as anarchists and foreign revolutionaries
whose barbaric outrages at Barcelona, Zaragoza and elsewhere made
a mockery of the humanitarian intervention of ‘respectable powers’.
True Spaniards would not stomach such ‘democracy’, which amounted
to barbarism.200 His manifesto unwittingly coincided with news of the
execution of Ramón Cabrera’s mother, who had been held hostage by
Cristino forces for almost two years, and this apparently senseless act
of barbarity ricocheted around Europe in support of Don Carlos’s mani-
festo. María Griñó’s iconic end reveals a story of the intimate madness of
revenge, for she was shot in reprisal for Cabrera’s execution of the may-
ors of two villages in contested areas of Teruel province (Valdealgorfa
and Torrecilla), who had given Cristino mobile militiamen intelligence
about local Carlists.201 The mayors’ deaths prompted the Cristino arrest
of Cabrera’s three sisters, two of whom, ironically, were married to mili-
tiamen who appear to have connived at their escape. Tragically, as soon
as Cabrera got news of his sisters’ escape, he avenged his mother by
executing four Cristino hostages of his own, all wives or daughters of
serving Cristino ofﬁcers.202 Such high-proﬁle Cristino victims obscured
countless others known only to their families and, sometimes, the Radi-
cal press. Thus, the ﬁve-year Carlist occupation of the hill settlement of
Alcalá de la Selva (Teruel) began with the dragging out of his home and
public execution of the ‘only liberal in the village’.203
