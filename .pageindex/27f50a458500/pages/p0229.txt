political power, but his gradual rather than rapid offensive still left no
one in doubt as to the hopelessness of the Carlists’ strategic situation.
As we have seen, the immobilisation of Catalan Carlism caused by the
España affair gave Cabrera no relief from the north. Cabrera’s active
defence began well but turned for the worse in mid-December when
he fell gravely ill. In February 1840, the Valencia press reported popular
manifestations in the Maestrazgo willing Cabrera’s recovery, evidence
of resilient morale, perhaps, but also evidence of how personalised the
leadership of the Carlist east remained.143 Whilst Espartero picked off
fortiﬁed points one by one, Cabrera could not even rise from his bed
until May 1840.144
   Cabrera knew that his line could only hold if the war were reignited
in the north. But the French police frustrated most plots and prevented
suspicious Spaniards from concentrating on the border. Despite these
measures, it was remarkable that Balmaseda still managed to invade the
Spanish Basque country in June 1840 with some 510 men and 470 cav-
alry. But this failed to reignite the war. Seeing this, Cabrera, on 5 July
1840, crossed the French frontier at the head of some 4,600 infantry
and 300 cavalry.145 Cristino forces now occupied all national territory.
But the victorious Liberals did not take any chances. The major Carlist
arms foundries at Villarluengo were destroyed in order to dissuade local
Carlists from restarting the war. The First Carlist War was over.146
