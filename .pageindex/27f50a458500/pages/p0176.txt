   The third reason was military. The Carlists had proved secure in their
northern heartland, and the Gómez expedition had shown their abil-
ity to destabilise the enemy’s rearguard. Thus, a three-pronged strategy
gave them the initiative: whereas the actual Royal Expedition marched
south-east, a smaller expedition struck south-west, and the bulk of the
Royal Army remained in the Basque country to harass Cristino garrisons
in a series of local offensives. Moreover, the Royal Government had
demanded the removal of as many Carlist forces as possible from the
Basque country, as the local economy was on the point of collapsing
under the ‘total war’ burdens of men, food and munitions.43 March 1837
had seen Navarrese battalions in Estella openly insubordinate for the
ﬁrst time, shouting ‘Death to the ojalateros and the junta! Long live the
people (paisanos)!’, as their pay arrears had made them desperate. They
disbelieved the King’s promise of redress.44 It remains unclear whether
the Expedition’s champions were exaggerating in their warning that the
Basque country could support only 15 more days of operations.45 But
rising interest from some Carlists in quack military solutions to their
strategic blockade (including a British adventurer’s offer to arm cor-
sairs with a prototype torpedo from the Woolwich arsenal) is highly
suggestive.46
   The fourth reason was moralistic in nature. The ‘re-evangelisation’
of revolutionary Spain was Don Carlos’s main strategy, even though,
in measuring his success in the number of Masses held in liberated vil-
lages, he invited criticism from contemporaries and historians.47 Church
ordinations and attendance had collapsed in the wake of two years
of Cristino anti-clericalism. The Carlist press trailed stories of aban-
doned parishes in the wake of the schism with Rome, the powerlessness
of the bishops, the expulsion or imprisonment of priests by Cristino
soldiers and militia, and, above all, the mendizabalista disentailment;
indeed, some 400 parishes closed due to lack of ministers during the
ﬁrst half of 1836 alone.48 Linked to this moralistic reason was the
real hope that Madrid would bloodlessly pronounce in favour of Don
Carlos as soon as his army reached the city walls. A foreign journalist
who had visited Madrid the previous year reckoned that some ‘three-
fourths of the respectable portion of its population are in favour of
Don Carlos’.49
