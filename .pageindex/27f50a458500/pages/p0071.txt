   Although religious personnel in the rest of Cristino Spain were spared
a massacre for the rest of 1834 and the ﬁrst three months of 1835, the
climate of hostility grew in the wake of Madrid. To some degree this
was remarkable, as even though relations between Madrid and the Holy
See deteriorated, culminating with the expulsion of the nuncio in 1835,
all but one of the Spanish bishops obeyed Rome’s orders not to recog-
nise the Carlist insurgency as legitimate. Thus, clerics who joined the
insurgency did so as individuals rather than as an institution, but given
the well-known religious claims of the Carlists, it was understandable
how episodes of anti-clerical violence spiralled at local levels in Cristino
Spain.70 Monks in Huéscar (Granada) were deported after they tried to
evict militiamen who played revolutionary music and adorned their
hats with the green ribbons of the Constitution. Fines were imposed
upon priests who refused to offer blessings to Isabel II during Mass.71
The enemy’s anti-clericalism gave Carlists hope of intervention in their
favour from abroad. One priest whipped up popular Carlism in the
Almanzora river area of Almería – hundreds of miles from the front line –
with the promise that Carlists from Genoa would make landfall.72 In fact
the expedition landed in Catalonia, and comprised mainly Catalan émi-
grés from the transition of 1833 rather than Italians. These went to
ground in the Barceloneta district of Barcelona, troubling the watch-
ful Cristino city authorities who feared a ﬁfth column.73 This was the
