   In retrospect, Don Carlos’s campaign of re-evangelisation is not as
supine as it may seem. Both the Catholic resistance against revolution-
aries during the Vendée during the 1790s and during the twentieth-
century Mexican Cristiada showed a link between religious fervour
and popular resistance, and Don Carlos had been encouraged by the
Church’s policy of excommunicating expropriators and purchasers of
disentailed ecclesiastical property. But the King’s thinking created two
ﬂaws, one of strategy and another of perception. As the expedition was
supposed to be a government-in-waiting, its mobility was burdened by
excessive numbers of non-combatants and shoestring logistics, which
impeded the Carlists’ ability to transport and deploy sufﬁcient artillery
and alienated ‘liberated’ communities more than necessary by forcing
the ‘liberators’ to live off the land.53 The second ﬂaw was far more
profound. Don Carlos believed that the enemy’s will to ﬁght was still
coerced by the ‘usurping’ elites of 1833, and that the war remained
a dynastic dispute. In fact, such had been the wave of revolutionary
change since the start of the civil war that the Bourbon family troubles
had become irrelevant, or at the very least their solution could no longer
