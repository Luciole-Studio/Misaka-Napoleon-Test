   The Carlist insurgency obliged María Cristina to look for political
support amongst the exiled Spanish Left. On 23 October 1833, despite
Cea’s objections, a near-total amnesty was proclaimed for exiled Lib-
erals. Meanwhile, army ofﬁcers whose careers had survived the purges
were rewarded with powers to control, and disband where necessary,
the Royalist Volunteers. At ﬁrst these measures, along with initially suc-
cessful counter-insurgency operations and the onset of the northern
winter, promised an early peace. However, the Carlists had merely dis-
persed – ‘changed their shirts’ – and would return to ﬁght again.16 Given
that the number of rural insurgents was potentially limitless, and given
that the Cristino army was still too small, no end to the conﬂict was
in sight in 1833. A Cristino cordon sanitaire around the Basque coun-
try was spread too thinly and sapped reserves which might have dealt
a decisive counter-insurgency blow. Meanwhile, the process of purg-
ing the Carlist-ridden Volunteers was still not complete. At one end of
Spain, the weeks after the start of the war saw Liberal Cádiz reinforce
its Volunteers against any Carlist attempts, whilst at the other end the
Royalist Volunteers had either gone over to the insurgency or were held
in abeyance by Cristino captains-general reluctant to call them out in
a poisoned and uncertain political climate.17 Into this vacuum stepped
the Cristino army, but this was too small due to Cea’s fears of a coup,
and it had risen to only 60,000 effectives by the end of 1833. Also, a
