methods of raising internal revenue. In 1817 a ﬁscal reform created the
derechos de puertas, which saddled municipalities with a greater burden
of taxation to fund state penury.10 Smugglers and bandits who managed
to dodge provincial revenue guards became as much popular heroes as
saboteurs of the state coffers. Meanwhile, the voided attempts of the
Liberals to substitute state jurisdiction in the countryside for that of
the manor, thereby creating a free market in landownership, were now
revived as a desperate means of raising revenue.11 Attempts to conﬁs-
cate and sell off ecclesiastical and military lands met with the successful
opposition of the Church and nobility. But a portion of lands owned
directly by the Crown, together with some common lands held by
municipalities, were sold off.12 Whilst this measure barely helped the
state’s ﬁnances, it obviously reduced the resources of the municipalities
and squeezed the amount of land available for rent.
   Underlying the economic crisis was what contemporaries identiﬁed
as a moral crisis. Parish priests anathematised the recent ‘atheism’ and
‘Jacobinism’ of the Liberals in equal proportion to what one royal
decree termed the ‘vices introduced during the war’, which ranged from
widespread prostitution to the new practice for women, even respectable
ones, to walk the streets unaccompanied by men.13 This practice would
be compounded during the Carlist War when women entered public
roles in the absence of male heads of family, a handful even becoming
militants and strikers. The rural masses, which during the war had seen
their hopes of owning nationalised land dashed by the Liberals, now
faced similar disappointment as the King kept the land in the hands
of its feudal owners.14 The rural population accordingly took varying
forms of direct action. Where legal appeals for redistribution (reparto)
failed, peasants tore noble insignia from churches, refused feudal dues
and turned ploughshares back into swords.15 The Fernandine regime
responded with the rigours of an absolutist police state, reviving for a
ﬁnal lease of life the Inquisition which, as one Liberal fugitive discov-
ered, kept ‘an active agent in every single village in the realm’.16 The
