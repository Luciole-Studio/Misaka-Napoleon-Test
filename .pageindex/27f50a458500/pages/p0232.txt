   But the real legacy of Spain’s bloodiest civil war was embodied in
the thousands of individual anonymous tragedies, beyond the ken of
historians. Often these were given a radical political meaning. Wid-
ows guarded their martyred menfolk’s memory, cherishing images of
Espartero as the people’s war hero, hanging veterans’ militia uniforms
in a pride of place. When militia veterans presided over the 1841
exhumation of the massacred victims of the 1835 Carlist atrocity at
Rubielos, they did so not just to remember these martyrs to liberty,
but also to reafﬁrm the claim of the citizenry-in-arms to a stake in
Spain’s Liberal future.5 The suppression of the militia in 1844 did not
remove it as an actual force. Disbanded veterans retained their own net-
works which sometimes sprang into life during later revolutions. Neither
Cristino militia nor soldiers remained silent about their harsh treatment
imposed upon them as a consequence of the peace treaty, the failure
of land redistribution or even of decent conditions of service in the
peacetime army.
   The Cristino victory was attributed to Espartero, leftist war hero and
‘Duke of Victory’. His success was a cause for both national and sec-
tarian celebration. Whereas his victory parade through Barcelona was
so rapturous that he had to dismount and allow himself to be carried
aloft by the crowd, in Seville, where Espartero’s presence was substituted
by a giant portrait, thousands marched against the moderados and sev-
eral hundred hotheads burnt to the ground the ofﬁce of the rightist El
Conservador newspaper.6 Espartero was leading a groundswell of veter-
ans’ opinions against the socially regressive centralisation of political
power inherent in the moderado abolition of the autonomy of all but
the smallest municipalities. Both militia and army veterans joined this
‘peace dividend’ revolution, and the situation in Granada, where the
army took no time at all in following the lead of the militia, was typical.7
Hopes were brieﬂy raised by the ‘peace dividend’ revolution of October
1840, caused by moderado attempts to abolish municipal autonomy, and
which forced the abdication of that obsolescent symbol of the conﬂict,
María Cristina, and her replacement the following year as sole regent
by the war hero, Baldomero Espartero. The revolution was started by
hundreds of thousands of militia and army veterans, and it was their
common identity as veterans of a struggle for liberty which united
them. Many veterans were swept up in the class conﬂict that reached
an unprecedented scale in Barcelona during 1842 and again in 1843,
