   As the Radical insurrection spread, the patriarch of exaltismo, Evaristo
San Miguel, deﬁed the Istúriz government’s orders to resign his com-
mand of the Army of the Centre and rallied the Zaragoza Militia
to the cause of the Constitution of 1812, keeping loyal government
forces at bay. Santander’s garrison joined the revolution because of pay
and supply grievances.21 Málaga, the cradle of revolution, exercised its
sovereignty by ‘declaring war’ on the pro-moderado French after one of
the latter’s vessels refused to acknowledge salutes from the revolution-
ary authorities.22 For the time being, meanwhile, Madrid stayed loyal.
Cardero had been mooted as leader of a revolutionary pronunciamiento
in the capital, but Quesada showed some timely ruthlessness for which
he would shortly pay with his life. He proclaimed martial law in the
capital, threatening any political activists who disturbed the peace with
the death penalty. But Madrid’s temporary quiescence was overtaken
by the expanding revolution elsewhere, including Cristino Spain’s sec-
ond city, where Captain-General Espoz, though responding admittedly
less quickly than San Miguel had done in Zaragoza, used his revolu-
tionary credentials to win over Barcelona’s powerful popular Radical
movement to his leadership and to the proclamation of the Constitu-
tion of 1812, thereby forestalling the sort of political violence to which
Catalonia’s capital had become accustomed. The welcome bloodless-
ness of Barcelona’s revolution was Espoz’s last service before he ﬁnally
succumbed to his agonising stomach cancer on 24 December 1836.23
   The revolution culminated at La Granja, at the hands of sergeants
posted to the Spanish monarchy’s summer residence at the town of San
Ildefonso, some 80 kilometres north of the capital. Nineteenth-century
writers, for whom the French Revolution was the dominant example
of royal humiliation, were fascinated by María Cristina’s submission
at La Granja, seeing this as the product of conspiracy funded by Rad-
icals, unlike the popular revolution of the juntas the previous year.24
Certainly, structural pay arrears made even the royal bodyguard vulner-
able to agitators, and it was logical for ofﬁcers and sergeants who could
not pay their men to search for political solutions instead. Twentieth-
century writers gave the La Granja revolution secondary importance to
that of the juntas the previous year. Whilst the social signiﬁcance of
lowly sergeants dictating to royalty was noted, writers saw La Granja
