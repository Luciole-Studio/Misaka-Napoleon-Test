   In the wake of this rising a memorable debate ensued in the Cortes
in which Palarea, a mooted beneﬁciary of the rising, bizarrely attributed
the 18 January events to a Carlist plot, whilst Alcalá Galiano and others
condemned it as showing how honest, liberty-loving soldiers and militi-
amen had been led astray. The rightist Liberal, Fernández de Córdova, by
contrast, was scathing both of Cardero’s careerism and of Llauder’s weak-
ness in allowing the insurgents to march out unpunished. All pointed
the ﬁnger at Manuel Llauder, who had combined Bonapartist vainglory
with shocking timing and incompetence. Llauder was dismissed from
the War Ministry and dispatched with a token decoration to captain the
generals in Catalonia. His replacement was Jerónimo Valdés, a man of
the Left, whose reputation had grown as Captain-General of Valencia,
in which role he had virtually wiped out the insurgency, and who had
acted as temporary Commander-in-Chief of the Army of the North in
April 1835.27 When the speaker of the Cortes on 21 January made a
widely reported speech condemning the heavy-handed treatment of
Liberals across Spain, including their imprisonment in Cartagena for
singing the Riego Hymn, it was clear that the Cardero rising had shifted
Cristino politics to the Left.28
   Madrid remained in a state of heightened tension for months. A riot
on 11 May 1835 provoked by the Eliot Treaty led to rioters surround-
ing the Cortes in protest against Martínez de la Rosa, who narrowly
avoided being lynched by indignant Urban Militia when he alighted
from his coach. The capital was becoming ever more radicalised, as
remaining royalists found that they could not walk the streets in safety.29
Popular royalism had been greatly diminished by the Bourbon feud
which saw Don Carlos rebelling in the north and the emigration of
other family members, leaving María Cristina and her two daughters
alone in the capital to negotiate the leftist shift in Cristino politics.30
Thus, a leftist junior ofﬁcer and his network of Liberal conspirators had
caused the death of Madrid’s captain-general, the humiliation of the War
Minister, an embarrassed debate in the Cortes and a terminally weak-
ened moderado administration that was unable to survive any further
radicalisation.31 For a while it even seemed as though the controversial
question of foreign mediation, in the form of the Eliot Treaty, could be
such a spark. Despite the Francophile de la Rosa’s constant reluctance
to see British-led foreign intervention, the government now sought it,
citing relevant clauses in the Quadruple Alliance which had been signed
