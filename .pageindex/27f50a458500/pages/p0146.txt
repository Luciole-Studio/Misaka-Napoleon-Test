   This security crisis meant that villages and small towns in both
Castiles which had no Cristino garrison could be regarded as ‘Carlist’,
either because they were occupied or invaded by bands or because
these bands operated close enough in the vicinity to induce willing or
unwilling ‘geographical loyalty’. At least 24 Carlist bands were known
by Cristino security forces to be plaguing both Castiles in 1836. Their
members came from one or more of the following four categories:
deserters/draft-dodgers, men conscripted by the Carlist bands, crimi-
nals/adventurers and agricultural workers displaced by war-ravaged har-
vests and the Liberal property revolution. The presence of so many rural
poor in Carlist bands, and the fact that these were usually led or men-
tored by reactionary priests, led generations of historians, especially the
Marxists and neo-Carlists of the second half of the twentieth century, to
understand the Carlist war as a conﬂict between a liberal, urban, mod-
ernising bourgeoisie and a backward, traditional, Catholic rural society,
the latter comprising a broad spectrum of threatened livelihoods united
by their false consciousness of throne and altar.68 Yet the twentieth-
century preoccupation with class conﬂict was too self-contained and
hermetic. Castile showed that ‘Carlism’ covered many more categories
than declassés, and in any case the same economic grievance could be a
recruiting sergeant for Cristinism, too. In New Castile, for example, the
naturalised Spaniard and Irish-born veteran of the British Army, Colonel
Jorge Dawson Flinter, beat the Carlists at their own game. Inﬂuenced by
his ﬁrst-hand knowledge of the Spanish American Independence wars,
Flinter created a Cristino counter-guerrilla force of desperate men called
peseteros (they earned one peseta per day) who were armed with nothing
more than shotguns and who fought the Carlists and their support-
ers in a private, asymmetrical fashion.69 Later in the war, the Cristinos
recruited a mounted volunteer guerrilla force in Logroño under Martín
Zurbano Baras (alias Martín Varea), which plundered and terrorised
Carlist villages in the Basque country for pecuniary gain.70
