Conclusions




At the war’s end in 1840, the Catalan priest and ‘common sense’
philosopher, Jaime Balmés, posited an apposite question: ‘if the Carlists
had been nothing more than a gang of thieves and outlaws, why could
the Cristino armies not destroy them?’1 Of course, thieves and outlaws
may have used Carlism as a ﬂag of convenience, but this was largely
outside the safe zones of the Basque country, and to a lesser extent,
Catalonia and the Maestrazgo. But Balmés was correctly parodying the
self-assured arrogance that made Spanish Liberals view opposition to
their transition and regime as a symptom of rural, priest-ridden igno-
rance. The Cristino denigration of their enemy as ‘bandits’, caribes and
facciosos was as facile as the Carlists’ ﬁght against negros and ‘usurpers’.
Yet the fact that Carlist wars would resurface (in 1846, in 1872 and again
in 1936), albeit on nothing like the scale of the ﬁrst war, showed that
there was no peace and reconciliation, and the persistence of belligerent
language represented far deeper tensions.
  For one thing, the peace of 1840 was really no peace at all. Spain
would remain in a revolutionary versus counter-revolutionary crisis
until 1844 at least, when a draconian moderado crackdown and the insti-
tution of the ﬁrst rural gendarmerie (Civil Guard) illegalised most open
recourses to popular radicalism (i.e. deﬁnitively abolishing the veterans’
Militia) while also presiding over the ﬁrst real economic boom of the
nineteenth century. The immediate weeks and months after the con-
ﬂict saw violence persist. The Vergara settlement had positive effects in
Asturias and Galicia where ‘Carlists’ had to revert to banditry and were
therefore targeted by united local communities.2 But it was powerless
in other areas to arrest violence. The area around Ciudad Real (New
Castile) was particularly disﬁgured by conﬂicts between ‘Liberals’ and
