42   Spain’s First Carlist War, 1833–40


   Whilst Liberals attacked Fernandine absolutism from the Left, Carlists
did so from the Right. The rightists’ moment came in the so-called
‘War of the Aggrieved’ of 1826–27, a rising centred in rural Catalonia
(where the rebels were called Malcontents) which was Carlist in all but
name: quite literally, as the rising rallied to its ‘Manifesto of the Fed-
eration of Pure Royalists’ (Maniﬁesto de la Federación de Realistas Puros),
signed in Madrid in November 1826 by anonymous ‘Members of this
Catholic Federation, graced by Heaven and Eternal Blessing’ who explic-
itly called for the replacement of King Ferdinand by Don Carlos. The
authorship of this manifesto remains in doubt, but its spirit is clear. Its
demands for the re-establishment of the Inquisition, banishment of the
amnestied afrancesados, an end to technocratic reforms and the removal
of the constraints on the power of the Royalist Volunteers, all described
the demands of the most reactionary (apostólico, or theocratic) wing of
the Carlist insurrection of 1833.32 The Aggrieved were led by Agustín
Saperes and the smuggler, Josep Bosoms, both ‘heroes’ of 1822–23. Their
followers – whose numbers varied between 20,000 and 30,000 that
summer – comprised unemployed ofﬁcers and men, suborned Catalan
militia (known as somatenes) and Royalist Volunteers, villagers made
desperate by ongoing capitalist reforms in agriculture and several ide-
ologically committed ‘true believers’, most of whom were priests.33 The
Agraviados’ insurrection brieﬂy invaded the Basque provinces, the cradle
of the later Carlist revolt, seizing some Royalist Volunteers arms near
Vitoria.34
   Despite their manifesto, most of the Aggrieved claimed to be ‘lib-
erating’ Ferdinand VII from ‘evil counsellors’, the amnestied courtiers
of moderate-Liberal and afrancesado backgrounds, whilst at local level
manifestoes spoke against economic modernisation. Spain continued
the drive towards a single national market and a cash economy at a time
when crop prices were plummeting: from their peak level of 100 during
the famine year of 1812, agricultural prices plummeted to 63 in 1814,
49 in 1820, 45 in 1826 and to a low of 33 in 1830.35 Catalan parish
priests deﬁed the loyal Fernandine bishops in droves, drawing money
and even weapons from the restored monasteries.36 All bishops except
one stayed loyal for strategic reasons: the cloistered clergy had recovered
their numbers from a low during the Jacobinism of 1823 of 8,000 to a
ﬁgure which would climb to 61,272 by 1833.37 This ecclesiastical recov-
ery had been achieved by Ferdinand’s decision in 1823 to return to the
Church its lands which had been nationalised during the Triennium,
