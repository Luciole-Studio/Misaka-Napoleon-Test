90   Spain’s First Carlist War, 1833–40


   The worst anti-clerical violence struck Catalonia, where mountain
Carlists threatened Cristino garrisons protecting the Catalan coastal
plain. Carlists were blockading the proto-industrial town of Reus and
ambushing its garrison’s supplies and patrols. An ‘exterminating angel’
monk led an ambush in July which left, amongst seven Cristino dead,
a mutilated militiaman who was father of eight children. In response
Reus’s convents went up in ﬂames, whilst Llauder had few troops to
spare outside of Barcelona even to ﬁght the Carlists let alone pacify
the militia on his own side. Even in Barcelona the military authorities
were losing control.43 Monasteries were torched and religious person-
nel killed, often at the hands of what contemporaries called ‘obscene’
women. The defection to Carlism of the Catalan nobility had made
the Principality’s clerics second only to those in the Basque country in
their pro-Carlism, and they were confronted by Cristino Spain’s reddest
city in 1835.44 Barcelona’s new anti-clerical tradition was forged by its
war refugees, nascent proletarian militancy, and even its inauguration
in 1834 of crowd-pulling bullﬁghting in the working-class Barceloneta
district.45 Part of this revolutionism was being channelled in support
of the federal Republican agenda of Xaudaró y Fábregas, editor of El
Catalán and a militia commander known for recruiting citizen-soldiers
who failed to meet supposedly stringent property qualiﬁcations.46 The
ministerial press, by contrast, was relegated to impotent commentary,
complaining how ‘the worst symptom of our situation is the simultane-
ity of the killing of the clerics on the one hand and the, if not universal,
then common indifference with which they were witnessed, heard, and
spoken of on the other’.47 Ambivalence describes attitudes better than
indifference, as Catholics were cowed by the collusion of the author-
ities with the massacre, or, as a young girl later remembered: ‘I recall
the upheaval at home and my family’s despair. As if in a dream I recall
the reddish glow illuminating a high wall overlooking our roof-terrace,
and of the horror etched in my mother’s face as two friars ran for their
lives past our house.’48 An out-of-town lawyer who tried to stop a textile
worker beating a captive priest was told to ‘fuck off!’49 Captain-General
Llauder’s humiliation during the Cardero crisis had weakened the once-
strong relationship he had enjoyed with Barcelona’s dominant business
community and the militia it patronised. His military tribunals failed to
arrest any serious anti-clerical instigators, and his inability to protect the
Church matched the distant Carlists’ inability to rescue it. Barcelona’s
realistic civic authorities conﬁscated the contents of the religious insti-
tutions, boarded their doors and windows, and then abandoned them
