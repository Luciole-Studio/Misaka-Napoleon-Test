   Cristino Spain in 1836 was more worried about Carlist than Bolivarian
subversion of its colonial and ex-colonial relations. During 1836, Don
Carlos’s agents in Rome focused on trying to frustrate the Vatican’s
impending recognition of the former New Spain and putative jewel
in the Pretender’s empire.52 But various schemes also ﬂourished in the
extant colonies, including a bid to raise Cuba for Don Carlos once
Gómez Expedition had been launched in Spain, on another occasion
when Bilbao was under its second siege, and on a third occasion when
the Royal Expedition crossed the Ebro. Conditions were ripe for sub-
version in this colony. Captain-General Tacón, a sexagenarian veteran
of the Spanish American Independence wars and high-handed architect
of administrative and police reforms, countermanded orders from the
revolutionary Cortes in 1836 to hold elections on the island under the
Constitution of 1812. But whilst Tacón’s order held sway in Havana,
Cuba’s second city, Santiago, inspired both by the possibilities of the
Constitution of 1812 and by resentment at the capital’s economic domi-
nance, staged a ‘Liberal’ revolt, which saw the underdeveloped east hold
out for three months against Tacón’s military rule. Tacón’s paciﬁcation
brought him no respite, as news reached him of a mysterious ‘English
