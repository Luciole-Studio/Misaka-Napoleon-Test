   This ‘atheistic chaos’ was presided over, since the fall of Toreno, by
Prime Minister Juan Álvarez Mendizábal. His comparatively humble ori-
gins (and Jewish ancestry) are the exception that proves the rule of
Spanish Liberal elites having long-established privileged backgrounds.117
Mendizábal’s cosmopolitan experience in ﬁnance had allowed him to
source vital funds for Espoz’s invasion of 1830 and the cause of Dom
Pedro in the Portuguese civil war.118 His premiership of 1835–36 marked
the peak of British inﬂuence in Cristino Spain, via Ambassador Villiers,
who was so powerful that at times he virtually chaired cabinet meet-
ings. Mendizábal’s background was targeted by the Carlist press which
condemned the usurping Prime Minister as a Wandering Jew merchant
bent on looting the Spanish Church as collateral for international Jewish
ﬁnanciers and their British guardians.119 Mendizábal’s representative
government was attacked by the Carlists, as a ‘repugnant Protestant
innovation’ that distorted 14 centuries of Spanish Catholic monar-
chy, and later, when his rule became more dictatorial, as Robespierre
reborn.120
   Yet Mendizábal’s immediate task was neither to democratise nor
demonise Spain, but to resurrect a working central government which
could both revive the deteriorating war effort and subject the revolution-
ary juntas. This daunting task he approached with the mantra of ‘Liberal
union’, the rallying of all anti-Carlist forces, which he later summarised
as ‘forgetting, respecting, revising, repairing and reforming’.121 Whilst
rejecting, for now, demands to reinstitute the Constitution of 1812,
Mendizábal prorogued the Royal Statute and incorporated the juntas
into provincial deputations along the 1812 model but with different
powers. They were charged with establishing and managing committees
