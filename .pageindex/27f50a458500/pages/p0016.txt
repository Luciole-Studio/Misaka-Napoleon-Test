   Even though both the traditionalists and neo-traditionalists sympa-
thised with historical Carlism, they were alienated by the leftward shift
of ‘Carlism 1968’, which inspired a new generation of neo-Carlist schol-
arship. The neo-Carlists are the fourth category, and their revisionism
is impossible to understand without reference to the socialist ‘self-
management’ (autogestión) doctrine advocated from the late 1960s by
the Carlist ‘Prince’ Carlos Hugo de Borbón Parma and his followers. The
neo-Carlists resented how the Francoist ‘families’ of neo-traditionalists
and National Catholics had hijacked their movement since 1937 in
the name of the ‘Movement’, and the neo-Carlist movement accord-
ingly faced repression under the last years of the Franco dictatorship.20
Their most strident scholar was Josep Carles Clemente. Clemente argued
that early Carlism was an inchoate social protest against nineteenth-
century oligarchs who were using the Liberal property revolution to
marginalise villagers’ livelihoods and erode the communitarian model
enshrined in the Basque fueros (devolved economic and political pow-
ers under the Spanish crown)21 Carlism predominated in the Basque
country, he explained, because since the eighteenth century landhold-
ing there had become concentrated in fewer, more exploitative, hands,
causing rents to rise whilst agricultural prices remained depressed, and
to varying degrees this class conﬂict pertained elsewhere in Spain.22
Clemente thus turned Marxist social history against its ‘correct’ con-
stituency, representing Carlism as a ‘social dissidence’ springing from
a form of ‘feudal socialism’ under attack from the centralising and
capitalist drive of Spain’s modernising Liberals;23 a neo-Carlist interpre-
tation that was expanded upon by other scholars equally exasperated
by Spain’s oligarchical liberalism.24 But Clemente’s ire targeted not the
Marxists but the traditionalists who had hijacked the true historical
