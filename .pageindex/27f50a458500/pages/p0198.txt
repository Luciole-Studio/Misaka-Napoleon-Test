   This grassroots discontent could not fail to play out on the battle-
ﬁeld. The Madrid government, thanks to growing Carlist defection to
the Cristinos and to the cross-border intelligence networks of Spymaster
Aviraneta, was keenly aware of the divisions in Carlist ranks and sought
to exploit them. José Antonio de Muñagorri, a native of Verástegui
(Alava), began the war as the backbone of Carlist war-related industry,
owning six factories in Guipúzcoa and Navarra. He has been repre-
sented as a treacherous buffoon in traditionalist scholarship.132 After
his repeated petitions to the Carlist court for a compromise peace fell
on deaf ears, he emigrated to the French side of the Pyrenees where
he contacted Cristino agents and forged a plan to raise a guerrilla
force of Spanish Basques. By November 1837, Muñagorri had become
a salaried Cristino ofﬁcer, and on 18 April 1838 he raised his standard in
Verástegui calling for peace and fueros (paz y fueros), designed to divide
the fuerista Basques against the transaccionista Castilians. Muñagorri’s
gambit reﬂected the growing willingness of both many fueristas and
many disenchanted absolutists (who would soon be captained by
Maroto) to forge a compromise peace sacriﬁcing Don Carlos in return for
Basque autonomy.133 In fact, Muñagorri’s military operations proved to
be tragicomic, and Cristino generals were so vexed by the desertion his
‘free corps’ attracted from conscripts that they refused to cooperate with
him, and his whole force was interned in France the following year.134
   The sudden Cristino rediscovery of the Basque fueros was as much
for its own security as for the division of the enemy. On 27 May 1838,
the Cristino free corps in Pamplona rose in rebellion, killing General
Sarsﬁeld and Colonel Mendívil, and occupying the city for more than a
month before Espartero’s paciﬁcation action.135 The motives for this ris-
ing betrayed the divided loyalties of Basques serving the Cristino cause:
for its leaders, the Peninsular War guerrilla veteran, Colonel León Iriarte,
and Comandante Pablo Barricart, were motivated by the anti-foralismo
of Madrid, and proclaimed the independence of Navarra.136 But the
fueros were still peripheral to Cristino strategy, whereas for the Carlists,
except in Aragón and the Catalan far west, they were part of its termi-
nal crisis. An unlikely understanding of two Ayacucho veterans, now
opposing one another as rival commanders-in-chief, would soon bring
the war to an end over the heads of crowned princes and even elected
governments.
