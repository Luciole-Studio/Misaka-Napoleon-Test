   The sum of Spanish historiography allows two relevant observations
to be made for this volume. First, it lays bare just how few histories of the
First Carlist War beyond travel and campaign narratives are available in
English. Although serious studies have been written, they have discussed
the First Carlist War only as part of either a wider narrative or a particu-
lar interest.32 The best English-language study is by John Coverdale, but
even this covers only one-third of the conﬂict’s span and, more impor-
tantly for this book’s rationale, explains the Carlist side much more than
the Cristino.33
   Second, twentieth-century research into the First Carlist War has
been more interested in the Carlist losers than the Cristino victors.
Nineteenth-century writers often referred to this conﬂict as the ‘seven
years’ war’, arguably a better title as it removes Carlist ownership of
the conﬂict and instead obliges scholars to understand the war’s impact
on the Cristino side. Carlism was both Europe’s most enduring legit-
imism and, paradoxically, a creed of losers, even when it was part of
the winning side in 1939. As a corollary to this, Spain’s Liberal revo-
lution, which was deﬁnitively victorious as a consequence of the First
Carlist War, has been seen as the normal trajectory of Spanish history.
This attitude was internalised by the school of Marxist social history
pioneered in Spain in the 1950s by Miguel Artola-Gallego, which was
morally driven by the need to historicise the Left’s defeat in the recent
Civil War, in other words, to explain the defeat of the ‘normal’ trajec-
tory of history. To this end, these social historians footnoted the military
history of the Cristino side to what they saw as the far more impor-
