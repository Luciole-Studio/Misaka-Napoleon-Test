create a revolutionary dictatorship in the Cristino zone.
   Mendizábal’s most important decree was to conscript 100,000 men
for Córdova’s Army of the North. Three separate rounds of conscrip-
tion, each for 25,000 men, had been enacted since the start of the war,
but Mendizábal’s measure augured the mass militarisation of society.
The 100,000 quinta made all males aged between 18 and 40 liable to
serve, the only free exemptions being disability, unigeniture, ordina-
tion and veteran status. Alternatively, draftees could pay 4,000 reales,
or 1,000 reales and a viable horse, in order to avoid service.134 The net
‘blood tax’ collected by the government was some 25 million reales and
700 horses; this income was in the spirit of Mendizábal’s demand for
‘abundant men and money to end the war soon’.135 But the sums col-
lected made little impression on an annual state deﬁcit of 30 million
reales, which was made acute both by the lapsed customs revenues dur-
ing the revolution (some £500,000 worth of British imports had entered
Spain that summer without suffering any duties) and the expanding
army budget, which had reached some 600 million reales annually.136
Only Mendizábal’s long-standing contacts with the Paris and London
bourses, combined with the structural reforms of the desamortización,
kept the Cristino state liquid, ensuring terms of credit which no other
Spanish prime minister could have achieved.137
