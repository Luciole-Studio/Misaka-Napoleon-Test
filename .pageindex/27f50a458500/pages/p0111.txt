ship now secure, Mendizábal set out to channel the anti-clericalism of
the revolutionaries to politically useful ends. Already, on 7 July, the
Toreno administration had expelled the Jesuit order for the second
time in Spanish history, conﬁscating its assets to reduce the balloon-
ing National Debt. This legislative anti-clericalism opened two years of
government onslaught on corporate Catholicism which would be scaled
back, but not halted, only when the moderados returned to power in
1837. Mendizábal expanded this anti-clericalism by suppressing all reli-
gious houses containing fewer than 12 inmates and auctioning their
assets. Citizens who had bought Church property during the Triennium,
only to see it conﬁscated in the 1823 counter-revolution, were reunited
with their purchases courtesy of a July 1837 decree.124 The tithe, along
with an extra clerical tax levied in Galicia called the Voto de Santiago,
had long been unpopular, but their abolition became politically impossi-
ble to resist as Carlism spread during 1835.125 These were both abolished
and replaced from 1837 by a smaller tax (culto y clero) collected by the
constitutional municipalities in order to pension off monks and nuns
evicted by the disentailment.126
   Mendizábal did not expect formal French or British intervention and
so pledged to use nationalised property to fund a decisive campaign in
the Basque country.127 The degree to which he channelled revolutionary
anti-Carlism was remarkable. His Liberal union saw an initial ‘patriotic’
ﬂurry of donations to the Cristino war effort: ministers volunteered cuts
to their salaries, ladies’ charity circles made clothing for the front and
Liberal students answered the call to enlist.128 Despite the paternalis-
tic opposition of the moderados, enlistment to the militia was expanded
by removing the requirement that unmarried recruits obtain permis-
sion from their heads of family.129 A freer intellectual environment grew
in proportion to the mendizabalista assault on corporate Catholicism.
