absolutists were ﬁned, imprisoned, expelled, even killed. Espoz y Mina,

ror against royalist villagers in the north, destroying the Catalan town
of Castellfullit.59
   If the constitutional army had managed, by the end of 1822, to
crush royalist bands, whether it would stand up to Congress Europe was
another matter. In October 1822, the Great Powers met at Verona to dis-
cuss intervention against Jacobinism in Spain. In the same month the
San Miguel administration formally militarised Liberal society by impos-
ing martial law on the National Militia and calling up some 67,000
men into the army.60 For a generation afterwards the men of 1812
remembered these actions as proof that the exaltados were the ‘real
absolutists’.61 But foreign diplomatic pressure only increased in response
to these measures, which emboldened the timid King to sabotage the
work of his Radical ministers. In February 1823 Ferdinand, seeing that
San Miguel had split the exaltados, exercised one of his remaining pre-
rogatives by dismissing him, surviving the inevitable mob invading the
palace shouting ‘Death to the king!’62 The Radicals retaliated by derogat-
ing the King’s remaining powers, pending the ‘recovery of his health’.
