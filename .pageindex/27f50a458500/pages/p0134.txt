women in Gandesa who were related to the besieging Carlists. Gandesa’s
heroism was thus overshadowed by a mundane cycle of reprisals.2
   The war in the east was Cabrera’s war: on 11 April Eguía promoted him
to brigadier, and on 15 August 1836 to major-general.3 On 29 March
1836 Cabrera took Liria, just four hours from Valencia and a wealthy
town in its own right. Some 20 militiamen were killed in the ﬁelds and
streets. Twenty-seven more were captured and led to Chiva where they
were slowly murdered by a gruesome ritual, which included being shot
in the kneecaps at point-blank range before having their genitals sliced
off.4 Castration had been a common ritual in the French Wars of Reli-
gion for emasculating the enemy, feminising bodies in death which in
life had not been ‘truly masculine’.5 Carlists saw their enemy as emascu-
lated by the double usurpation of an illegitimate and female candidate
on the throne. The wives and daughters of the Chiva victims, for their
part, did not submit to the domestic grief traditionally prescribed to
their sex. Rather, they joined militiamen on campaign seeking to exact
vengeance on the perpetrators. Palarea overwhelmed Cabrera’s rear-
guard near Chiva and committed a revenge massacre against captured
Carlists.6 But Torner soon counter-attacked, dispersing Palarea’s men.
Women avenged their men not only by killing Carlists, but also incom-
petent or cowardly ofﬁcers on the Cristino side. When some militiamen
were killed in September 1836 in a skirmish near Alcaraz (Albacete) with
the Carlist irregular, Orejita, their widows went to the Militia captain’s
house and, ﬁnding him absent, burnt it down, killing his wife who was
inside.7
