As one historian put it with perspicacity, ‘Men who did not want to serve
were led by men who did not know how to serve.’39 And as the Riego
revolution had burdened the state with an excess of political ofﬁcers,
these potentially dangerous men had to be bought off with appoint-
ments which the Constitution had designed for civilians. Much of the
blame for this situation should be laid at the polarisation of Liberal pol-
itics. Certainly, Spanish liberalism was steeped in generational conﬂict,
young Radicals resenting the stranglehold of old conservatives. The men
of 1820 grew so embittered at the older Liberal generation’s stranglehold
on public ofﬁces that they forged pseudo-Republican Comunero clubs
which promoted increasingly violent attacks on the Church and monar-
chy in a bid to win power. The Left-Liberal challenge to the men of 1812
(moderados) in turn divided as the 1820 generation was caught between
the rock of moderado and court disapproval and the hard place of Radical
pressure.40 Anguished by the collapse in April 1821 of a sister revolution
in Italy, the Comuneros launched a press campaign to uncover absolutist
sympathisers and rebellious priests.41 Rightly fearing the growing hos-
tility of the Holy Alliance, the Comuneros also tried to safeguard the
Spanish revolution by internationalising it. They welcomed Italian émi-
grés (the carbonari) into their lodges and militia battalions, whilst one
Cortes deputy, Zorraquín, ‘the foremost revolutionary in Spain’, estab-
lished correspondence clubs in Gerona and Catalan frontier towns with
the aim of enlisting public support for Liberal Spain in the southern
départments of France.42 When, during September 1821, Riego compro-
mised himself in a plot forged by a group of Spanish and international
Republicans, the moderate-Liberal (moderado) government dismissed
him from the Captaincy-General of Aragón.43
   Meanwhile, government attempts to remedy state ﬁnances fell hard-
est on the poor. The unpopular consumption taxes (derechos de puertas)
were re-established. Even though the Argüelles law began evicting
monks and nuns and bringing their property to market, it was the
highest bidder, not the landless labourer, sharecropper, tenant farmer
or demobilised soldier who got possession of the nationalised property.
The rural masses might yet have been appeased by the early 1821 Cortes
decree that tithes for the parish clergy would henceforth be halved.
But the half-tithe was accompanied by other taxes payable in cash, a
massive burden for a rural population still largely unaccustomed to a
cash economy, which, most devastatingly, came at a time of slump in
prices for agricultural goods.44 In fact, at least some foreign observers
