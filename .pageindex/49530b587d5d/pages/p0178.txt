brought about a severe drop in the number of arrivals, ﬁrst in
Leghorn, then in Marseilles. Between 1805 and 1810, thirty vessels
a year were recorded for Marseilles, and only twenty for Leghorn;
in the two years from 1811 to 1813, traﬃc was almost non-existent
in the two ports, which received fewer than ten ships a year. A spec-
tacular contrast is provided by the dramatic increase for Malta, whose
traﬃc doubled between 1806 and 1812, reaching almost 200 ships
in that year. During the Napoleonic Wars, Malta had become a
highly active port, with a double function: it was ﬁrst of all the prin-
cipal English naval base in the Mediterranean, where the numerous
warships blockading the coasts of the French Empire and pursuing
its corsairs put in for supplies and repairs; it had also become the
largest entrepôt port for the redistribution of merchandise in the
Mediterranean. The restoration of peace in 1814, however, led to a
