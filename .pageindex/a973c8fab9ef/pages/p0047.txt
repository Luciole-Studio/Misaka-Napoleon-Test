Schroeder’s comment about French hegemony, which he repeats elsewhere (Transformation of European Politics, 229), appears to violate another central proposition of balance of power theory – that hegemonies do not emerge from multistate systems. The proposition is better framed in terms of sustained hegemony, which has not arisen in Europe for well over a half-millennium. It is also important to note that although the identification of balancing failures within wars is theoretically important, any more systematic assessment of the relative frequency of balancing must not restrict the analysis to wars. One must also investigate the occurrence of balancing during peacetime, to include both the overt balancing that may have deterred wars, and the anticipation of balancing that deterred aggressive behavior. See Levy, “Balances and Balancing.” For a quantitative demonstration of systematic patterns of balancing against high concentrations of power in Europe over the last five centuries, but not against comparable concentrations of power in the global system, see. Levy and William R. Thompson, “Balancing on Land and at Sea: Do States Ally Against the Leading Global Power?” International Security 35:1 (Summer 2010): 7-43.
