modities of the department’s trade and their value in 1805.
Fargés-Meéricourt alas gives no specific estimate of the out-
going trade in wines, other agricultural products, and indus-

favourable. Exports and re-exports must then have exceeded
the sum of 21,277,500 francs, which is his figure for total
imports that year.!!? Of this traffic, the largest shares were
made up of meat (4.5 million); horses and livestock (2.5
million); groceries and drysaltery (2.8 million); wool and
woollen       goods    (2.2   million);      linens,   muslins,   silkstuffs
(together 1.6 million); firewood (1.5 million); wines for

gether 1.2 million); and salt (4 million kg worth 1,117,500
francs). The remaining items (grain, tanned goods, glass- and
crystalware, mirrors, ornamental ceramics, German hops)
varied in value from 450,000 to 200,000 francs. The implica-
tion of all this is that if the Bas-Rhin could pay for its in-
coming trade to that extent and still register a positive
balance, its export and re-export trade in 1805 must have
been far from ‘decadent’. Against this background, the’
Chamber’s gloomy reports and anxious petitions appear as a
deliberate public relations exercise, or orchestrated variations
on the theme of doomsday.
