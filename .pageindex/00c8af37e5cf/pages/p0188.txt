of Levantine cotton show an even sharper rise. The estimated
18,000 metric quintals for the first half of 1809 already
represented twice the earlier total for the whole of 1808.
Fernand L’Huillier, presumably by doubling this half-yearly
return, concludes that Strasbourg’s trade in Levantine cotton
was four times greater in 1809 than in 1808.5% He may well
be right. Apart from the Chamber’s evidence of stockpiling
before the war with Austria, the primary sources copiously
confirm that the progress of the imperial cotton industries
during the expansionary period after Tilsit depended on raw
materials from the Levant. This was especially so after the
rupture in Franco-American relations and the troubles in the
Iberian peninsula during 1808, which together undermined
the supply of trans-Atlantic cotton. Montalivet felt able to
inform Napoleon on 2 July 1810 that ‘le fonds de notre
fabrication roule sur les cotons du levant’.*4
   While Strasbourg remained the principal imperial inlet
for Levantine cotton until the government enforced a switch
of route   in 1811,     this trade did not follow an even course.
