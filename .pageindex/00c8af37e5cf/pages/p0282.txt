    The official statistics for the Haut-Rhin’s industrial
 showing in these years give at least a crude guide to the scale
 of the recovery. L’Huillier’s figures,!?4 extrapolated no doubt
 with some headaches from the jumble of prefectoral tables,
 show good headway in cotton spinning. From 26,332 active
 spindles during the first half of 1811 the departmental total
 rose to 47,908 by the end of that year, and then remained
 fairly steady at this level during 1812. The output of yarn
 over the same period increased from a half-yearly average of
 just over 54,000 to nearly 80,000 kg. In the cotton-weaving
 sector, the number of active installations rose slightly from
 3,643 to 3,843 during the first quarter of 1812, but there-
 after it did not apparently reach its level of 4,500 before the
 crisis. In Mulhouse itself there may even have been a slight
 decline: at the beginning of 1812 the number of active
 weaving installations was 995, compared with 1,220 during
  the first half of 1811.'75 In the key sector of calico-printing,
 however, the departmental totals for employment and pro-
