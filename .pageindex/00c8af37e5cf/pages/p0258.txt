 Here, then, the obvious inference was that a growing rather
 than a stagnant trade was being threatened and that this was
 why the measure of 5 September was so disliked. Certainly,
 a branch of Strasbourg’s export trade which had almost
 sextupled between unspecified dates hardly conforms to the
 forlorn image of a lost prosperity. Perhaps an allusion to the
 good times of 1807-10 was intended, to stress the current
 set-backs by rhetorical contrast? If so, the Chamber was in
 effect offering a nuance of the ‘golden age conceit’, and the
 reaction in Paris was accordingly sceptical.
    Finally, the Chamber’s own statistical record of the city’s
 trade during the crisis was internally inconsistent with the
 burden of its routine reports at the time. One may reasonably
 suppose, though without being able to quantify the point or
 cite chapter and verse in support of it, that illicit trade was to
 some extent restrained by the ‘terreur douaniére’ after
 October 1810. At any rate until the autumn of 1812, its
 risks and penalties were greater than during the expansionary
 period. But as the Chamber was understandably anxious to
 keep a respectable public distance from the ups and downs of
 the     contraband       and   fraudulent     trade, its statistical records
 applied only to regular traffic. And as for that, if there was
 a sharp decline in the city’s river trade, which would seem to
 follow from the descriptive record of the reports between
 September 1810 and September 1811, then there are dis-
 crepancies with the Chamber’s figures compiled towards the
 end of 1812.57 These show a total down-river trade which in
 1811 was actually — nay, miraculously! — greater than that
 for 1810.°* The monthly fluctuations are often quite sharp
