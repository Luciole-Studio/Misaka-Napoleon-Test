254         CRISIS, RECOVERY,   AND DECLINE   1810-1813

The general trend was one of variable advance rather than of
any new expansionary momentum. Mechanization progressed
in the larger enterprises, but 1813 was on the whole a year of
set-backs    for the manufacturers.   As   in the case    of trade,
there was a good deal of variation between and indeed within
the different industrial branches. In the cotton industry, for
example, Jean Malapert was something of a special case. A
marked reallocation of resources occurred in his workshops.
The number of active spindles remained fairly stable at about
the crisis figure of 8,000, and yet his employment rose from
340 persons in 1811 to 550 in 1812, before dropping to 420
early in 1813. Since his production of yarn declined steadily
from even the reduced level of the crisis, the new hands must
have been employed to boost his output of muslins and per-
cales, so that the enterprise clearly changed in character after
1810. Engelhardt-Kern and Sandherr & Redslob were appar-
ently unable to override the effects of crisis by similarly
diversifying production. There was no sustained recovery in
either case, though the second half of 1812 saw a slight
improvement in output. Louis Roederer had a struggle to
maintain his employment level at between 145 and 160 and
his production    of percales, muslins, and calicoes at between
27,000 and 31,000 articles per quarter during 1812. Both
figures fell sharply in the first half of 1813, and in the second
the firm collapsed.
   In woollens, it is clear from the official reports that J.-G.
Dietsch never recovered his expansionary momentum of
1807-10. His output of spun wool, cloth, jerseys, and
blankets was lower in 1812 than during the year of crisis
preceding it. It appears that he was hit by the centralization
of military purchases in Paris before and during the Russian
campaign. Conversely, when his production made an out-of-
phase improvement in 1813, military orders were again the
major stimulus. The linen industries fared no better on the
whole. The figures for Vetter and Joseph Bernard in Stras-
bourg show a steady decline from the crisis through to the
summer of 1813. Vetter’s troubles were reportedly due to
the loss of foreign markets, notably during the hostilities
of 1812 and 1813. Brackenhoffer’s tables suggest that the
