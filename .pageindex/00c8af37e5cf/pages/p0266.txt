246           CRISIS, RECOVERY,        AND DECLINE        1810-1813

   Over all, when compared with the quarterly returns for
1810, the statistical record of the Haut-Rhin’s industrial losses
averages out somewhere between 20 and 30 per cent. Yet
both output and employment varied considerably between
industrial branches as well as between particular firms. In the
cotton industry, for example, weaving and printing were
worse off than spinning. The retrospective official returns
even show a modest improvement in the latter.”* Whereas the
number of active spindles in Mulhouse itself remained more
or less stable at around 1,400 during 1810 and 1811 alike,
the department as a whole saw an increase from 22,280 in
the first half of 1810 to 24,064 in the second, and then again
a more impressive jump from 26,332 to 44,168 during the
two halves of 1811. After ministerial queries, however, it
became apparent during the following months that the
returns for 1810 had been incomplete, and so the actual trend
during 1811 is left in doubt.
   In any case spinning was the exception, 7f it was an excep-
tion, which proved the rule. According to the same
retrospective account,’”? during the first half of 1811 the
number        of active weaving installations             (métiérs a tisser)
in the Haut-Rhin fell by at least a fifth to 3,653, while in
Mulhouse the drop to 1,220 installations was nearer a
quarter. In the branches of the department’s cotton industry
taken altogether, the total number of articles produced
during 1810 had been 332,690 in the first and 330,632 in the
second half-year; during the first of 1811, when the crisis was
at its peak, the figure slumped to 240,753, a loss of some-
thing more than 27 per cent, before a partial recovery to
277,455 during the second half-year. As most of the articles
referred to were printed calicoes, it is clear that the industrial
crisis had indeed been most conspicuous in the department’s
most dynamic and prosperous industrial branch. Within it,
