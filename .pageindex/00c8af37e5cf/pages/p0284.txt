                              CONCLUSION


A traditional lopsidedness in the historiography of Napoleon’s
Continental Blockade has left us with a no less unbalanced
view of its aims and effects. Its ‘sea aspects’ remain more
prominent than its ‘land aspects’ in the literature on the
subject, and this in turn has influenced the most familiar
interpretation      of the Blockade’s    importance.     Most   earlier
writers, though      indeed   not all, have concluded    that its aims
were based on an outmoded and impracticable economic
dogma and that its results were largely unsuccessful, and even
self-destructive,    for this reason.   Our   verdict   on   the whole
episode, it thus appears, ought to be suitably negative.   _
   Yet a closer study of many inland parts of the Napoleonic
Empire, and even of some of the allied states on the Con-
tinental mainland, puts the full impact of the Blockade in a
rather different light. Since the great mass of the Continental
population did not inhabit the beleaguered maritime ports
of that time, and did not therefore experience the Blockade
directly through the disruption of sea-borne trade, it seems
necessary to broaden the focus so as to include inland areas
whose traditional economic orbits were differently orientated.
This broader view reveals in fact that some of the Blockade’s
alms were more positive and constructive, and some of its
effects more beneficial, than would appear from the generally
dismal record of the maritime regions under its regime.
The Blockade should not then be construed as an unqualified
failure throughout the lands subjected to Napoleonic rule.
Even where its impact was certainly harmful, it was only
the climacteric of a wider economic dislocation whose
origins lay in the maritime wars of the Revolutionary conflict.
