238 Experiences of War and Nationality in Denmark and Norway, 1807–1815


   Attempts to impress the Swedes with a daunting vision of what they
could expect of a war in Norway had, in fact, begun several months
previously when Swedes had been warned that the Norwegians would
not ‘be subjugated’ and ‘that if their opponents allowed the situation
to go to extremes, they would do so at their peril’. Furthermore, allied
mediators who had been sent to Christiania in July warned Charles
John that in the event of an invasion and war, the Swedes could expect
to find ‘a courageous people’ who would defend themselves in rug-
ged terrain, and create great difficulties for the Swedish army. In his
detailed instructions to military commanders on how to deploy the
peasant militiamen, Christian Frederick went so far as to suggest they
should wage guerrilla war, word of which he obviously hoped would
reach the Swedes and deter them.67
   Christian Frederick was well aware of what had occurred elsewhere
in Europe in previous years and made remarks about the unwinnable
‘national’ wars in Germany and Spain. If Charles John drew a similar
conclusion, it was possible that the prospect of such a war in Norway
would help bring about a negotiated solution and increase the chances
of Norwegian autonomy. So, if popular mobilisation could be called ‘a
pet project’ for Christian Frederick, as one historian has it, it was due to
its potential as a political instrument, not because Christian Frederick
seriously intended to wage a savage guerrilla war.68
   The extent to which arming the Norwegian population in the summer
of 1814 was a source of concern for the Swedes is debatable, although
several historians have argued that there was considerable concern
that an invasion of Norway would meet fierce resistance.69 A lieuten-
ant wrote in his memoirs that, when he and his troops took part in the
invasion, they had braced themselves for ‘the second act of the Franco–
Spanish war because the Norwegians threatened to put up desperate
resistance, and the hatred of the Swedes in Norway was well known to
us’. Many expressed similar views and compared the situation to the
Vendée and Calabria. One observer remarked on the eve of the invasion
that ‘Hatred in Norway is greater than anyone can believe, even among
women and children.’70
