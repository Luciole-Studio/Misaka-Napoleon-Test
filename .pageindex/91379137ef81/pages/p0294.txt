  However, all of this was kept secret at the time, and the Second World
War dramatically improved Danish–Norwegian relations. While Danes
got through the occupation fairly lightly, the Norwegians suffered
greatly. In Copenhagen, the old capital of Norway, people began to send
emergency supplies to children north of the Skagerrak during the war.
This turned into the single greatest relief effort in Danish history. In
return, after the war, Norwegians took the initiative to establish the
Danish–Norwegian Cooperation Fund, which still works to maintain
close ties between the two countries. This fraternisation and the Nordic
cooperation that took place after 1945, however, was weakened when
Denmark and Norway chose different directions in 1972 as regards the
European Union. The ties that linked Denmark and Norway, once so
close, are slowly being forgotten. Some Danes today are not even aware
that Denmark and Norway were united for more than 400 years, and
neither north nor south of the Skagerrak are there many people who
know anything about the period of cultural fellowship that flowered
after the separation. None of this changes the fact that the histories of
Denmark and Norway are inextricably intertwined and have a common
story to which their ‘two’ languages can bear witness.
