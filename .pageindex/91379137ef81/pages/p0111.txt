   For the count, however, this realisation only dawned gradually.76
Initially he thought along state and national patriotic lines, and his
energy prompted him to place himself at the head of a volunteer corps
upon the outbreak of hostilities with Sweden in 1808.77 In no small part
as a result of his patriotic efforts in the war, he was given a seat on the
Government Commission in January 1809, which confirmed his posi-
tion as one of the leading men in Norway.78
   Following the Swedish revolution in March 1809, Count Wedel Jarlsberg
had wanted a constitutional Scandinavian union under Frederick VI,
which he wished to use as a lever to end Frederick’s autocratic rule in
Denmark and Norway. As this could not be realised because of the king’s
intractable attitude to the constitutional issue, he turned instead to the
idea of a constitutional union of Sweden and Norway, as he felt that
this would give Norway far-reaching national autonomy and much bet-
ter economic conditions than Denmark had to offer. This idea, which
entered the count’s mind in the spring of 1809, was the point at which
Count Wedel Jarlsberg’s national patriotism met constitutionalism and
liberalism, which would soon turn his national patriotic sentiments in
a more radical direction.
   This was what underlay his activities in 1809 and was the reason why
he convened the meeting at his ironworks at Bærum in late August. As a
person who was actively involved in obtaining supplies for Norway and
as a member of the Government Commission, Wedel Jarlsberg was fully
aware of the critical position Norway was in. He therefore wanted peace
with Sweden and Great Britain in order to save Norway. On Norway’s
behalf, he had taken a leading role in the efforts to make Christian
August the successor to the Swedish throne in the spring and sum-
mer of 1809 through contacts and conspiracies with leading Swedish
circles. His reason for this was that Frederick VI’s reactions to the
Swedish revolution had convinced him that the interests of Norway and
Denmark were becoming increasingly incompatible, and that a united
Scandinavia under the rule of Frederick VI was not a desirable option
as it was unlikely to put absolutism to an end.79 Wedel Jarlsberg’s subse-

ing other than pure conspiracy aimed at removing Frederick VI from
the Norwegian throne. He secretly exchanged letters and held meet-
ings with Swedish revolutionaries, including the officers Adlersparre
