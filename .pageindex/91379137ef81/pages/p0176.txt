Resentment and Resistance in Holstein

It was not necessarily the case that all Holsteiners, as if by default, vehe-
mently opposed the annexation itself or even all of the reforms planned
or introduced. In 1801, the first Battle of Copenhagen had led many
Holsteiners to express patriotic pride in being ‘Dänen’ and, moreover,
there were Holsteiners of a rational bent who could well greet many
of the reforms because they strengthened the state across ethnic divi-
sions, improved its efficiency and, crucially, thus made it more cap-
able of resistance to Napoleon and Prussia. Moreover, many members
of the Holstein elite – not least those who occupied top official posts in
Copenhagen – were bilingual and had no difficulty in using Danish as
the official language of administration, although their everyday social
language was German. The introduction of Danish as the language of
administration therefore had little effect on them, while the ordinance
that would have introduced Danish in all churches, schools and law
courts in those parts of the duchies where Danish was already spo-
ken by most of the populace was never put into practice at any rate.40
Therefore, not too much should be read into the role of the annexation
and the proposed linguistic reforms themselves for the development of
anti-Danish sentiments.
   Above all, anti-Danish reactions reflected what could be called a
widespread Landespatriotismus in the duchies, which stopped short of
German nationalism and was not incompatible with other identities
and patriotisms.41 The dual notion of fatherland in the duchies, where
Holstein was regarded as the natural fatherland and Denmark as the
civic fatherland, survived the Napoleonic wars, even if affection for the
Danish state cooled considerably.
   As far as a German identity went, the people of Holstein had increas-
ingly begun to emphasise their German origin in the latter half of the
eighteenth century, which to some extent coincided with a measure of
Reichspatriotismus (imperial patriotism associated with the Holy Roman
Empire). In some ways, Landespatriotismus and Reichspatriotismus were
mutually reinforcing in Holstein, much in the same way as in the
Rhineland.42 Regional rights and the Holy Roman Empire, until its dis-
solution in 1806, protected the existing order in Holstein and served
as guarantees against being incorporated into the absolute Oldenburg
state – or for that matter any other major states. Moreover, German iden-
tity in Holstein was also tied to the German language. Many Holsteiners
