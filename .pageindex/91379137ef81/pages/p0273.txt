War and Separation

During the last two decades there has been a growing tendency among
international scholars to paint a more variegated picture of the national,
political and social upheavals and of developments that can be traced
back to the Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars. The historian Michael
Broers speaks of a ‘New Napoleonic History’ in which Napoleon’s Empire
is the focal point of scholarly attention that has provided new insights not
only into the nature and effects of Napoleon’s imperialism but also into
how Europeans reacted to the empire. This has constituted a fundamental
challenge to a wide range of traditional historical interpretations and has
exploded a number of myths. For example, neither Germany’s Wars of
Liberation against France in 1813–15 nor the hostilities in areas such as
Spain and Italy are any longer considered broad national uprisings.1
   When it comes to the population at large, the ‘New Napoleonic History’
has thus shouldered a substantial degree of revisionism in which Norway
and Denmark should now be included. Neither in 1808 nor in 1814 had
the Norwegian population fought or been willing to fight a people’s
war, any more than had Danish peasants during the British attack on
Zealand and Copenhagen in 1807. In other words, the conceptual world
of the peasantry was still solidly and conservatively rooted in tradi-
tionalism. However, the picture is somewhat different if the spotlight
is turned on the elites of the Danish-Norwegian state. What becomes
visible here is not only a highly developed ideology of state patriotism,
but also clearly articulated and distinct ideas of national patriotism that
border on modern nationalism. In the case of Norway, the transition
from the former to the latter took place in 1814 in one of the most strik-
ing turning points in Scandinavian history.
