106   Experiences of War and Nationality in Denmark and Norway, 1807–1815


Crown Prince Frederick issued a privateering regulation as early as
14 September 1807, which, in a slightly revised form, was proclaimed in
Norway by Prince Christian August on 29 December. The privateering
regulation chiefly laid down guidelines for which ships it was permit-
ted to seize and how these ships and their cargos – known as prizes –
should be approved by prize courts, realised and the proceeds distrib-
uted between stakeholders, crews and the state.14
   This was of course controversial, but the British bombardment of
Copenhagen had lowered the moral threshold of many people. Initially,
it was well-to-do people, broadly speaking, who invested in privateers,
chiefly on patriotic grounds, but as rumours gradually spread of the
potentially huge profits privateering could bring, even less prosperous
citizens began to invest in such enterprises. Even Christian August,
who was not enthusiastic about privateering, had shares in a privateer,
although he managed his ownership with the greatest discretion. Yet,
he also felt that privateering posed a serious problem in relation to
Sweden, as he wished to avoid ‘provoking the common people in Sweden
and thereby uniting the parties [there] to our detriment’. Furthermore,
during the campaign in the spring of 1808 he had made it a point of
his propaganda that, unlike the Swedes, Norwegians did not engage in
looting. It scarcely fitted his bill, then, that Norwegian privateers were
extremely active along the west coast of Sweden, sometimes engaging
in outright looting focused on Swedish ports.15
   Perhaps above all, the Danish-Norwegian privateering campaign
showed how patriotic and economic interests could combine to encour-
age the participation of a large number of people, without there neces-
sarily being a hard-and-fast distinction between patriotic and economic
interests for the individuals involved. For example, the general in com-
mand of northern Norway, the arch-patriot Georg Frederick von Krogh,
had shares in a privateer and, as his private letters show, he was just as
interested in the economic advantages of privateering as he was in its
patriotic aspect.16
