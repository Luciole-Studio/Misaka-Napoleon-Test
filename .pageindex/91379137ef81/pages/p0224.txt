Postlude – Freedom and
Suppression, 1814–15




Peace in Scandinavia?

Frederick VI had lost and Charles John had won. On 16 January the
Danish king issued proclamations releasing the Norwegians from their
oath of allegiance and encouraging them to accept the new regime.
The trouble was that quite literally no one passed the news on to the
Norwegians. Although a banner headline in a Norwegian newspaper
shouted, ‘Peace, peace in Scandinavia’ on 25 January, there was no men-
tion of what the peace meant for Norway. This was partly due to the
fact that Christian Frederick was ignorant of the precise contents of the
peace treaty, as Frederick VI initially failed to send the prince those sec-
tions of the treaty dealing with the cession of Norway and the docu-
ment that released Norwegians from their oath to the king. This was
simply the king playing for time. Despite the anger this caused Christian
Frederick, it gave the prince some latitude. The heir to the Danish
throne attempted to calm his war-weary Norwegian people and buy
himself time. Rumours of the terms of the peace, however, spread rap-
idly and they were officially proclaimed a month later. Although many
Norwegians had anticipated the separation, the news came as a shock,
and matters were not improved by the fact that they had been surren-
dered to the Swedish arch-enemy like ‘a herd of cattle’ without a fight.1
