   For their part, King Frederick and the government in Copenhagen
energetically countered Swedish propaganda with pamphlets, texts
and proclamations of their own. Furthermore, a close watch was also
kept on suspicious individuals such as Count Wedel Jarlsberg.43 Yet, no
arrests were made in Norway on the grounds of alleged illegal relations
with Sweden, and peasants usually handed over Swedish propaganda
material distributed in the borderlands to the authorities. While this
shows that propaganda was certainly being circulated, it also suggests
that not even the peasants in the area – who had extensive cross-border
relations – were very receptive to what Sweden had to offer.
   This was not only a consequence of the prejudices about and sus-
picions of Sweden, but also of the policies of the government in
Copenhagen. In his desperation in April 1813, King Frederick feared
that Norwegian faith in the government – that even the union with
Denmark itself – was faltering and therefore decided to send his heir
apparent, Prince Christian Frederick, to Norway as governor to boost
Norwegian morale and loyalty. The king hoped that, as a prominent rep-
resentative of the monarchy and of the Oldenburg dynasty, Christian
Frederick would reinforce the traditionally deep-rooted dynastic loyalty
in Norway and create a counterweight to the Swedish propaganda cam-
paign. Prince Christian Frederick arrived in Norway on the morning of
21 May 1813.44 He immediately embarked on a tour of eastern Norway
to win the confidence of the middle-class elites and other notables, par-
ticularly those who had expressed pro-Swedish sentiments or criticised
Danish foreign policy. His aims included winning the unanimous sup-
port of the population and organising mass resistance to any offensive
Swedish moves, as well as preventing Charles John from turning the
population against Danish rule.
