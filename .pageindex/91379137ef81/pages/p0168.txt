Danish propaganda distributed in Scania contained expressions such as

Similar concepts and turns of phrase were used in Danish lobbying of
Swedish politicians and the Riksdag in 1810, and they also anticipated
many of the ideas that became current in Scandinavist movement later
in the nineteenth century.
   In addition to large numbers of Danish poets, writers and journalists,
this proto-Scandinavianism also attracted many of the key German-
speaking figures in or near Danish-Norwegian government circles who,
like Count Wedel Jarlsberg, may have been motivated by a desire for
political reform and the introduction of a constitution. They included
Prince Christian August and Finance Minister Ernst Schimmelmann,
who even wrote a number of propaganda pamphlets. While it is true that
a number of the arguments for proto-Scandinavianism, such as many of
those made by Schimmelmann, were employed in promoting Frederick
VI’s candidacy for the Swedish throne, several influential thinkers, such
as the Danish priest N.F.S. Grundtvig and not least Christian August,
were genuinely and openly enthusiastic about common Scandinavian
cultural features which, in their eyes, held the promise of future con-
cord, harmony and peace.29
   If only in the longer term, the kind of dynastic union envisaged in
both Danish-Norwegian and Swedish circles might have facilitated the
formation of a common and distinct Scandinavian identity that could
have proved stronger and more durable than the Scandinavianism that
would, at least in political terms, fail in the second half of the nine-
teenth century. This is not to say that an election of Christian Frederick,
for instance, as heir to the Swedish throne in 1810, or the survival of
Carl August after 1810 would have automatically led to a politically and
culturally united Scandinavia. But it seems probable that, had Christian
Frederick been elected or had Carl August not died prematurely,
Scandinavian history would have taken a completely different course.
However, as matters stood, a new war was the almost certain outcome
of the policies Charles John would pursue, causing intra-Scandinavian
enmity to flare up once again and putting Scandinavianism on hold for
another two decades.
