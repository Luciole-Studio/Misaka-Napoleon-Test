When Home Is a Foreign Country

Nationality in absolutist Europe was defined by country of birth, and
Denmark-Norway was no exception, as is made clear by the various
government circulars written at the Danish Ministry of the Interior to
civil servants in Denmark and Norway regarding the separation. All
Danish-born officials and officers in Norway were recalled to the coun-
try of their birth, while their Norwegian-born counterparts serving in
Denmark were obliged to state in writing within four weeks whether
they wished to resign or remain in Danish service. Officers and civil
servants were forced to choose between love of their native country,
loyalty to Frederick VI and their best career prospects.
   The problem was only aggravated by the fact that the absolutist ‘objec-
tive’ definition of nationality based on country of birth did not always
harmonise with the individual’s ‘subjective’ self-knowledge. One cate-
gory faced with a dilemma here was the civil servants and officers born
in Denmark of Norwegian parents, many of whom lived in Norway
in 1814 and regarded themselves as Norwegians. One example was the
Danish-born Norwegian, Captain Johan Ræder, who was preparing to
obey the king’s order and move to Denmark in the spring of 1814 to
avoid a dishonourable discharge but was persuaded to stay by Christian
Frederick, who reminded him of his duty to Norway.18
   The fluid transition between Danish and Norwegian was nowhere
better exemplified than in the case of the author Christiane Birgitte
Koren, who was unable to differentiate between the interests of
Denmark and Norway, and who could speak seamlessly of ‘we Danes’
and ‘we Norwegians’, without this involving any contradiction for her.
After the separation and defeat by Sweden, the Danish-born Norwegian
priest Frederick Schmidt remained divided between his two fatherlands
because, as he wrote, he was neither Danish nor Norwegian, but either
Danish-Norwegian or Norwegian-Danish. The separation remained an
open wound for him as well as for Koren, and their feelings were shared
to a greater or lesser degree by many Danes and Norwegians.19
