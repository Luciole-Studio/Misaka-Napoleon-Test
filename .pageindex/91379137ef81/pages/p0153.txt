   It is not surprising that resentment against Great Britain was deep and
long-lasting in Denmark and Norway, but here, too, there were differ-
ences of opinion within the state. The fury against Britain in Denmark
after the bombardment and the seizure of the fleet lived on, and the
British were still referred to as robbers and thieves several decades
after the Napoleonic Wars, the memory lasting well into the twentieth
century.86 The Norwegians, however, viewed these matters in a rather
different light. Even though Norway had been hard hit by the block-
ade, rather paradoxically, perhaps, they did not necessarily place all
the blame for their distress on the British. During the crisis years from
1812–13 there was a great deal of anger with the Swedes as contributors
to the blockade, and it could even be claimed that the traditional view
of Sweden as the arch-enemy was projected onto something for which
the British were primarily responsible. Swedish observers also noted
in 1813 and 1814 that it would be difficult to regain the friendship of
the Norwegians as a consequence of their bitterness against Sweden
because of the blockade. Furthermore, many Norwegian merchants had
long-lasting relations with Great Britain and realised that trade with
Britain was vital for their enterprises, so this could have reduced the
tendency to direct their rage exclusively against the British. Instead,
anger in these circles was increasingly directed against the govern-
ment in Copenhagen, which was held responsible for the long war with
Britain that many Norwegians felt was unnecessary and unwanted.
