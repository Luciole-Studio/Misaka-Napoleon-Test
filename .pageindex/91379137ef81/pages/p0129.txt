   In the summer of 1810 Napoleon allowed licensed trade with Great
Britain, which was regularised through his Saint-Cloud decree of
3 July. Licences were also issued for exports from the empire, something
that Napoleon regarded as doubly beneficial, as they helped to sell off
surpluses from the empire and drained British gold reserves, which
Napoleon believed was the chief means by which Britain attracted
allies.30 The Trianon decree of 5 October 1810 modified the Continental
Blockade even more by allowing imports of certain colonial goods
hitherto prohibited. In theory this even undermined the basic idea of
the blockade and may as such appear quite paradoxical. This paradox
derives from the combination of Napoleon’s fiscal need and wish to line
French coffers as well as his wish to tighten customs surveillance in the
empire. Yet, while the Trianon decree allowed for certain colonial goods
to be imported, the subsequent Fontainebleau decree of 18 October did
in fact toughen the blockade against British manufactured goods and
was followed by a significant expansion of what has been called the
‘customs terror’.31
   ‘France first’, as Napoleon remarked in August 1810, applied to the
Continental Blockade as a whole. Although primarily aimed to strike
Britain, it also promoted sales of French industrial goods for the benefit
of France proper and what Michael Broers has called the ‘inner empire’,
that is, most of the areas annexed before 1807. There was a significant
economic upswing in many of these areas following their annexation
to the empire, in no small part as a result of their access to the French
domestic market which, in accordance with Napoleon’s essentially mer-
cantilist economic policy, was all but closed to the ‘outer’ empire and
his allies. This was why the Trianon and Fontainebleau decrees and
Napoleon’s annexation of most of north Germany in 1810–11 would in
the end cause great problems for Denmark-Norway and the duchies.32
   Spoils from smuggling had kept the economy in Schleswig and
Holstein going after the outbreak of war in 1807, a situation in many
ways similar to that of the Rhineland where smuggling across the Rhine
flourished until Napoleon tightened up controls of the river in 1810. Yet,
despite keeping local economies going, smuggling also had its disadvan-
tages for the Danish-Norwegian state as it deprived the government of
tax revenues, while the networks of smugglers, draft dodgers and crimi-
nals hiding and operating in the marshlands of northwestern Germany,
Frisia and Schleswig-Holstein also increasingly contributed to the break-
down of morale and social stability in these areas as crime rates soared.33
