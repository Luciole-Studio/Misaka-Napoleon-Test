Scandinavian War, 1808–09




From Copenhagen to the River Kymme

The alliance of its arch enemy, Sweden, with Great Britain aroused the
suspicion of the Danish-Norwegian government that the Swedes had in
some way played a part in the attack on Copenhagen, or intended to
exploit it to their own advantage. Therefore, almost as a knee-jerk reac-
tion, it turned its attention towards Sweden and from the end of August
kept close watch on the Swedish–Norwegian border, while every move-
ment on the Swedish side of it was closely monitored. King Gustav IV
Adolph’s amicable reception of Cathcart and Gambier in Helsingborg
when the British fleet left Zealand on 20 October – with the Danish-
Norwegian fleet in tow – aroused vigilance and not least anger on the
other side of the Sound.
   There was arguably some justification for Danish-Norwegian suspi-
cions, at least as long as Swedish participation in the occupation of
Zealand had been under discussion. Gustav IV Adolph refused this,
however. Few of his central political and military advisers had much
faith in the British, not least since they had recently abandoned Sweden
in Pomerania. The king himself was concerned that agreeing to the
British offer would provoke Danish-Norwegian and Russian attacks. He
was painfully aware that the Peace of Tilsit had left Sweden in a very
precarious position and he had decided to keep a low profile for the
time being. Gustav IV Adolph’s policy in the autumn of 1807 was to
maintain a neutral position without severing ties with Britain.1
