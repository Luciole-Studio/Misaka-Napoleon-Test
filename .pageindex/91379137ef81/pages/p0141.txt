   The Danish-Norwegian state was a market in which the individual
areas played different roles and had a number of direct or indirect
monopolies on supplies to certain areas. The hub of this system was the
joint capital, Copenhagen, which enjoyed special privileges. This made
Norway – and for that matter more remote parts of Denmark proper such
as Jutland – peripheral, and Norway could unquestionably be described
in many ways as a kind of internal colony within the state, though not
necessarily any different to other, equally peripheral parts of the state.57
   Some historians, however, particularly Norwegian historians, have
viewed Danish–Norwegian relations in this respect as an expression of
Danish colonialism, but it would appear to be more appropriate to view
the policy of the government from a state perspective, which, it is true,
was very often to the advantage of the centre in Copenhagen. But the
war cut off Norway from this centre, and that enabled the Government
Commission and the Norwegian middle-class elite to act more inde-
pendently and promote a policy that would lead to greater independ-
ence for the nation with regard to its economy and food supplies. While
there were still Norwegians and people of Danish origin in Norway who
felt that there should be economic independence within the framework
of the state, other Norwegians increasingly thought of economic inde-
pendence in purely national terms.
   This is the perspective from which the Society for the Welfare of
Norway, founded at the end of 1809, should be viewed, since many of its
efforts involved turning Norway into a self-sufficient economic entity
based on cameralistic ideas.58 In other words, the society was an expres-
sion of the idea of national autonomy, unity and identity, linked to a
Norwegian homeland. Even though it was most strongly represented in
the eastern part of southern Norway, in practice it was nationwide and
was not far removed from nationalism in a political sense.
   Patriotic societies emerged in the duchies of Schleswig and Holstein,
too. The most well known was the Patriotische Gesellschaft established
in Altona in 1812, which, somewhat contrary to the main currents and
moods in Holstein at the time, worked to strengthen relations with
Denmark proper by supporting the crown and the state rather than
looking to the allies or to the idea of a united Germany.59 In essence, it
based its thinking on Enlightenment rationalism, which led to a cosmo-
politan stance that was necessarily devoid of the national sentiments
that increasingly began to influence other circles in the duchies, includ-
ing the Society for the Welfare of Norway.
