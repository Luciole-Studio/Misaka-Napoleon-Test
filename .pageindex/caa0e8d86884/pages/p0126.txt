114   Caring for the Nation’s Families


 1. British Museum, London, Department of Prints and Drawings, Thomas
    Rowlandson, ‘Soldiers on the March’, 1808 is an oft-cited print of soldiers’
    families. Kathleen Wilson brings together existing work on the wives and
    families of non-commissioned ofﬁcers and privates in the eighteenth cen-
    tury wars prior to 1793: Kathleen Wilson, The Island Race: Englishness, Empire
    and Gender in the Eighteenth Century (London: Routledge, 2003), pp. 97–105,
    notes 16–37. Clive Emsley, British Society and the French Wars, 1793–1815
    (London: Macmillan, 1979) was one of the earliest studies to mention these
    military families during the French Wars.
 2. For more information about the new system of care, see Patricia Y. C. E. Lin,
    ‘Citizenship, Military Families and the Creation of a New Deﬁnition of
    ‘Deserving Poor’ in Britain, 1793–1815’, Social Politics 7 (Spring 2000):
    5–46, also Lin, ‘Extending Her Arms: Military Families and the Transfor-
    mation of the British State, 1793–1815’, University of California, Berkeley,
    Ph.D. dissertation, 1997. The East India Company provided some pen-
    sions for selected widows and training for selected orphans of its soldiers.
    However, the beneﬁts for the families of His Majesty’s forces were far
    more extensive, systematized and institutionalized. See Durba Ghosh, ‘Mak-
    ing and Un-making Loyal Subjects: Pensioning Widows and Educating
    Orphans in Early Colonial India’, Journal of Imperial and Commonwealth
    History 31:1 (January 2003): 1–28, and Ghosh, Sex and the Family in Colo-
    nial India: The Making of Empire (Cambridge: Cambridge University Press,
    2006), pp. 206–245.
 3. Linda Colley highlights the multi-racial and multi-cultural aspect of the pur-

    Dream of Global Supremacy, 1600–1850 (New York: Pantheon Books, 2002).
