

### PART I11 INTERNAL HISTORY AND WORKING OF THE CONTINENTAL SYSTEM

CHAPTER I TREATMENT OF CONFISCATED GOODS

THE task that Napoleon made the central point of his policy manifestly imposed the greatest demands on its inventor and his helpers, especially when we take into consideration the administrative powers at the disposal of the governments of the time. With regard to what was by far the most important point, namely, the exclusion of British and colonial goods, the question of the application of the system at once struck upon a peculiar difficulty, namely, the problem of what to do with the con fiscated merchandise. To Napoleon himself, strange as it may seem, this problem was a matter of minor importance, inasmuch as from first to last he adhered to the view taken over from the politicians of the Convention, that all goods were sold on the credit of Englishmen and thus were not yet paid for when they were seized, and that, accordingly, the loss in any case hit the enemy, With a persistence that never wavered he preached to his allies and helpers the doctrines that, ' inasmuch as the (continental) merchants never buy except on credit, it is a fact that no goods are ever paid for,' and that, ' all goods being the property of the English,' their confiscation means ' a back handed blow for England which is terrific '.l On this assump tion, moreover, the whole difficulty would pretty soon have been overcome ; for after a sufficiently large number of such losses had been inflicted on the English they might reasonably 1 Quotations from two letters addressed to his brother Jerome, King of West phalia, on Jan. 23,1807, and to the Emperor Alexander of Russia on Oct. 23, 1810.
