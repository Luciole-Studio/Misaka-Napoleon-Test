

[Nicholas I](https://cdn.britannica.com/83/76183-050-00443A6C/Nicholas-I-Russia.jpg)Emperor Nicholas I of Russia.(more)

Nicholas especially attended to education; he wished to clear it of everything politically dangerous and confine it to the upper class. He [abolished](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/abolished) the liberal university statutes of Alexander (1804). By the new statutes of 1835 he detached the [primary education](https://www.britannica.com/topic/elementary-education) intended for the lower classes from the [gymnasiums](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Gymnasium-German-school) and universities, where only children of gentry and of officials were to be admitted.

The expulsion of [Charles X](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Charles-X) from [France](https://www.britannica.com/place/France) and the [November Insurrection](https://www.britannica.com/event/November-Insurrection) (1830–31) in [Poland](https://www.britannica.com/place/Poland) determined the legitimist tendency of Nicholas’s [foreign policy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/foreign-policy). He wished to become a real “policeman” of Europe, and at Münchengrätz (Mnichovo Hradiště), in September 1833, he renewed relations with Metternich. His excessive interest in the “sick man” (Ottoman Empire) in [Constantinople](https://www.britannica.com/place/Istanbul) finished by rousing Europe against him. In 1833 Nicholas saved the sultan from the Egyptian rebel [Muḥammad ʿAlī](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Muhammad-Ali-pasha-and-viceroy-of-Egypt), and by the [Treaty of Hünkâr Iskelesi](https://www.britannica.com/event/Treaty-of-Hunkar-Iskelesi) (July 8, 1833) appeared to receive for that service free passage for Russian ships to the Mediterranean. To all other powers, the Dardanelles were to be closed during wartime. This [concession](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/concession) drew the attention of the European powers, and in 1841 all the five great powers (France, Britain, [Prussia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Prussia), [Russia](https://www.britannica.com/place/Russia), and Austria) agreed that the Dardanelles should be closed to warships of all nations.

## [Slavophiles](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Slavophile) and [Westernizers](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Westernizer)

[Mikhail Bakunin](https://cdn.britannica.com/21/13221-004-B0F6E0C4/Mikhail-Aleksandrovich-Bakunin.jpg)(more)

In sharp contrast with Nicholas’s educational policy, a new generation grew up which was bred by Russian universities, especially [Moscow State University](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Moscow-State-University), between 1830 and 1848. They were not politicians or liberals of a Franco-English type. They were idealists and students of the philosophy of [Schelling](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Friedrich-Wilhelm-Joseph-von-Schelling), [Fichte](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Johann-Gottlieb-Fichte), and [Hegel](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Georg-Wilhelm-Friedrich-Hegel). In [Moscow](https://www.britannica.com/place/Moscow) literary salons, they did not discuss the form of the government but dug deep into the very foundations of Russian history and the Russian national mind. Most of them declared that Russia was unlike Europe and that its type of civilization was potentially far higher than the European. They execrated [Peter the Great’s](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Peter-the-Great) Europeanization of Russia as a fatal [deviation](https://www.britannica.com/dictionary/deviation) from the genuine course of Russian history, and they wanted Russia to come back to the forsaken principles of the Eastern Church and state—to [orthodoxy and autocracy](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Orthodoxy-Autocracy-and-Nationality). Nevertheless, the majority of public opinion, led by [Aleksandr Ivanovich Herzen](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Aleksandr-Ivanovich-Herzen), [Vissarion Grigoryevich Belinsky](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Vissarion-Grigoryevich-Belinsky), [Mikhail Bakunin](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Mikhail-Bakunin), Timofey Granovsky, and others, revolted against this [Slavophile](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Slavophile) doctrine. They opposed it with their own doctrine of the Western origin of Russian civilization. Herzen and Bakunin emigrated from Russia on the approach of the [Revolutions of 1848](https://www.britannica.com/event/Revolutions-of-1848). They became the originators of Russian [socialism](https://www.britannica.com/money/socialism), and Herzen saw socialist elements in the Russian peasants’ commune ([mir](https://www.britannica.com/topic/mir-Russian-community)).
