North Africa. The British navy would not only have lost its harbors at
Augusta and Syracuse, but also the fresh food and water that the island was
able to provide. Without Sicily, its arduous role in the Mediterranean would
have been hard to fill. Sicily was never a firm base, but all the same it was one
Britain needed to sustain her effort in the war with Napoleon.
   Of the legacy of British rule in Sicily, Louis Simond, a Frenchman, wrote
in 1828:

  The English, it must      be acknowledged,      have left here no honourable
  monument of a power paramount to sovereignty . . . the roads, the pris-
  ons, the ECan         the corrupt and barbarous administration of justice,
  remained in just as wretched a state as before they interfered. It is true they
  saved the island from French dominion; from the violence and plunder
  which attended it at De and in Calabria; they kept strict discipline;
  they paid honestly and liberally for all they had; but they did not mix
  cordially with the people; they continued meddling, teaching, ruling, with
  a ee hand and a supercilious pride, till all classes were tired out; and yet
  all classes regret them, simply because they saved them from Naples for a
  while.?4

   Apart from the fact that the British did do something to improve the
appalling roads in Sicily, certainly in the northeast tip of the island, this rather
superficial judgment really misses the important point. As has been stressed
so often in this book, the ministry in London was extremely loath to interfere
in Sicily’s internal affairs, and when eventually it was constrained to do so, its
representative, Lord Wiliam Bentinck, spent his time trying to get the
Sicilians to effect the needed reforms themselves. His lack of success in
persuading them to work a system of parliamentary government induced
them in the end to turn back to their king, who personified Neapolitan
domination.
