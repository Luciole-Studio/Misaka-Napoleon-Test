   It was for this reason that Bentinck’s instructions specified that he must
reassure the court. Britain, he must tell it, had no intention of interfering in
its internal affairs or of trying to effect a revolution in the Sicilian constitu-
tion. Nevertheless the king and queen must be informed that Britain, being
as anxious as they were to see them restored to the kingdom of Naples and
equally anxious to use Sicily’s resources in order to achieve this end, felt that
the people must first be appeased. This could be done only by granting a
share in the government to Sicilians, by paying heed to Sicily’s parliament,
and by redressing popular grievances. If the court was not willing to do these
things and so was faced by a revolution, it could not expect from its British
ally any military or other assistance.
   Bentinck’s arrival in Sicily was awaited with both anxiety and hope,
according to Douglas, the secretary of legation, who told Wellesley that most
Sicilians would now “be satisfied with nothing less than the British flag flying
all over Sicily,” and a “total eradication of the abominable system.”!4 When
he did arrive on 18 July, only two days had elapsed after the arrest of the five
barons—something he thought not coincidental, for having learnt of his
reputation the court was afraid he might enlist the support of these noble
dissidents. He stayed in Sicily five weeks only, at the end of which he
returned to England to report and to ask for wider powers. He had, during
his very brief stay, encountered a completely negative attitude on the part of
the king and queen and their ministers, who denied there was any sort of
crisis and therefore the need for any measures to meet one. He was told that
the levy of 1 percent had been entirely constitutional, the barons’ protest had
been illegal and disapproved of by parliament, the stories of popular discon-
tent were entirely without foundation, and the barons who had lodged the
protest were in no way representative. Although he considered it unlikely
there would be a serious revolt with the opposition leaders removed, spies
and informers everywhere, and the royal army disliking the people, Bentinck
was convinced of the need for change—a change that could only be effected
by controlling the British subsidy and by employing forcible language.!5
   Before he left Sicily for England, Bentinck had appointed General Freder-
ick Maitland to act in his absence as his deputy, and Maitland soon convinced
himself that the Sicilian government had joined hands with the enemy.
Maitland wrote to Wellesley in some anxiety:

  We are in such a state as our enemy would wish—we believe the Sicilian
  government to be plotting and combining for our ruin, and whether from
  consciousness of their intrigues with Bonaparte or from apprehension that
  we will bring about a change of government, from a despotic one, which
