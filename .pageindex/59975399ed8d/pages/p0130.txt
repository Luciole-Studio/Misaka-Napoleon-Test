  British public opinion favored the return of Ferdinand to Naples, as
Castlereagh told the Austrian ambassador, if only because he had lost his
kingdom through having allied himself with Britain; it was a matter of
national honor and dignity to see him restored. Metternich shared the view
of Castlereagh about putting Ferdinand back on his throne. He told the
Sicilian minister in Vienna in confidence that he did not intend that Murat
should keep the throne of Naples and that Ferdinand must have his kingdom
back, with Sicily, but not with a constitution. The Austrian emperor's
invitation to Ferdinand to behave more liberally in future clearly therefore
did not extend to his governing his kingdom with a constitution.!7
    Bentinck’s reaction to Ferdinand’s new posture may at first appear puz-
zling. Before leaving Sicily for good on 18 July 1814 (his position there as
minister having been assumed by William A’Court), he penned a dispatch to
Castlereagh in which he seemed to show satisfaction at the way events had
turned out recently—surely the exact opposite of what he for so long had
hoped and worked for, and of sentiments expressed in his journal as recently
as a week before (“I never could think it desirable that the king should
conduct the government” and “How could one suppose that a man who had
governed ill for fifty years could all of a sudden change?”).!8 But now,
writing on 5 July, he congratulated Castlereagh on what he described as a
happy ending to a long and embarrassing relationship between two allied
governments, and he dwelt on the king’s “sincere desire” to support the
Sicilian constitution and to be reconciled to his political foes.!9 Yet clearly
Bentinck did not believe this; he was either seeking to deceive Castlereagh or
was putting the best face he could on the matter. He had tried in vain to get
the barons to work together for a compromise and now was disgusted at the
outcome. He told his friends he would be glad to go away—“I owed nothing
to the country which I despised.”2° As A’Court (whom Bentinck thought
“clever” and “honorable,” though he held very different political views)
wrote privately to William Hamilton, “Having brought the comedy to the
fifth act, he [Bentinck] seems to be by no means sorry to get away before the
dénouement actually takes place.” He [A’Court] was grateful that the king had
resumed power before he took over and that “it was Lord William and not I
who did it, otherwise I should have been reproached.”?1!
   Not surprisingly, the king now chose to employ as his ministers men who
had been no friends to the party Bentinck had supported, indeed the very
ones he had got rid of in the purge of March 1813. The British government
had given assurances that there was to be no victimization, but not feeling at
all confident about that promise being fulfilled, the duke of Orleans and
Prince Belmonte both decided to seek safety in exile—in Paris, where they
