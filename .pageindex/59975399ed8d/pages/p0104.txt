   Bentinck was also increasingly frustrated at what he regarded as the weak-
ness of the ministry—the lack of mutual confidence shown between ministers
and the hereditary prince, disunion among ministers themselves, and the lack
of cabinet government as that was understood in England. Bentinck said that
all he could give was support to the administration—it was for ministers to
lead the House. Belmonte in whom hitherto Bentinck had reposed such
confidence now seemed “blinded by his own vanity,” could not apply himself
to business, and was quite hopeless as a prime minister. Without assuming
the government himself, Bentinck found it difficult to take a firmer line, as
Orleans was urging him to do. “We appeared to have everything our own
way and to have all the power . . . while in fact we have nothing. . . .°52
   Bentinck through Belmonte now approached the king, requesting that he
sanction the constitution, call a new parliament as soon as possible, and order
the queen away from Ficuzza. Although the queen was induced to go to
Castelvetrano in the south of the island, the king on 9 March 1813 arrived
unexpectedly in Palermo and announcd that he was resuming power, without
informing either Bentinck or the prince. Acclaimed vociferously in the
