                                 Preface


I n writing this short history of the British occupation of Sicily during the
     war against Napoleon, I lay no claim to originality. My debt to the two
perceptive books by Dr. John Rosselli on Lord William Bentinck and to Dr.
Piers Mackesy’s masterly history of the war in the Mediterranean from 1803
to 1810 are, I need hardly add, enormous. I am also much indebted to the
writings of Antonio Capograssi, Denis Mack Smith, Jacques Rambaud,
Charles Auriol, R. M. Johnston, Angela Valente, and Harold Acton, though
I have naturally had recourse to those primary sources available in this
country.
   But my aim has been to try to synthesize the findings and conclusions of
others, in works in English, French, and Italian, and to attempt to answer
these questions—what were the British doing in Sicily, and did they on the
whole succeed in achieving what they set out to do there?
