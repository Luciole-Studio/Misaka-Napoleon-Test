   Castlereagh was not entirely pleased when he learnt from Bentinck what
had happened in March. “We must,” he wrote back, “so try to arrange as to
obviate the necessity of frequent acts of undisguised power.” Withholding
the subsidy, he thought, should be a sufficient curb on the king, and stronger
measures ought not to be necessary.°* It was certainly true that financial
embarrassment continued to force the king and the prince to lean heavily on
the British. The king complained of being burdened by debt, which he would
not allow parliament to shoulder, fearing that the latter might at some time
call him publicly to account. Bentinck, who the previous November had
intervened to prevent parliament voting as much as a fifth of its revenue to
meet the needs of the royal family, now had to lend the king and the prince
enough money to tide them over.©> But he thought that the situation in
Sicily was sufficiently stable for him to leave it to take command in eastern
Spain, where his army had in the past few months suffered from unsatisfac-
tory leadership. Accordingly he sailed from the island on 27 May 1813,
delegating to Lord Montgomerie his plenary powers as minister. Neither
Belmonte nor Orleans had wanted Bentinck to go away, but the latter felt it
his only chance for what he termed “recovering his troops,” by which he
meant, presumably, being able to retain a lien on them for a future Italian
expedition.©® Even so, he took the precaution of taking with him a private
letter in which Belmonte had given him warning of democratic perils ahead,
so that he could, if the occasion demanded, justify a sudden return to Sicily.°”
