   In 1811, most British merchants withdrew from the license
trade, and only 7,602 licenses were issued, less than half the
previous year's total. This diminishment was especially appar­
ent in the category of licenses issued for export to the Baltic.
Gorged with the Baltic's products, fearful of confiscation of
whatever cargoes they might send out, and discouraged by the
sinking prices of colonial produce in Sweden and Russia, most
British merchants avoided that sea's cold waters. In August of
 1811, Captain John March , one of the Americans languishing
in Gothenburg, wrote h o m e that the quantity of colonial pro­
duce sent by England into the Baltic was "I suppose not more
than one-third what was last year." 8 This constriction of her
export commerce in 1811 brought Great Britain closer to defeat
than she would be again until the days of the Kaiser's U ­
boats.
