

Ronnie was a realist, less deeply versed in Spanish letters, more aware of the north and centre of the country, and its urban life. But Spanish society had, of course, changed fundamentally, and with it Spanish culture, by the time he settled there in the nineties. The passions that fascinated Brenan, their embers still glowing in Blood of Spain, had burnt themselves out. Napoleon’s Cursed War, not a book his predecessor could have written, was composed in the banalized, self-satisfied landscape of Felipe González and Julio Iglesias, José María Aznar and Pedro Almodóvar. In Valencia, Spain’s third-largest city, he could see the provincialism of its new European conformities, and suffered from it. But the Jacobin streak, and his perseverance, remained. He was correcting the proofs of his last collection of essays, Las dos guerras de España, containing his comparison of the Peninsular and Civil Wars, when he died in February 2012. In England, the class he detested was back in the saddle, amid riot and recession; in Spain, a bloated monarch astride big game, corruption oozing from the palace, demonstrations of the young in the streets, the illusions of counterfeit wealth vanishing, a Third Republic overdue. It was the kind of scenery that had made him the iconoclast he was.

Perry Anderson
