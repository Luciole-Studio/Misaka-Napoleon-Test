

About this theoretical tension Ronnie was himself direct and clear-sighted. After Blood of Spain appeared, he published a significant pendant to it in NLR, which began, ‘As oral history broadens its field of study, and in particular as it moves into the field of political history, it cannot elude the task, bounden on materialist historiography, of providing a causal knowledge of the processes it is studying’. Scattered throughout Blood of Spain, and secondary to oral testimony, were observations bearing on that commitment, and he would now attempt ‘to set them out in a more coherent and accessible form’. There followed a masterly synthesis of the reasons why the Civil War broke out, and why the counter-revolutionary bloc around Franco won it, each examined in fine grain. The first lay above all in an unstable balance of class forces, once capitalist modernization failed to generate a political system – whether in Primo’s dictatorship or the Second Republic – capable of containing the new social forces it had unleashed, and so producing an explosive condition in which ‘the dominated (especially rural) classes no longer wanted to live in the old way, and the ruling (especially rural) classes feared that, shortly, they would be unable to continue in the old way’, and thus, ‘long accustomed to dominate by force’, opted for a ‘military coup to bring in an authoritarian state’. The causes of the second were to be found, above all, in the disputes between the divided forces of the Republic over the relative priority of war or revolution, and their failure to develop instead a strategy for revolutionary war, combining popular and regular armed forces, heavy armour at the front and guerrilla warfare behind the lines, with an ideology capable of neutralizing smallholder support for the enemy camp.14

The analytic detail and precision of this assessment of the objective realities determining the onset and outcome of the Civil War was no afterthought. This was the other side of the Fraser approach to history. Two outstanding examples of it can be found in contemporary surveys of the economic and political landscape of Spain under, and just after, Franco which he published here in these years. Both were the product of direct investigations on the ground, trips to Spain in which he gathered documentation and opinion. The first, written in 1969, offered a periodization of the successive models of capital accumulation and class coercion that Franco’s regime had represented, across the three decades since his victory in the Civil War, and looked at the new working-class and oppositional forces which had emerged with its economic liberalization. The second, a report written in early 1976, after he had completed the interviews for Blood of Spain but before he was done editing them, laid out a commanding panorama of the array of social and political forces jockeying for position in the first months after the death of the dictator, and the range of possible scenarios before the country.15 Like most immediate political analyses, both articles had their miscalculations, in a common overestimation of the potential for radical insurgency within the make-over of the system Franco had built, and in the second an underestimation of the efficacy of the decisive legacy he bequeathed to Spain. The monarchy that would neutralize any democratic rupture – as once conceived – with the established order the Caudillo had saved and transmitted to posterity is the only mechanism missing from that inventory of the forces in play after his death, and so any premonition of the subsequent abasement of once-republican parties before both. But with this qualification, both remain impressive texts that can be consulted with profit today.16
