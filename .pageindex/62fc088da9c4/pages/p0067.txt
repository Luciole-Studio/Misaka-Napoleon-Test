

The Church thus bore the brunt of the state’s fiscal crisis.52 As state bonds were now backed by the sale of church property, they seemed to many churchmen similar to the assignats of the French Revolution;53 and the Church’s opposition to these measures was in no small part responsible for the downfall of Godoy and his royal master in 1808.54

None of the measures solved the treasury’s crisis. Even government ministers did not get paid; by 1808 their salaries were the second highest item on the list of outstanding debt.55 Fiscal pressure forced many municipalities to sell communal lands; as a result townships and villages dominated by noble-appointed oligarchs became ‘inveterate enemies of the government’.56 More accurately, it could be said that all the monied classes had ‘lost confidence in the solvency of the crown’.57 Godoy paid the political price. Hatred of the royal favourite grew in all sectors of the population which suffered, to a greater or lesser degree, the cost of an unpopular war.
