   The only difficulty he foresaw was in our Treaty with Murat,
who, precisely because he would wish for an occasion to conquer
Sicily, would violently object to our occupation of it: but this
might be met by an article, on which the British Government
would insist, stipulating that Murat should consent to any
agreement which Great Britain might conclude with the King
of Sicily on this point.
   On the whole His Lordship entered most cordially into your
view of this question and promised not to lose sight of it during
the negotiations....I have no doubt, therefore, My Lord, if
the present negotiations should happily terminate in a peace,
that your hopes for Sicily will not be disappointed. . . .’

              (James Graham to Bentinck, 7 March 1814, B.P.)
