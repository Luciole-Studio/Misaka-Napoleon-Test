   But these economic straits alone are not the whole explanation.
It will not do to assume that men are always aware of their own
interests. In fact, conservatism and esprit de corps often led a
majority of the barons to act against what might have seemed
their economic interest; and, in contrast, another reason for the
growing concern with reform was the spread among the small
class of educated Sicilians of the commonplaces of Enlightened
thought. These motives in turn were often mixed. It is striking
to see how the particularism and attachment to tradition charac-
teristic of an island on the edge of Europe infused into the
Enlightenment a distinct local colour. It is curious, for instance,
that the historian and jurist Gregorio, writing at the end of the
18th century, should have based his assertion of the supremacy
of public law over private, and of the monarchy over particular
privilege, on an appeal to the past—to the Norman Conquest
and to the golden age of strong central government that followed
it, as against the Aragonese period of baronial anarchy. Reform,
in Gregorio’s view, was to be achieved not by wiping the slate
clean, but by restoring the Sicilian Constitution to its pristine
purity, shorn of later and ‘unnatural’ growths.* Similarly, the
Abate Paolo Balsamo, a professor of agriculture and political
economy, while he followed the teaching of Arthur Young and
Adam Smith in these subjects, looked, when he came to deal
with constitutional issues, to the Aragonese period as to the
fount of Sicilian liberties; and when he turned to Blackstone
for guidance in drafting the 1812 Constitution, he drew not
merely on Blackstone’s paraphrase of Montesquieu, but on his
interpretation of the English common law—between which and
the inherited mass of Sicilian law a parallel could be drawn.*

the sympathy they shewed with English institutions. At a time
