  It was in these circumstances that Bentinck, during his tour
of Sicily in November and December 1813, brought to light
the notorious ‘philosophic dream’ which, if carried into effect,
would have made of the island ‘after Ireland, the brightest jewel
in the British crown’.
   Bentinck’s tour through the towns of the East coast and the
interior was remarkable in that, for the first time since the
Court’s flight from Naples, someone concerned with the govern-
ment of Sicily took the trouble to visit it extensively. Bentinck,
who like the Royal Family had until then scarcely stirred out
of Palermo and its environs, was much struck: ‘it was impossible
to know the country without having seen it’, he told the Prince
on his return. His was a quasi-royal progress; everywhere he was
received with banquets, balls, triumphal arches, fireworks, and
allegorical tableaux of England and Sicily embracing. He ‘ha-
rangued’ the local councils, urging them to see that the bricconi
of the 1813 Parliament were not re-elected; he interviewed
place-seekers, discussed local affairs with the leading citizens,
and returned, as has been seen, convinced of the need to strike at
aristocratic quite as much as at royal ‘prepotenza’ in local affairs.
Though the opposition was no doubt merely silent rather than
reconciled, still Bentinck’s tour was to all appearances a triumph.”
