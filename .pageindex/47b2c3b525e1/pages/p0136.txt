      DICTATORSHIP                 AND      THE    ‘DREAM

‘The Vicar kept quiet, while Bentinck alone acted and laid down the
law, running about from one department to the next, writing instructions
himself and having them written at his will.”*
  So the King heard at the beginning of November 1813. He
was not misinformed: Bentinck was now, as Orleans said, ‘the
first Minister of the country’, and the secretaries of state were
his ‘clerks’. With them he discussed measures of policy and
government appointments; while the British generals and agents
in other parts of Sicily acted as unofficial political representatives,
recommending local supporters of the Constitution for the
places and favours which Bentinck could obtain for them.
Actually, Bentinck’s power was rather that of a monarch than
of a Prime Minister. Great nobles called him in as arbiter of
their family disputes; such conservative barons as Butera,
Pantelleria, San Marco, Gargallo—some of whom had been
unseated by the Patriotic victory of 1812—now sought to in-
gratiate themselves with him; the Senates of various towns
apostrophised him as the Moses of Sicily.* Criticism was stifled
at the outset of his dictatorship by the arrest, trial, and im-
prisonment of two nobles who had published violent attacks
on him—one of them, ironically, was Anjou, one of the ‘five
barons’ whom the Court had arbitrarily arrested in 1811.*
According to a story current in Palermo, the Prince commented
on this state of affairs by telling his wife one day ‘“This morning
I had to wait a long time in Council for His Majesty’—his
wife, astonished, answered, “What, the King?”—‘No, I was
waiting for Lord Bentinck” —and laughing he went away’.*
   The country was outwardly quiet. From the officers he had
sent to garrison the interior Bentinck heard that the great
majority of the people appeared to feel nothing but gratitude
for British protection. The Royal Family also seemed perfectly
docile. The Prince effaced himself, called Bentinck his ‘ange
tutelaire’, and professed the most commendable feelings; but
