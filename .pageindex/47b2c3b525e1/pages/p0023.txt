Castelnuovo, like Belmonte, had travelled and read widely: but
while Belmonte was for a time on intimate terms with the Kin
and Queen, and remained a fast friend of Louis-Philippe, Duke
of Orleans, Castelnuovo led an austere and retired life, shunning
the Court, which, indeed, thought him inclined to democratic
ideas; while Belmonte had the confidence and the gifts of an
active politician and party leader, and seems to have been some-
what shallow and volatile where issues of public policy were
concerned, Castelnuovo was rather the political theorist, the
conscientious administrator, the dispassionate and often despond-
ent observer, little suited for the rough-and-tumble of everyday
politics. His friend Balsamo, though he praised ‘the uprightness
and ready perspicacity of his mind, the firmness of his character,
his delicate sense of honour, the purity of his morals and his
disinterested devotion   to public affairs’, nevertheless had to
admit more than once that Castelnuovo was liable in moments
of crisis to throw in his hand, with deplorable results, rather
than compromise his Roman firmness in the cause he held right.*
In both Belmonte and Castelnuovo an aristocratic, authoritarian
streak came out often enough: but if Belmonte may be com-
pared to a great Whig nobleman, Castelnuovo’s convictions
sprang rather from an idealisation of Republican Rome and
America; and his concern for equity led him into policies which
could only undermine the existing aristocratic system.
  These men, and their no less varicoloured friends, the British
representatives came to know very well. But in the early years
of the occupation they were more familiar with disunity and
inconsistency at Court. Here too several persons or cliques
meddled in affairs of State, often at cross purposes—the Queen
and her friends; the King when he took the trouble; the King’s
favourite Ascoli; the Ministers. Jealousy and intrigue had endless
