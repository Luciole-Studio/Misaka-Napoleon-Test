groups, the rest of the patriotic party was typified rather than
led by Belmonte, who seemed to have no very clear notion of
what the reformed constitution should be like. He too wished
to diminish the power of the sovereign, who was ‘now a Sultan’;
one guesses that he inclined to an aristocratic form of govern-
ment: “H.R.H. could not suppose’, he asked the Prince, ‘he
wanted revolution and that born to the situation he was he
could wish to lower himself to an equality with his groom.’
He talked at one time of reverting entirely to the old constitution,
at another of having it ‘revised, well washed and cleaned and
put into an English dress, habillé 4 anglaise’. Yet he must have
been won over temporarily to Castelnuovo’s views, for Bal-
samo’s draft—which they both approved—included a clause
abolishing feudal privileges.*
  Bentinck deprecated this somewhat conservative attitude of
Belmonte’s: he agreed with Castelnuovo that the day was past
when the nobility could claim a monopoly of power. Yet he
once again gave in to Belmonte on the important question of
how constitutional reform was to be carried out. Castelnuovo,
no less than Belmonte, feared democracy and the backward
mob: for the very reason that he wished to establish political
liberty and a measure    of social change, he dreaded a recru-
descence of faction and class egoism in the new Parliament; he
