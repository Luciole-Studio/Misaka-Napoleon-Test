   We should not underrate the effect of such outbursts on a
man who liked a quiet life. But on the other side Cassaro urged
the King to give way, and so did the wretched and flustered
Prince, whom Bentinck’s thunderings on the army question had
deprived of any will to resist. Even so the King stood his ground
as long as he could; he urged his son to dissolve Parliament and
summon another to choose between the old Constitution and
the English. In the end all he would consent to was to scribble
a note authorising the Prince to sanction such articles as ‘con-
formed absolutely to the English Constitution’.*
   There was reason to fear that this was one of the King’s usual
devices to avoid committing himself; but it is doubtful whether
any oath or signature would have bound him. What did matter
was that from then on he invariably insisted that the new
constitution must not depart by one iota from the English. He
probably had no very clear notion of what the English system
was like—he later decided to send to England for an authori-
tative version—but it was at any rate known or ascertainable;
it would set bounds on innovation; and it was something to
which Bentinck could not easily object. The Prince’s assent to
