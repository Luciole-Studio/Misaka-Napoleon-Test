    It took six weeks’ negotiations to undeceive Bentinck. At
 first he was prepared to act moderately; he objected to Bel-
 monte’s and Orleans’ policy of ‘frightening’ the King and his
 associates; he refused to be drawn (as the Queen hoped he
 would be) into making it a condition sine qua non that she and
 her husband should live apart. As he later pointed out to Cassaro,
    ‘I must be always prepared for my defence...I was liable to be
 attacked in England... I was threatened with the publication of every-
 thing in Russia and Germany, and therefore I could let nothing pass
 without its refutation.”*
    He began with a Note (15 January) to Cassaro and Circello,
 recalling the King’s ‘parole sacrée’ and requesting that the Queen
 should go back to Santa Margherita. He had already privately
 suggested to the King, through Caccamo, three possible courses:
 he might live with the Queen at a considerable distance from
 Palermo; or he might abdicate, in which case the Queen would
 be free to go where she chose; or he might come to Palermo
 and resume power if he wished, provided the Queen fulfilled
 the ‘promise’. There was no reply for a fortnight: confusion
 reigned at La Ficuzza: the Queen refused to budge, and the King
 did his best to dodge the issue.* On the advice of Cassaro (who
 apparently hoped thus to frighten the King into action) Bentinck
