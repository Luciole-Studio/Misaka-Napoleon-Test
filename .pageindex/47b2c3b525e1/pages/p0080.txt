danger that Sicily might lapse into feudal reaction: ‘these proud
barons’, they agreed, must be induced to ‘lay aside selfishness;
and take the people (not the mob) for something’. But they, as
well as Bentinck, were quite satisfied on this score by the reso-
lutions of July 20th. Not so Leckie: he complained bitterly that
Bentinck had been deceived; the ‘English’ Constitution was
‘one of the greatest fallacies ever exhibited’; it would lead to an
‘unbalanced oligarchy’ more oppressive to the mass of the
people than the former despotic monarchy. Bentinck was sensi-
tive to this charge, which French propaganda in Italy fastened
on from the first, and he had it refuted in the official British
paper: significantly, the article was written, not by a Sicilian,
but by a professed agent of the Italian patriots, and described
the Constitution as the symbol of a risorgimento, a raising up of
the whole people to a better condition of life.*
   To Bentinck these tokens of British commendation or blame
mattered less than the official opinion of his Government. From
Harrowby’s outburst, already mentioned, and from Liverpool’s
later strictures, it is plain that those Ministers who considered
the matter at all had no liking for the new constitution: they
were ready if need be to explain it away as the by-blow of
Bentinck’s Whiggish enthusiasm. Castlereagh was more cau-
