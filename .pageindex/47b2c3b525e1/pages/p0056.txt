                           CHAPTER*II

   THE\PROTEGCTORATE                     AND      THE     ‘BASES’

The theme of British policy in Sicily—so Bentinck had written
home at the end of March—must be ‘the firm and just exercise
of our superiority’. What exactly this might mean in practice
was Bentinck’s chief problem throughout the ensuing year.
  By her intervention Britain had established a de facto protec-
torate over Sicily; but it was a protectorate born under an am-
biguous star. Apprehension and distrust, carefree optimism and
good intentions, had all gone into the original decision to
intervene; it is not surprising that the result was a diplomatic
muddle. Britain could not very well appear to adopt Napoleon’s
methods: the fiction had therefore to be kept up that the two
nations were equal and independent allies. Castlereagh allowed
Bentinck to go on using the language of force—though not
force itself—to achieve British aims; but he repeatedly insisted
that the ‘honour’, ‘dignity’, and ‘security’ of the Sicilian Royal
Family must be safeguarded.* There was a pretence that some-
how it was all for the good of the Crown and people of Sicily.
Not the least reason for muddle was that this was not wholly
a pretence. Bentinck really believed it; “an interference merely
to carry some British object,’ he said, ‘might not be justifiable’; but
‘listening to the call of the whole country and having no view but
their benefit and most of all of the Royal Family themselves, the British
Government will surely be considered by the present and future gener-
ations as only fulfilling the duties of a good and great nation.”*

Castlereagh, for his part, seems to have taken the fiction for
granted until it became too inconvenient to keep up. Besides,
no one had attempted to think out the possible consequences of
intervention: Bentinck, Amherst and other British observers,
by inference Wellesley himself, all had assumed that, the first
crisis over, the ‘machine’ would run on smoothly without
further interference. The negotiations over the new Ministry
and the calling of Parliament had begun to undeceive Bentinck;
