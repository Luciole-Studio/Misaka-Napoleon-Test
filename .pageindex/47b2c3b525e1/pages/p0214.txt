  adopt the Constitutions and chiefs most agreeable to them’: to R/Adm. Greig,
  27 Aug., Secret Letter-Book, B.P. But a few months later he told the Prince that
  ‘by not forcing himself upon the Neapolitans, he would gain many friends—the issue
  would always be the same, because the British influence if they made the conquest
  would always direct with ease the will of the people’: Journal, 22 Dec. 1812; cf.
  the same argument in Bentinck to Bathurst (secret) 24 Feb. 1813: Gallavresi and La
  Tour, 357.
the ‘private ends’ ofAustria and Russia. Bentinck to Bathurst (secret) 24 Feb.; the Hereditary
  Prince to the Prince Regent, enclosed with Belmonte to Castelcicala, 23 Feb., FO
  70/61; Bentinck to Bathurst (confidential) 7 Apr., Bathurst MSS. 230; Lamb to
  Hamilton, 25 May, FO 70/62 (‘I fear there is... enough to get me the name of a
  most troublesome fellow’).—It has been a commonplace with a number of Italian
  historians, both contemporary and modern, that the secret societies (especially the
  Carboneria) which made their appearance in the last years of the Napoleonic regime
  were subsidised with ‘British gold’ and encouraged by swarms of British agents
  throughout Italy: cf. for instance P. Colletta, Storia del Reame di Napoli (Capolago,
  1834), bk. VIII, ch. xlix; A. Luzio, La Massoneria e il Risorgimento Italiano (Bologna,
  1925), 1, 118-9, 162-6; O. Dito, Massoneria, Carboneria e altre societa segrete (Turin, 1905),
  208-9; Capograssi, op. cit., ch. xi. Such statements are generally based, if at all, on
  informers’ reports (during the Restoration period), hearsay, and inference. Capograssi
  even speaks (p, 184) of Bentinck’s ‘innumerable agents scattered throughout the
  peninsula’—whereas as late as the spring of 1813 Bentinck decided, because he had
  hitherto been unable to establish any satisfactory communication with Italy, to set
  up three intelligence agents at Malta, Vienna and Ponza for the purpose (Bentinck
   to Castlereagh, secret, 21 Mar. 1813, in M.H. Weil, Le Prince Eugene et Murat (Paris
   1902), i, 433-4); and a ‘Memorandum        of the Secret Missions under Lord William
   Bentinck’ (drawn up by his secretary, 24 May 1813: PRO GD/7/1) confirms that
   these were the only agents dealing with Italy. Moreover, neither Bentinck’s des-
   patches nor his private papers supply any evidence that he was ever in direct contact
   with the secret societies before 1814. The indirect contacts he did have can be listed
   as follows: 1. The Archduke Francis and his supporters: their plans came to nothing,
   at any rate in Italy, and after 1812 little is heard of them. 2. One Alessandro Turri,
