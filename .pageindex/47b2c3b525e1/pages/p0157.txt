   Bentinck himself did not trouble to conceal his hopes. His
Italian Levy had landed at Leghorn with a banner bearing the
device ‘Italian union—national independence’; although there
is only tenuous evidence that Bentinck actively helped the
Carbonari and other patriotic societies,* he certainly said and
did enough to deserve Mier’s description of him as a ‘préneur
enragé de constitutions’, and Ferdinand’s complaint that he was
behaving ‘like Dr Sangrado, who wished to bleed everybody
whether they needed it or not’.* Constitution and independence
was Bentinck’s watchword; the making of a united Italy seems
to have been a rather hazier ideal, which might come into being
through some process of self-determination. This is shewn by
the most   notorious   episode of Bentinck’s Italian career, his
single-handed revival of the Genoese Republic. Bentinck’s short
campaign up the Ligurian coast ended when he took Genoa on
April 18th, two days after fighting had stopped in Lombardy.
A week later he issued a proclamation restoring the Genoese
Constitution as it had stood before France swallowed up the
Republic, but modifying it in the spirit of the old sixteenth-
century constitution. It was Sicily all over again, with this vital
difference—nobody had questioned Sicily’s independence,
whether real or formal; but Bentinck knew quite well, from
specific instructions of Castlereagh’s, that he was not to restore
any of the old Italian sovereigns apart from Tuscany and
Piedmont; in fact he had reason to suspect that the Allies would
merge Genoa with Piedmont. That, of course, was exactly what
the Allies did in the end, to the cost of the supposed ‘vertu
anglaise’ which—as Stendhal reported three years later—this
‘fameux manquement de foi’ was to brand in Italy as ‘pure
tartuferie’. From new documents it appears that by this time
