   So much for the outline of a growing conflict. But when we
come to look beyond outlines, classes, or abstract forces for the
motives of individual men who took part in it, we find some
curious blanks. The fact is that (for reasons that have less to do
with Sicily’s backwardness at the time than with its backward-
ness since) we are worse informed about the leading Sicilians
of a century and a half ago than we are about many ancient or
medieval characters. Most of them are names to which we can
tack a few anecdotes. But even what we know shows clearly
enough how hard it would be to fit these men into a convenient
mould. Among the ‘patriots’ motives and actions were as various
as the influences that set the movement going, and often no less
contradictory.
   Take, for instance, the Prince of Cassaro: A leader of baronial
resistance in the 1798 Parliament; later reconciled with the
Court; re-emerging to head the patriots in the 1810 Parliament;
following afterwards a line of conduct equally ambiguous and
variable—this enigmatic figure seems nevertheless to have acted
from some consistent principle besides mere opportunism: one
may perhaps see in him a man attached to the old aristocratic
constitution and equally opposed to royal interference and to
liberal reform.* No such consistency, however, can be found in
his sometime henchman the Prince of Aci, who throughout
his various and violent shifts of allegiance seems to have been
little more than a hothead.* As for the two most prominent
patriotic barons, the Prince of Belmonte and his uncle the Prince
of Castelnuovo, character and convictions differentiated and, in
the end, divided them.
