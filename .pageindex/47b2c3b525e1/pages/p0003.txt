       He found himself drawn gradually, against
    his will, deeper and deeper into the shifting
    sands of Sicilian politics. Protracted at-
    tempts to remove the Queen, to impose a
    new constitution (modelled on British lines)
    upon an almost feudal society, and to free
    Sicily from Neapolitan domination led to
    Bentinck’s becoming a virtual dictator.
    Benevolent though his rule was, it began to
    crumble when he conceived an ill-timed plan
    to establish Sicily as a British Colony. This
    was resented, naturally enough, by the
    Sicilian Royalists, but also by his own
    Government, whose foreign policy at the
    time did not favour the move. Bentinck’s
    irresponsible behaviour as commander of
    an expeditionary force in Italy brought
    matters to a head, and he was recalled in
    disgrace in 1815.
       The war ended; Britain ceased to take an
    interest in Sicily’s domestic troubles, and all
    traces of the abortive Parliamentary system
    gradually disappeared.
       Such, in outline, is Dr Rosselli’s theme;
    and his telling of it is no less entertaining
    than the full story itself. His book will
    interest not only students of European
    history and British foreign policy, but all
    those who are concerned with the moral
    issues that arise when a great power inter-
    feres in the affairs of a smaller power.
