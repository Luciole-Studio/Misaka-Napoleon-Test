   Both his new recklessness and his stubborn attachment to the
Italian cause came out in his wrangle with Murat. Immediately
after Bentinck’s landing at Leghorn the two men fell out over
who was to occupy Tuscany. Murat wanted it to cover his line
of communication to the North; Bentinck suspected that he
had his eye on a good deal more. The wrangle went on for a
whole month; as it grew Bentinck attempted more and more
openly to throw Murat out of the coalition altogether.* Murat
was from the start a prey to contradictory influences; there is
little point in asking whether Bentinck had some excuse for his
initial hostility to ‘le plus perfide des Gaules’-—a man more like
Maria Carolina than anyone he had ever seen;* whether, too,
this behaviour did or did not encourage Murat in his duplicity
towards the Allies. Nor is there much point in saying that the
alliance with Murat had already become more of an embarrass-
ment than a help, even to Austria, by the time Bentinck came
along; nor that the Austrian generals shared Bentinck’s distrust
of Murat if not his pugnacity; nor even that Castlereagh himself
shortly came to describe Murat as a ‘worthless dog’ who had
been ‘a mere calculator’, and later found Bentinck’s stiff-necked
attitude at this time highly convenient, since it had left a way
open for a reversal of British policy towards Naples.* The main
points are two: first, Bentinck kept up his hostility towards
