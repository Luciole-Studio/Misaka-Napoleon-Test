  What had happened is still not clear. One conjecture, put
forward at the time by the Prince, was that the King had
promised the Queen to resume power when he returned to
Palermo on February 6th; finding himself still caught between
the twin intransigences of the Queen and Bentinck, he had
changed his mind. “The Queen’s answer was rage’, however,
and now the King had given in to her; ‘the present plan as he
believed made by di Aci was to resume and to endeavour to
upset the Constitution by Parliamentary numbers.’ Another
possible explanation is that the King was induced by Cassaro
to gamble on Bentinck’s making no effective resistance, and
was encouraged by some ‘pleasant’ news he received from
Castelcicala on February 23rd: the contents of Castelcicala’s
despatch are not known, but he very likely reported Castlereagh
as saying or implying that Bentinck was not empowered to use
force.*
   Whatever the reasons for this sudden decision, it shook the
scales from Bentinck’s eyes. He now saw ‘the impossibility of
placing even the least reliance on His Majesty’s word or good
faith’; he quarrelled with Cassaro—in whom he at last detected
the chief instigator of the King’s move—and told him that ‘if
the King resumed I was determined to enlever the Queen’:
    ‘This was a trial of strength. It was the first effort of a long and con-
certed plan to destroy the system of liberty, to which in every part of
its progress every impediment had hitherto been thrown, but in vain.’*
   He refused to deal with Cassaro any longer; negotiations
would henceforth be carried on through regular channels, that
is, through Belmonte. To him Bentinck announced that ‘it was
necessary that I should not be compelled to retreat a step’.
