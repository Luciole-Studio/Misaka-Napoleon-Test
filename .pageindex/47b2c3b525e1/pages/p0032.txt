to interfere in the concerns of an independent sovereign; but if His
Sicilian Majesty is to be preserved on his throne, he must be induced to
listen to the admonitory voice of his disinterested ally.’

     There might be advantages for England too in sucha reform:
for the nation was ripe for resistance, and

‘to become a province of England would not, I believe, be looked upon
as a misfortune by the major part of its inhabitants. But an independent
Government and a free Constitution would be considered as a boon, the
attainment of which would irrevocably bind the Sicilians to the nation
which should procure it for them.”*

   Amherst, however, was not to carry out the intervention he
recommended: both he and Stuart resigned their posts in disgust.*
For nothing ever seemed to go right after the summer of 1810.
Even a fairly simple matter—a dispute over a ship under British
licence which Sicilian privateers has seized—now took on a
suspicious tinge and led to a string of acrimonious Notes.”
