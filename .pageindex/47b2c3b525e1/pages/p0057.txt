  Yet, in spite of the constant        difficulties he experienced,
Bentinck’s power seemed great, almost viceregal. He did, after
all, control Sicily’s finances through the subsidy, and her army
through his Captain-Generalship. His house was the headquarters
of the Patriotic party.* He conferred with Orleans and Belmonte
daily; saw the other Ministers, nobles, and politicians frequently;
discussed military business with the Prince and the Sicilian staff.
He raised public loans, distributed corn when it was short,
arranged the redemption of Sicilian slaves from Tunis, and
gave magnificent entertainments, some of which the Prince
attended.* Noble families asked him to arbitrate their contested
marriage settlements; the very Ministers he had driven out of
office—one, indeed, into exile: Ascoli—now cringed to him and
solicited his favour.* He did not hesitate to rebuke the Prince of
Butera in strong terms when that first baron of Sicily became
involved in riots against the government;* if a monk betrayed
to the Queen a confidential letter of his, he made the government
deport the monk to Lipari.* The supporters of the ‘good cause’
he marshalled and encouraged: true, his diplomatic position
prevented him at first from appearing quite openly as the leader of
a party: he refused, for instance, to see the masters of the Palermo
guilds when, in the election of May 1812, they chose to flout
tradition and the Prince’s wishes and came to him for advice;
but secretly he sent a message urging them to stand firm. He was,
in fact, already playing a dual role as British Minister and Sicilian
politician; the new ‘system’ was patently his creation. To the
Patriots he seemed, anyhow at first, the redeemer of Sicily, and in
his praise they wrote a great mamy stanzas, satires and sonnets.*
   It may seem strange that with all this display of power
Bentinck constantly complained of delay and frustration. The
chief trouble was, of course, his running battle with the Royal
Family; another was the peculiar character of the British inter-
vention, which at once made Bentinck’s power irresistible, and
prevented him from exerting it to the full. Furthermore, the
weakness of the Patriots—which their accession to the Ministry
soon revealed—hindered him even while it forced him to behave
like an active local politician.
