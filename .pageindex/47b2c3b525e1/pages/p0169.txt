   Sicily’s fate was in fact decided not at Palermo, but at the
Congress of Vienna, and then—when Napoleon came back and
Murat joined him—at Waterloo and Tolentino. In May 1815
Ferdinand dissolved Parliament for the last time and sailed to
Naples. There ensued the piecemeal destruction of Sicily’s
constitution and independence, until in December 1816 Ferdi-
nand proclaimed the union of the two kingdoms and, in the
words of a contemporary historian, made of Sicily a ‘posthu-
mous conquest of Napoleon’:* for almost at one stroke he swept
away every one of her native institutions—those handed down
from Norman and Aragonese days together with those set up
in 1812—and put in their place the centralised French system
which Joseph Bonaparte had already introduced at Naples in 1806.
   This counter-revolution did not spring from the King’s
personal wishes alone: he had promised Austria not to allow in
his dominions institutions at variance with those of Austria’s
Italian territories.* This engagement, and its consequences, were
quite in line with Castlereagh’s policy of giving Austria a free
hand in Italy; but they were scarcely in line with Britain’s
Sicilian policy over the last few years. Hence the last act had to
wait on complicated negotiations among the British, Austrian,
and Neapolitan Governments, the result of which was that—for
the benefit of the British Parliament—there was inserted in the
decrees of 1816 a reference to a Sicilian Parliament which had
in fact ceased to exist. By this means—so A’Court maintained—
the whole course of British intervention in Sicily was made to
‘offer a plain, honourable, and consistent tale’.*
