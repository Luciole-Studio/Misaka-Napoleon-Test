  Bentinck had been aware throughout the crisis of the risks
he was taking; he had already decided to send Lamb, his
Secretary, home to explain the complex Sicilian situation to
Castlereagh personally. He now doubly regretted that Castle-
reagh’s instructions had not come sooner; for if he had not
exactly used force, he could not very well quibble over the
distinction between the threat and the deed.
    ‘T feel that I have taken upon myself the most serious responsibility,’
he wrote. ‘H.R.H.’s gracious approbation can alone determine whether
I have really consulted, as was my sincere desire, the real honour of
Great Britain, the happiness, the liberty and the independence of Sicily.
I anxiously await this approbation for my own comfort, but I still more
ardently desire it for the sake of this great population who will view in
it the irreversible confirmation of the great charter of their rights and
liberties.”*

   Castlereagh’s reply was approval tinged with regret. In a
private letter he hinted that

‘in giving to our adherents the support they are entitled to claim at
our hands, and in affording the new system the aid and countenance
which the safety of the island requires and without which it would in
its infancy perish, we must try so to arrange as to obviate the necessity
of frequent acts of undisguised power’.

   Bentinck should if possible arrange for the King and Queen
to become entirely dependent on Britain for their income, and
so acquire a means of coercion over them less flagrant than that
which he had been led to use.*
   It is somewhat odd that Bentinck should have used in his
despatch the glowing phrase ‘this great population’ of those
whom, in a private letter to Castlereagh dated three days later,
he called ‘this very timid people’. Throughout the crisis he had
complained that the Sicilians were not supporting him properly;
