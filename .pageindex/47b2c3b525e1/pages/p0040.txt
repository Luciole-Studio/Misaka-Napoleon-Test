  The chance that Italy might rise against the French and win
her independence with English help was another reason for
returning to London. In June, Wellesley and Bentinck had
discussed a proposal lately received from the Archduke Francis
of Este—then in Sardinia—for the formation in the Mediter-
ranean of a force made up of Italians and of Austrian officers
unemployed since the Austro-French peace treaty. Its intended
purpose was to raise the standard of revolt in Italy or in the
conquered Austrian provinces, and eventually to establish an
Italian Kingdom under the Archduke, at least as large as
that founded by Napoleon. Bentinck had supported this plan
warmly: Italy, he said, was thoroughly discontented with French
rule and might prove another Spain; the country offered not
only a long coastline accessible to British seapower, but also
‘a people equally brave with the Spaniards, and equally en-
thusiastic in the cause of liberty, but much more intelligent and
more tractable’. All Wellesley had given him was a vague
authorisation to look into the matter: the Government, taken
up as they were with the Peninsula, could spare little attention
for Italy, and Bentinck had had no illusions on the point.* Now,
however, he had met the Archduke, and intelligence from the
Continent confirmed his enthusiasm for the plan. On his return
to London he pressed for, and got, an authorisation to promote
it actively: he might form an Italo-Austrian force and make
ready for a possible rising in Italy by getting in touch with the
patriotic party there; for these purposes he might spend up to
