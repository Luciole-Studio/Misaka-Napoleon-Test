   “Were we happy enough’, a Sicilian nobleman told the English traveller
Brydone as early as 1770, ‘to enjoy the blessings of your constitution, you
might call us rich indeed, many hidden doors of opulence would then be
opened which now are not even thought of, and we should soon re-

English institutions took on an added prestige in Sicilian eyes
from the common          origin of both nations in the Norman
Conquest. The example of the English Constitution could there-
fore be quoted in defence of Sicilian liberties; it seemed an
attractive model to those who wished to mend rather than end
their ancient institutions.*
  But Sicilian particularism took yet another form, and this the
most deep-rooted, most widespread, and most lasting: resent-
ment at seeing the country ruled in the interests of Naples, by
Neapolitans who were too familiar to be foreigners, too foreign
to be compatriots.* This resentment, fed by centuries of past
rivalry, was re-awakened by Caracciolo’s attacks on local insti-
tutions; it could only be exacerbated by the presence in Sicily,
in 1799 and again from 1806 onwards, of a Court bent on
recovering Naples and seemingly indifferent to Sicilian concerns,
and of a Neapolitan Ministry hostile to Sicilian privileges. The
revenues of the island were being used ‘to recover a kingdom
which, once reunited to the Crown, would again reduce their
own country to the harsh condition of a province’; and to
support a flock of ‘foreign’ pensioners and officials. Desire for
independence and an end to Neapolitan meddling was the one
feeling that could be counted on to unite the Sicilians; it was
shared even by staunch conservatives, devoted to the monarchy.”
This local jealousy contributed more to the growth of the patri-
otic movement than some Italian historians—with the later
unification of Italy in mind—have been willing to allow.
   Thus the French Revolution and the ensuing wars widened
and intensified the conflict between Crown and Parliament.

a halt to the attempt to make Sicily a modern absolutist State;
