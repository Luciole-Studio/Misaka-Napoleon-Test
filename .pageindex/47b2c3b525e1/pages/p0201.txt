  question of the queen’s ‘treachery’ is difficult (and largely unprofitable) to disentangle.
  There is evidence that she was in correspondence with Murat and possibly with France.
  There is also evidence that at various times she thought of giving up Sicily in exchange
  for her husband’s restoration to Naples. On the other hand, there can be no doubt
  that the marriage of her granddaughter Marie-Louise (which aroused such suspicion
  among the British in Palermo) genuinely horrified her (see her letters to Ruffo, to
  the Empress Maria Ludovika, and to the Emperor Francis, 22, 23 Mar., 7 Apr. 1810,
  in Corti, 768-9, 772). But eighteen months later, after Bentinck’s first remonstrances,
  she was ready to take refuge in Vienna (as she had often thought of doing before)
  and to ask Napoleon’s leave to do so; she hinted that the Emperor should intercede
  with Napoleon to reconcile him with King Ferdinand and with herself (to the
  Emperor Francis, 30 Aug., to Ruffo, 9 Sep. 1811, in Corti, 614). At the same moment,
  however, she was thinking of calling in the Russians (see p. 26 and note). In fact she
  had no settled plan at all—only a settled desire to recover Naples—and it seems most
  likely that by this time her mind was too unstable to allow her to follow any coherent
  policy.
