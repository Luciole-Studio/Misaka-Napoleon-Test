had committed himself too far to retreat: and he continued on
the whole to pin his hopes on the King.*
  Drummond was in a difficult position. The Court clamoured
for an invasion of Italy, a plan he approved: but it had constantly
to be put off during 1807 and 1808, either because the British
garrison was depleted, or a French invasion was feared, or the
state of the Continent made it impossible. The Court knew,
besides, that the British Government still would not commit
themselves to the recovery of Naples at a peace. So Drummond
got little thanks from the Court, and little support from anyone.
It was, one may say, Drummond’s vanity in thinking he could
manage the Court by his own influence alone, without support,
which led to his downfall. He had already irritated Canning by
forcing, on his own initiative, a break between Sicily and Russia.
A few months later he caused serious embarrassment to the
British Government by furthering, again on his own initiative,
and unofficially, the Court’s claim to the Regency of Spain.
Canning recalled him at once, in September 1808.”
