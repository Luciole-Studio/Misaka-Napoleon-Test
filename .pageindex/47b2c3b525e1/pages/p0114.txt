   ‘As for the King and Queen, there was no argument but force that had
any weight with them’, he told Sangro. ‘Shew it and they were down
to the ground—withdraw it and the next moment they were on their
high horse.’*
     He therefore despatched a body of troops to the neighbour-
hood of Castelvetrano—where, rumour had it, the Queen was
hoping to raise the local population—with orders to make her
embark, if necessary by force. By yet another ultimatum he
finally made the King sign, on March 28th, a full delegation of
powers to the Prince, together with an undertaking not to
interfere in the government,          and not to resume         it without
Britain’s consent.
     On the evening of the same day, by an ironic coincidence,
despatches arrived at Palermo from Castlereagh and Castelcicala,
which if they had come sooner would almost certainly have
forestalled the whole crisis.* For Castlereagh had impressed it
on Castelcicala      that the British Government             required the
Queen’s departure for Vienna; furthermore, ‘the only hope of
our cooperation’ towards the recovery of Naples‘... rested
upon the absolute extinction of her Majesty’s authority, and
upon the establishment of a system in Sicily which by com-
manding the public confidence might prepare the Neapolitans
to regard the return of the Royal Family with favour’. To
speed the Queen on her way the British Government would
pay her debts and give her a pension (this decision in particular,
if known sooner, might well have shortened or averted the
crisis). Castlereagh, in fact, had fully accepted Bentinck’s view
that the Queen was the chief obstacle in the way of a Sicilian
settlement; he regretted that Bentinck had been unable as yet
to join his troops in Spain, but commended his ‘zeal, prudence
and firmness... under the many difficulties with which you
have had to contend’; and the same packet brought Bentinck
the K.C.B. In his instructions, however, Castlereagh told
Bentinck not to use force except (as laid down by Wellesley)
in an emergency and in the face of treachery:
