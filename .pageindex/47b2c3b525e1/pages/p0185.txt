               BENTINCK’S CONDUCT              AT GENOA                 169
   Bentinck’s private campaign diary tells a different story. On
April 22nd—he recorded—he told a deputation of Genoese
officials that he would set up a provisional government, ‘their
final disposition necessarily depending upon the decision of the
high Allied Powers’. During the next three days he discussed
the matter with some of the leading Genoese—Pareto,                  Serra,
Durazzo—and found them aristocratically inclined but ‘liberal
enough’. On April 26th, the date of the Proclamation, he wrote:

   ‘Tt was a question whether in the Proclamation it should be stated
that this arrangement was to continue until the Allies should otherwise
order—but it was considered that the most popular would be to make
an arrangement as definitive as possible which it would be the more
difficult for the Allies to upset. The union with Piedmont as supposed
likely was most of all offensive to the Genoese. With the Milanese they
would not equally dislike to be annexed—but their original independence
seemed the universal wish—I thought it politic for Great Britain that is
for her interests and influence that the wishes of the people should receive
her consideration while it was always in the hands of the Allies to
make whatever arrangement they pleased.”*

  We may never know just what Bentinck thought he was
doing; to judge from the muddle-headedness exhibited in this
curious document, he scarcely knew himself.
