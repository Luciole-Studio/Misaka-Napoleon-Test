  Bentinck therefore felt he had ample grounds for demanding
her utter removal from the political scene, taking up the question
where he had left off in April. Occasion served him: he had
received at the end of August Castlereagh’s instruction giving
him complete discretion in matters concerning the Queen; and
she herself had made a fatal slip during the interview of August
tst—when she and Bentinck had met face to face for the last
time—by saying, in her distraction, that she meant to go to
Vienna, and by asking for a frigate to take her away. In subse-
quent weeks she announced and cancelled her departure several
times. Bentinck was of course all for it; but ‘desirable as this
event might be’, he felt “very great reluctance to enforce it—at
the Queen’s time of life, in the state of her health, and at this
advanced season such an act might appear cruel’. In the letter to
the King with which he opened his attack on September 13th,
he accordingly referred only to ‘positive orders’ from his govern-
ment which made it imperative that the Queen should remove
to a greater distance from Palermo—that is, by implication,
that she should part from her husband. It was the resistance this
demand met with that raised Bentinck’s terms to include, first
a verbal, then a written promise by the King of her departure
from Sicily altogether.*
