expeditionary force, to the tune of a proclamation by the Prince
—addressed to the very troops who were supposed to fight side
by side with Murat’s—in which he stated once again his claim
to Naples. On March oth Bentinck landed at Leghorn and met
the Neapolitan generals. On the 14th he issued a proclamation
to the Italians at large. In it he offered them Britain’s help in
throwing off the ‘iron yoke of Buonaparte’: the help through
which Spain had already regained her freedom, while Sicily,
happier still, had saved herself from the ‘universal deluge’:
   ‘Through the beneficent disposition of her Sovereign, she passes from
slavery to freedom, and hastens to resume her ancient splendour among
the independent Nations. Holland will speedily have attained the same
object: is then Italy to remain under the yoke? Shall Italians alone contend
against Italians, in favour of tyrant, and for the thraldom of their country?
Italians, hesitate no longer;—be Italians, and let Italy in arms be convinced
that the great Cause of the Country is in her hands.’

  The ‘warriors of Italy’ he called upon, not indeed to join
him, but to vindicate their own rights and to be free. “Only
call, and we will hasten to your relief; and then Italy, by our
united efforts, shall become what she was in her most prosperous
periods, and what Spain now is.™*
   ‘Be Italians!’ Voila le grand mot laché. But more was let loose
besides words.
