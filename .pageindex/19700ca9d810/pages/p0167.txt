committing itself on the propriety of his actions, was so carelessly 
worded that Americans interpreted it as an outright attack upon 
the good faith of their government. A declaration that a charge 
d'affaires would succeed Jackson, a comparatively mild reproof if 
the hiatus between envoys did not last too long, became an added 
insult when months passed without any evidence that Wellesley 
had even begun to search for a successor. Although the British note 
did not please William Pinkney, when he learned of the American 
reaction to it he hastened to assure the President that "it is not so 
bad in Intention as it is in Reality, nor quite so bad in Reality as it 
is commonly supposed to be. It is the production of an indolent 
Man." 25
