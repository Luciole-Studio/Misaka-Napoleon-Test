       APPENDIX M—
       THE ECONOMIC SIGNIFICANCE OF CENSOS IN LAS NAVAS
       The analysis of the economy of the seven towns in the middle of the eighteenth century has been made without the use of one kind of
       information provided by the catastro. This is the annual payments due by owners of property for the censos imposed on their property.
       Censos were a form of lien common in early modern Spain. They could have been originally imposed in recognition of capital received as
       a loan, in which case the annual payment represented the interest on the loan (usually 3 percent) and the instrument represented a kind
       of mortgage. They could also represent pledges of an annual payment to a charitable or other religious fund (such as a memoria to insure
       the performance of masses for the soul of a departed person) guaranteed by specified pieces of property, without any capital having
       originally changed hands.[1] The libros maestros of the catastro record the annual payments charged against each piece of property for
       each censo imposed on it.
             I chose not to record these payments in taking notes from the town catastros because the payments appeared in most cases
       relatively insignificant, believing my time better spent gathering other types of information. It seemed proper, however, to check their
       importance in at least one case, so that I noted the censos for Navas. A review of this information indicates that in this town, in effect,
       they were a matter of minor economic importance.
             The total amount owed by vecinos on censos was 1,005 reales per year, or 56 EFW. This is less than 1 percent of the net town
       income of 7,170 EFW. I did not attempt to trace the location of the owners of the censos, so that I cannot tell how much of this 56 EFW
       was paid outside the town. Even if it all went to outsiders, however, it would reduce the estimated per capita income by only 0.06 EFW,
