  “Trembling, hoping, or grateful holders of Brazilian invest¬
ments” which under the propaganda directed by a Brazilian
agency in England had rapidly accumulated in the years pre¬
ceding the crisis, socially prominent capitalists involved in
loans and speculations, eminent Britishers on boards of direc¬

merchants protested the action of the Foreign Office in the
Christie affair.113 Their position was stated in a Daily News
article on February 12, 1863, by a writer who complained:
“who of us, we ask Lord Russell, can trade safely with Brazil
or any other country, who can buy Brazilian or foreign bonds
of any kind, who can with common prudence invest his money
in the railway shares of small and defenseless states ... if
mines like this are to be sprung under his feet by his own gov¬
ernment”?114 It was not economic interests that forced the crisis
of 1863; on the contrary it was a question almost exclusively
of the perpetuation or failure of the traditional political in¬
fluence of Great Britain in Portuguese America.
