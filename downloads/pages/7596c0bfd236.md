‘AS FAR AS THE ORDNANCE DEPARTMENT IS CONCERNED’: SIR ARTHUR WELLESLEY, LORD CHATHAM AND THE POLITICS OF MILITARY DECISION-MAKING, 1808–1809

Jacqueline Reiter

In the summer of 1808, Sir Arthur Wellesley was sent to the Iberian Peninsula at the head of a combined force of about 20,000 men. Earlier that year, Napoleon had overthrown the Spanish monarchy and installed his brother Joseph as King of Spain; this had provoked Spain to rebel and ask Britain for assistance in money and weapons. Given that Portugal was a long-standing British ally, the whole Iberian Peninsula lay open as a theatre for British continental operations and Wellesley’s orders were to assist the Spanish and Portuguese in expelling the French invaders. Prior to his departure, he made the usual arrangements with the various relevant government ministers for equipping his expedition, liaising particularly with Lord Castlereagh (the Secretary of State for War) and the 2nd Earl of Chatham (Master-General of the Ordnance). Despite these discussions, Wellesley remained dissatisfied with the fact that the Ordnance provided no artillery horses for his army. Responding to this deficiency, Wellesley acquired 300 horses from the Irish Commissariat through his own efforts, but these turned out to be old, ill, and generally unfit for purpose. He claimed to have pressed for horses to be officially supplied, only to have his request refused: ‘I should have taken horses of the Artillery if I could have got them. But alas! I could not and have therefore taken those which will probably only do our work till we shall get others.’ 1 As it happened, however, getting others on the spot proved more difficult than anticipated, and by mid-August Wellesley had completely lost patience, writing privately to Lord Castlereagh: ‘Let nobody ever prevail upon you to send a corps to any part of Europe, at least, without horses to draw the guns.’ 2 Wellesley’s side of the story has been most recently explored by Rory Muir in Wellington: the Path to Victory, with additional material provided in the online companion to the book. 3 The Ordnance’s point of view is less well known, but it was nevertheless placed on record at the highest levels. Lord Chatham was well aware of Wellesley’s anger against his department; in a striking parallel with his behaviour following his disastrous command of the Walcheren expedition

At first glance, this looks like the kind of trouble experienced by many British 
military expeditions during the Napoleonic Wars in the process of getting large 
bodies of troops to their destination along with all their equipment. In the words 
of Roger Knight, ‘the growing scale and complexity of the war … inevitably put an 
enormous strain on the bureaucratic machinery that underpinned the conflict’. 5 
Wellesley’s woes were hardly unique, and his over-stating them in an attempt to 
get the problem corrected in London was understandable, as were Chatham’s 
attempts to mitigate the impact of the accusations on his department. As common 
as these matters were, the issue probably would not have made much of a ripple 
had the Peninsular campaign not gone badly wrong. Despite his victories over 
the French at Roliça and Vimeiro, Wellesley was rapidly superseded as expedition 
commander by Sir Harry Burrard and Sir Hew Dalrymple. Instead of following 
up on Wellesley’s victories by pursuing the French, Burrard and Dalrymple were 
convinced the British situation in the Peninsula was untenable. They negotiated 
the infamous Convention of Cintra, which allowed the French to evacuate their 
army and spoils of war from Portugal in British ships. The resulting public outrage 
at home led to all three British commanders being recalled and placed before a 
military inquiry at Chelsea, during which Wellesley, Burrard, and Dalrymple all 
defended themselves by flagging up the lack of artillery horses as a significant 
reason for their actions. 6 
At this point, the issue blew up into something much bigger than the 
inadequacy of Wellesley’s equipage: it was held to point to a potentially criminal 
deficiency in the way the government planned and executed its military 
campaigns. This was exploited by the political opposition in pamphlets, in the

As this article will show, the Ordnance was already under considerable political pressure by the summer of 1808; the Cintra inquiry’s publication of complaining correspondence from Wellesley and his artillery commander, Lieutenant-Colonel William Robe, was only the latest in a list of mounting pressures the department had to face. 7 Only the distraction of other, greater political crises may have stopped Wellesley’s criticisms from bringing all the troubles of the Ordnance to a head in the form of an official parliamentary investigation.

The Vimeiro campaign 
What went wrong was never satisfactorily explained, but some context is required 
to understand at least in part why there might have been so much confusion 
over who was responsible for the provision of horses. Sir Arthur Wellesley’s 
expedition to the Peninsula was put together in the immediate aftermath of the 
Dos de Mayo uprisings across Spain in 1808, but its roots went even further 
back to a time when Britain and Spain had been enemies rather than allies. 
In 1806, a British force had briefly occupied Buenos Aires and Montevideo, 
Spanish American territories. That expedition had ended in disaster, but the 
British government’s thoughts kept turning back to South America, particularly 
as there were so few military opportunities closer to home. Sir Arthur Wellesley 
had given his opinion in 1807 on the possibility of attacking Venezuela; after 
talking with South American separatist Francisco de Miranda in 1808, Wellesley 
drew up a proposal for attacking Caracas and Mexico, and in April 1808 the 
cabinet approved the idea of sending Wellesley with about 13,000 men on such 
an expedition. 8 
These plans changed after news of the Spanish uprisings reached Britain, 
along with impassioned pleas for aid. Although Spain wanted weapons rather 
than soldiers, the prospect thereby available to the British government of finally 
being able to make military headway on the mainland of Europe was too strong, 
particularly as 8,000 men destined for South America had already been assembled 
at Cork. For a while, however, there was some confusion about where to send this 
little force, and the government seemed to be keeping its options open to use it 
either on ‘the coast of Spain or eventually in South America, should no favorable 
[sic] opening present itself in Europe for their exertions’. 9 On 4th June, however, 
Wellesley informed an acquaintance that government ministers were beginning 
to talk of sending him to Spain with the 8,000 men from Cork to join with 5,000 
troops under Sir Brent Spencer who were already at Gibraltar. 10 The government 
debated this change in strategic priorities for the next ten days, before issuing

This meant, however, that Wellesley – almost at the bottom of the lieutenant 
generals list – was too junior to command it. Various alternative names were 
floated (including the Duke of York), but the man most expected to command 
was Lord Chatham, who was a lieutenant general in addition to being Master 
General of the Ordnance. Although he had little military experience, Chatham 
was a high-ranking member of the government who had recently been considered 
for other high-profile roles, including an appointment to Sweden, but he turned 
the Peninsular command down, probably for personal reasons. 13 This was the 
reason why the expedition was eventually placed under the command of Sir Hew 
Dalrymple, Lieutenant Governor of Gibraltar, with Sir Harry Burrard as his 
second-in-command. 
Decision-making at the Ordnance 
Time was of the essence, which was why Wellesley went on ahead of the other 
parts of his expedition, blissfully unaware that he had been superseded in his 
command until he arrived in Mondego Bay at the end of July. But why did no 
artillery horses accompany any part of the expedition? 
Something had clearly gone awry in the process of establishing what Wellesley 
needed for his expedition from the Ordnance. In respect of one important point 
which was made much of later, there had been no shortage of suitable horses for 
Wellesley to take. At least 300 artillery horses had been allocated to the commands 
of Brigadier Generals Anstruther and Acland, but they did not accompany their 
brigades when the troops were sent out to reinforce Wellesley at the end of July 
1808. It is not clear why this did not happen: Chatham himself did not know, 
or at least did not bother trying to find out, admitting that ‘they were in perfect 
readiness at the place of embarkation’. 14 Most likely, however, the reason was that

Eventually Abercromby scraped 800 horses together, but even so the British could only bring light guns into the field by getting the men to drag them over the sand. 18 Sir John Moore, who held a subordinate command during the expedition, bitterly condemned the decision to send Abercromby out so ill-equipped: ‘Not a horse for either cavalry or artillery, not a waggon or the means of conveying an article a yard from the beach.’ 19 Piers Mackesy, however, is more realistic in his history of the Egyptian campaign: ‘Shortage of transport and cavalry was the inevitable accompaniment of an amphibious operation’. 20 Like Abercromby, Wellesley in 1808 discovered the limits of equipping an expedition intended to fight far from home. Communication problems with the Ordnance, however, were almost 
certainly responsible for what happened. These were in turn traceable to 
changes at the decision-making level. Most significantly, the destination of the 
expedition had been changed, almost at the last minute, from South America 
to Portugal. Pertinently, no doubt, one officer who should have been sent to the 
Peninsula was none other than the Master-General of the Ordnance himself, 
who was thus heavily involved in the early planning stages of the expedition as 
a participant and would have had considerable information to hand about the 
logistical requirements for such an undertaking. 22 Moreover, these fundamental 
and hasty changes created considerable confusion regarding what equipment 
should accompany Wellesley to which destination. Chatham carefully preserved 
correspondence with Wellesley from mid-June 1808 in which Wellesley wrote 
that ‘the arrangement for the Ordnance demand is very satisfactory’ and that 
‘the arrangement for the artillery and engineers … is likewise very compleat’, 
but this referred to the period before Wellesley received his definitive orders for 
Portugal on 14th June. 23 How many meetings Wellesley had with Chatham to 
discuss matters after this date is unclear. From the Ordnance’s point of view, 
Chatham and other department officers felt they had experienced difficulties in 
getting Wellesley to specify his needs precisely enough. The Master-General’s 
deputy secretary, Colonel Charles Neville, complained that Wellesley’s failure to 
make himself available for meetings had left the department ‘somewhat in the 
dark’. 24 For the main part, this was due to the fact that Wellesley spent much 
of his time over the summer in Ireland in his capacity as Chief Secretary; he 
spent less than a week in England between receiving his orders for Portugal 
and leaving for Cork at the start of July. 25 This represented a major problem, 
because the Ordnance decision-making process relied strongly upon face-to-face 
discussion.

loosely over a department that had both military and civilian responsibilities, and 
which was broken into several almost independent sections. The Master-General 
headed the Artillery and the Engineers and acted as a political liaison between 
the Cabinet and the Army – something Chatham was particularly well-qualified

Board of Ordnance 26 carried out most of the practical duties of the department, 
from organising the production of ammunition and weaponry to overseeing the 
building and maintenance of fortifications. The Master-General himself did not 
usually get involved in Board business; his official remit remained reasonably 
vague, which meant he could largely interpret the scope of his duties in any way 
he wished. This was designed in part to allow the department to continue working 
smoothly if the Master-General were to serve actively abroad (as in fact occurred 
in 1809, when Chatham went to Walcheren). Even Chatham, however, could see 
the need for a central authority in such a sprawling department: ‘the powers now 
vested in the Master-General, distinct from, and controuling the proceedings of 
the Board, appear to me to be indispensably necessary.’ 27 
These departmental complexities, and the cumbersome bureaucracy that 
came with them, have often been criticised by historians. Richard Glover described 
the Napoleonic-era Ordnance as ‘too often dilatory’ and even ‘declining’ and 
referred to the department’s ‘sluggish obstructionism’. 28 More recently, Rory 
Muir called it ‘a moribund organisation notorious for its sloth’. 29 There is 
very good reason for this, and contemporary military men certainly found the 
Ordnance’s labyrinthine chain of command difficult to get to grips with; but 
recent scholarship has occasionally been much less negative. As Gareth Cole has 
pointed out, for all its slip-ups, the Ordnance did manage to keep the Army, 
Navy, militia, auxiliaries, and vast numbers of foreign troops equipped with guns 
and ammunition, while at the same time forwarding scientific discoveries at home 
and maintaining fortifications all around the globe. 30 Under Chatham as Master 
General, the Ordnance succeeded in arming Nelson’s fleet at Trafalgar, equipping 
the 1805 expedition to Germany, arming an amphibious assault to Copenhagen 
in 1807, and maintaining significant forces in the Baltic and Mediterranean Seas 
and Indian Ocean, not to mention building extensive fortifications and arming 
unprecedented numbers of Militia and Volunteers at home. Despite its bloated 
bureaucracy, the Ordnance certainly worked. 
This did not mean it worked quickly or efficiently, and a number of factors 
exacerbated the situation in 1808. The political scene at the time Wellesley was 
first sent to the Peninsula was not favourable for making quick and accurate 
decisions in putting together a major overseas expedition. The government was 
nominally headed by the Duke of Portland, but the elderly and infirm prime

No longer was major strategic direction established within a small group of highly-informed insiders, as had been the case in the previous decade when Pitt the Younger had mostly decided war policy outside cabinet meetings with the Foreign Secretary, Lord Grenville, and the Secretary of State for War, Henry Dundas. Under Portland, the cabinet discussed everything and made decisions as a group. Furthermore, several government departments had immediate responsibility for planning and military affairs besides the Master General of the Ordnance. The main minister with control over military affairs and resources was the Secretary of State for War and the Colonies, but he shared decision-making responsibilities with several colleagues. The First Lord of the Admiralty controlled naval resources and strategy; the Foreign Secretary liaised with military allies, and thus had considerable influence in the formation of military strategy and deciding where troops might be employed; and even the Home Secretary, with control over the Militia and other domestic auxiliaries, had some say in deciding what proportion of the Regular Army it might be wise to make available for offensive operations abroad. Outside the cabinet were the Secretary at War and the Commander-in-Chief, who also had to be consulted in Army matters. This division of responsibility led to confusion, power struggles, and inevitable delays in outfitting and deploying expeditions, as each government department jealously guarded the boundaries of its influence without exerting any checks on the others. 31 The resulting complications frustrated many politicians involved in military planning, who felt the lack of a central authority to bring decisions speedily and authoritatively to a point. ‘Nothing is so clear to me, as that a great and arduous War cannot be successful under a Government of Departments,’ William Huskisson, Secretary of the Treasury, complained. 32 The inevitable result of this decentralisation was that personality had a much stronger role to play than might otherwise have been the case. Individuals were much more capable of stamping departmental behaviour and arrangements with their own preferences, and the more cantankerous government members could exert tremendous influence on the decision-making process. The Ordnance was an excellent example of this. As a department it had very little direct influence over the course of the war and although he was considered the cabinet’s main military adviser, the Master-General of the Ordnance was not directly concerned with the formation of military strategy. This was the remit of the Secretaries of State, who nominally set the priorities and merely presented the Master General with a request for the arms and ammunition required to achieve them. 33

Chatham had first come to the Ordnance in 1801 and, by 1808, had served 
there under three governments. He had been a cabinet minister since 1788, with 
the exception of a brief 13-month hiatus in 1806–1807. He could be awkward 
and difficult to get on with and was always alive for any sign that colleagues might 
be trespassing on or belittling his authority. 35 Although he generally stopped 
short of direct obstruction, he had a habit of inflating the difficulties facing his 
department in order to stop it being taken for granted, or of throwing obstacles 
in the way of decisions he did not like. This did not necessarily translate into 
outright incompetence or irresponsibility, as many historians have concluded, 
leaning on stories of Chatham’s well-known laziness; but it could, and did, lead 
to inefficiency and friction within the broader wartime governmental machine. 
In particular, Chatham had a reputation for magically multiplying bureaucratic 
difficulties if his opinion was disregarded. Lord Hawkesbury warned Sir Arthur 
Wellesley as early as 1807 that ‘Lord Chatham is … very precise, and it is 
necessary therefore in all Business in which he is concerned to proceed regularly 
with him.’ 36 Lord Mulgrave, who himself later served at the Ordnance, described 
Chatham more bluntly as ‘a jealous votary of formalities’. 37 
Despite this, and despite the sensitivities that impinged upon his mode 
of conducting business, Chatham was a surprisingly central figure at the

Hadden did not explain precisely what Chatham’s ‘system’ was but examining the way his department planned expeditions gives some hints. 39 Decision-making at Chatham’s Ordnance was essentially a three-tiered process. The first was largely informal and conducted behind the scenes in private negotiation. The ministers responsible for planning a campaign (usually the Secretary of State for War, the First Lord of the Admiralty, or occasionally the Foreign Secretary) approached Chatham in person, with the prospective expedition commander in attendance. Together, they decided the precise details – the number of guns, the amount of ammunition, and the nature of the equipment. All these details depended on where the expedition was going, what it was designed to achieve, and how many men (or ships) would be involved. Nothing was yet set out on paper; everything was done in conversation. Only when all had been agreed verbally did the parties involved move towards the second stage of the process – securing an official requisition, which had to be laid before the Cabinet for approval. This allowed the minister responsible for the expedition to seal the deal with the established phrase ‘signification of the King’s Pleasure’. This phrase reflected more than just a command issued by a superior department (such as the War Department) that could not be refused; 40 by the time the ‘King’s Pleasure’ was signified, all arrangements should already have been tacitly agreed. The phrase was explicitly designed to provide departments such as the Ordnance with accountability to a higher power – the Cabinet and (beyond that) the Crown. As such, it was an absolutely essential part of a process that might become subject to parliamentary scrutiny. As Lord Mulgrave, the First Lord of the Admiralty, put it, ‘The King’s Pleasure cannot be received by a department at the head of which is a Cabinet Minister except by decision of the Cabinet, or (where … the object presses) by previous assent’. 41 Only at that stage did a proper paper trail begin. The details of the requisition were set down in a formal letter, using the phrase ‘the King’s Pleasure’ and specifying the exact amount of artillery, ammunition, or equipment required. The Ordnance under attack 
This whole process was tremendously slow but, as much as his colleagues may 
occasionally have wanted to hasten him, Chatham’s ‘precision’ (or pedantry) 
reflected the political circumstances under which the Ordnance operated. An 
important element of Chatham’s system was the need for accountability in a 
department that handled enormous amounts of public money. During his 
wartime tenure as Master-General, the annual Ordnance Estimates presented to 
Parliament grew from £1.27 million to £5.31 million. 43 This increase had already 
caused something of a stir in Parliament, and the finances of the Ordnance Office 
were being scrutinised more and more closely. 
Driven by the length and the expense of the war and by a growing sense of 
military impotence, Parliament had begun to assert the right of the public to know 
the full extent of the resources at the government’s disposal, establishing official 
enquiries to discover how they were being used or abused. The Commission of 
Naval Enquiry led the way between 1802 and 1806, producing 14 reports; a 
further 14 reports were issued by the Commission of Naval Revision between 
1806 and 1809, focusing on civilian aspects of naval administration. Between 1805 
and 1812, the Commission for Military Enquiry published 19 reports on several 
of the military-related branches of the executive. 44 The Commission reported on 
the Ordnance in 1811, after Chatham had left it, but he was well aware of the 
Commissioners’ interest in his department during his tenure as Master-General.

Robe’s letter was paired with another from Sir Arthur Wellesley to Sir Hew Dalrymple on 1st September. Wellesley laid the responsibility for the state of the artillery horses squarely at the door of the Ordnance, which, in refusing to send any horses at all from Britain, had forced him to source his own animals at short notice:

Wellesley concluded by making an urgent request for ‘an immediate supply of 300 artillery horses’ and explained he had left a whole artillery brigade behind ‘as the horses were unable to draw them on’. 55 This was damning for the Ordnance but became positively catastrophic when coupled with the oral testimony delivered before the inquiry by Sir Arthur Wellesley, Sir Hew Dalrymple, and Sir Harry Burrard, all of whom used the shortage of artillery horses and supplies to justify their conduct in not pursuing the French following Wellesley’s victory at Vimeiro, and in putting their names to the Convention of Cintra. Conclusion 
In the end, little came of the political assault on the Ordnance Department over 
the winter of 1808–1809. The government deflected Ponsonby’s call for a full 
parliamentary inquiry into Spain and Portugal by agreeing to release a number of 
official papers connected with the conduct of the campaign; and any attempt by 
Yorke to pursue his motion on the fortifications was lost in the furore surrounding 
the whole-House investigation into accusations that the Duke of York had been 
complicit in the selling of military commissions by his former mistress, Mary 
Anne Clarke. This kept Parliament busy until the end of the 1809 session, and 
when it sat again in 1810, the failure of the Walcheren expedition (commanded 
by Lord Chatham himself) dominated everything. The appointment of a new 
Master-General in April 1810, Lord Mulgrave, meant an Ordnance inquiry was 
even less likely to take place, particularly as Mulgrave did make some attempt 
to deal with potentially troublesome issues (dismissing Major James from his 
Ordnance command, for example). But inquiry was clearly the order of the day, 
and the Ordnance’s escape was perhaps due to luck rather than to any fundamental 
change in the way it conducted business. 
The question of Wellesley’s missing artillery horses in 1808 benefits from 
being placed in its general political context, providing an excellent example of the 
shortcomings of the existing British system in dealing with the unprecedented 
scale of the war and the need to juggle multiple domestic and overseas military 
commitments. It helps demonstrate how one government department helped 
plan, outfit, and implement military campaigns, and how it responded to 
criticism of that process. It clearly shows the constraints under which generals 
like Wellesley operated in the field, but it also illuminates the difficulties afflicting

The government was under pressure to meet a war effort that stretched British resources thinly across the entire globe. This was increasingly being done under the shadow of political scrutiny through the medium of parliamentary commissions and public inquiries. Such inquiries could claim important victims – as Gary Hutchison has argued, ‘the attempted impeachment of Henry Dundas [Lord Melville, in 1805] was … the … beginning of a narrative of government scandals’. 73 They were reported in the newspapers, discussed in pamphlets and popular prints, and brought up repeatedly in the published parliamentary debates. What Wellesley and his superiors said about the artillery horses, therefore, immediately became a matter of public record, and focused attention on the shortcomings of a department that was very closely involved in the conduct of the war and that handled a great deal of public money. As a hybrid political, military, and official government body, the Ordnance had to remain responsive to immediate needs while being transparent enough to satisfy political and parliamentary scrutiny. The issue in 1808 was one of image and politics as well as one of simple military logistics. Furthermore, if Chatham’s Ordnance showed itself reluctant to adapt to change, and if it was frustrating to deal with and cumbersome, it nevertheless occasionally managed to get things right. In 1809, for example, the Ordnance provided enough weapons for domestic auxiliaries, for British troops in Portugal, for insurgents in North Germany, for arming Britain’s Spanish and Portuguese allies, and for Chatham’s own expedition to Walcheren – which, whatever its shortcomings, did not lack for Ordnance supplies. This was a considerable achievement – although it might have been better for everyone had the lessons learned in 1808 been less political and more practical in nature.
